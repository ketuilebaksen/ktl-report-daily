The decision tree. Monken had three branches on the last play, he told you which one he took and why, and we are going to price all three, and then grade our own four criteria.

First, the title. The score is backwards, and the decision was not shocking.

The title is "Todd Monken Shocking Decision After New Orleans Saints twenty-two to twenty-three Loss!"

Two things before anything else.

The score is written backwards. Cleveland lost. Write it from the Browns' side as twenty-two to twenty-three, and better still write the thing that actually happened: Cleveland lost in overtime by one point, on a two-point conversion attempt that did not work.

And the decision was not shocking. It was explained, in full, at the podium, within half an hour of the final whistle, in some of the clearest language a head coach has used all season. A shocking decision is one nobody can account for. This one came with its own reasoning attached, unprompted, before anybody could accuse him of hiding from it.

So we are not going to call it shocking. We are going to do something more useful. Monken described a decision tree out loud. We are going to draw it, price every branch, and tell you whether he took the right one, and then we are going to grade our own homework from last week, because we printed four criteria before this game and we owe you the marks.

Here at Ktl Report the rule is simple: if you put a standard in writing before kickoff, you report against it afterwards whether you look good or not.

The tree, as he described it.

Here is the full quote, because almost nobody is going to play it in full and it is the whole article:

Q: "Going for two at the end, is that a decision you make before you get there?"
>
MONKEN: "Well, you make that decision as the clock winds down a little bit, because you're at that point to where they still have their two timeouts. So if you kick the extra point, the nature of the way the overtime rules are with ten minutes is that, okay, they're going to be in two-minute with two timeouts and need a field goal to win it. So you're not going to get the ball back, probably. So you're really saying tie or lose, is what you're saying there if you kick the extra. That's my opinion. You're saying tie or lose. And in that moment I said, 'Nah, that's not me.' So we're going to go for the win right here, and it's going to be win or lose."

Read that again and notice what it is. It is not a gut call. It is a stated comparison between two distributions of outcomes, built on three specific facts he identified in real time: the opponent had two timeouts, regulation overtime is ten minutes, and a field goal would win it for them.

That is the kind of answer you can actually evaluate. So let us evaluate it.

Branch one. Kick the extra point.

What it buys you immediately: a tie, and the ball goes back to New Orleans.

What happens next, by Monken's own reading: the Saints take over in a two-minute situation, holding two timeouts, needing only a field goal to win. Cleveland, in all likelihood, does not touch the ball again.

So the realistic outcome set from this branch is: tie, or lose. A win requires New Orleans to fail to reach field-goal range, Cleveland to then get the ball back with enough time, and Cleveland to score, in an offense that had been struggling all afternoon.

The honest price of Branch One: the near-certainty of not losing on that play, purchased by handing the other team the only live chance to win.

And the thing people under-weight: a tie is not half a win in the standings, it is a strange, specific object. It helps a tiebreaker, it costs a game of separation, and in a conference race it is mostly a game that did not happen.

Branch two. Go for two.

What it buys you immediately: a binary. Convert and you win the game on that play. Fail and you lose it on that play.

What happens next: nothing. That is the entire point of the branch, and it is also its appeal. No two-minute defense, no field-goal attempt by somebody else, no praying for a missed kick. The outcome is decided by your own eleven men executing one call.

The honest price of Branch Two: you convert the chance of a tie into a chance of a win, and you pay for it with the chance of a loss. You also take the decision out of the opponent's hands entirely.

Monken's sentence for this is better than any analytics summary of it: "it's going to be win or lose."

Branch three. The one nobody asked about.

There was a third branch earlier in the same game, and it matters because it shows this was a pattern and not a panic.

Earlier, on the goal line, Cleveland went for it rather than taking points, and did not get in. Monken was asked about it and did not flinch:

"We didn't get in when we had an opportunity to go up by a touchdown, and I feel good about going for it at that moment."

So the man who went for two in overtime had already gone for it on the goal line in the first half, and said afterwards he would do it again. Whatever you think of either call, they are the same call made by the same person with the same reasoning. That is consistency, and consistency is the thing you are entitled to demand from a coach.

So was he right? Our verdict, with the reasoning shown.

Yes, on the logic. And the logic is the only part a coach controls.

Here is why, in three steps.

One. He identified the right variables. Two timeouts, ten-minute overtime, field goal wins it for them. Those three facts together are what collapse Branch One into "tie or lose," and he named all three at the podium thirty minutes after the game. He was not rationalising backwards; he was describing what he saw at the time.

Two. The comparison he made is the correct comparison. The question is never "is a two-point conversion likely?" in isolation. It is "is this conversion more likely than the sequence of events I need on the other branch?", and the other branch required stopping a team twice, including once with two timeouts and only a field goal needed. When you phrase it that way, going for two stops being aggressive and starts being arithmetic.

Three. He accepted the accountability that comes with it, immediately. Asked what the play was supposed to be, he gave the only answer a coach should: "I'm not going to get into specifics what it was. We didn't execute it." No receiver named. No lineman named. No official blamed. We.

And now the honest counterweight, because we are not going to pretend this is free.

The strongest argument against Branch Two is not about the percentages. It is that the offense had been bad all day, by Monken's own description, "offense struggling" in the first half, and you might reasonably decline to put a whole game on one snap from a unit that had not executed cleanly for three quarters. A tie in a conference race is a real asset for a five-and-three team. If you believe your defense more than your offense, Branch One is defensible.

But that argument has to answer his question, and it does not: if you kick, you are asking that same struggling offense to get the ball back and score anyway, only now you are asking the defense to stop a two-minute drive first. You have not removed the risk. You have moved it, and added a step.

Final mark on the decision: correct call, lost game. Those are two different sentences, and here at Ktl Report we are not going to collapse them into one just because the scoreboard makes it easy.

And the criterion we did not print, which Monken printed for us.

He also handed us a number that functions as a fifth criterion, and it is the most important sentence in the press conference for anybody trying to judge this season:

"This is the NFL, man. You're going to play a bunch of one-score games, and where you finish on the ledger is going to determine your season. Right now we're five and two in one-score games."

Five and two in one-score games. That is the whole identity of this team, stated plainly: a side that cannot start and can finish, living in the margin. Sunday was the third loss in that column, and it was by one point, on a play of their own choosing.

Our overall mark: one pass, one clear fail, two incomplete pending the box score. That is an honest Monday, and it is less flattering than the version where we only grade the things that went well.

What he actually said about Shedeur Sanders.

This is where most channels will spend their whole video, so we will be precise about it and keep it in proportion.

The headline number nobody is quoting. Monken put the start in context with a figure he had clearly worked out in advance:

"For a guy that really hasn't taken snaps with the ones in what, ten weeks, when we announced it with two weeks left and you're eight weeks into the season, so you're basically two and a half months where you're basically running cards, to come in there and to continue to battle. Really, really proud of him."

Ten weeks without first-team snaps. Two and a half months "running cards", which is the scout-team job of holding up a play card and imitating somebody else's offense. That is the real baseline for Sunday, and it is harsher than "he had a good week of practice."

What he credited specifically: "He gave us a chance at the end. He really did. Made some plays. Made some plays with his legs to extend plays and get us first downs... He got us a third down conversion. Unfortunately, we got the holding call."

And the maturity answer, including the part where he corrected himself mid-sentence, which is worth keeping because it is the opposite of a prepared line:

"He's really matured. And I don't mean that in a negative way, that he was immature, that's unfair, that's not what I mean. What I mean is in terms of his work ethic, in terms of his diligence, in terms of what he will say to the coaching staff and ask the questions, his feedback. Those are all the right steps moving forward as any young player, especially at quarterback."

A head coach stopping himself to protect a player from his own phrasing is a small thing that tells you a lot.

And the question he refused, which he was right to refuse.

The exchange everybody will clip:

Q: "Did Shedeur Sanders make a case for himself to start?"
MONKEN: "I'm not even, I mean, how long has it been? Thirty minutes since the game ended. I'm not getting into the quarterback. If we want to talk about Shedeur, we can talk about Shedeur."

That is not a dodge and we are not going to sell it as one. A reporter asked a head coach to open a quarterback competition thirty minutes after an overtime loss, before he had watched a single frame of tape. He said no, and then said plainly that he would discuss the player all day if they wanted.

He also pre-answered the question honestly earlier in the same session, which is the part that gets lost: asked whether Sanders had given him confidence he did not previously have, he said "that's a great question," credited the improvement he had seen over six months, and then put the brake on himself:

"But ultimately, you've got to go play, right? You got to go on the field and you got to execute, and guys got to play well around him. It's not a one-man deal. We got to coach. We got to scheme. So, till I really see the tape."

"Till I really see the tape" is the correct answer to every question asked thirty minutes after a football game, and anybody presenting it as evasion is selling you something.

The schedule fact that reframes the whole season.

Buried near the end, and nobody is going to lead with it, is the most useful piece of context in the entire press conference:

"We've played six of the first nine road games. And if Peter was right here, only two other teams have done it. I think that'll harden us. I really do. I mean, who else plays six of their first nine games in a row on the road? I think our guys battled their rear ends off for our young team. And I look forward to coming back home. And hell, we don't leave till Christmas. We're there four games and a bye week."

Six of the first nine away from home, and then four home games and a bye before the next trip.

Hold that next to the other numbers. Seven of eleven offensive starters in their first or second NFL season. Five and two in one-score games. The worst first quarter in football for eight weeks, followed by a game lost on a one-point margin after six penalties in the opening quarter.

That is a young team that has been on an aeroplane for two months and is now about to sit still for a month. It is the single strongest argument for patience anybody has made this season, and it came from the coach, unprompted, after a loss.

And the injury, handled the way injuries should be handled.

Asked to specify what was wrong with the rookie who came back in, Monken gave the only answer available to him and showed his work:

"My understanding is an ankle. That's my understanding. But you're in the game, someone's telling you stuff in your ear and you're trying to figure out what the next call is and all kinds of stuff's rolling around. You're like, 'Okay, is he in or out?'... And that's not trying to be disrespectful, that it doesn't matter, but it's really, at that moment, if they're in, they're in; they're out, they're out."

And then the caution that matters more than the diagnosis:

"We'll see how he is, because that adrenaline rush, where it's at come tomorrow and the next day."

A player who returns on adrenaline is not a player who is fine. Monken said so himself. We are reporting "my understanding is an ankle" as exactly that, an understanding, from a man who was calling plays, and nothing more, and we will not be speculating about a timeline.

The bottom line.

Cleveland lost by a point in overtime because a two-point conversion did not work, and the head coach walked to a podium half an hour later and explained exactly why he had called it. Two timeouts for the other team. A ten-minute overtime. A field goal wins it for them. Add those three facts up and kicking the extra point is not the safe choice, it is "tie or lose", his words, so he took the branch where "it's going to be win or lose" and his own eleven men decide it. Then, asked what the play was, he said five words that most coaches cannot say: "We didn't execute it." No name, no excuse, no official.

Correct call. Lost game. Both true, and a channel that can only hold one of them in its head is not telling you anything.
