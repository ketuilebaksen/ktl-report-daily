Seventy pounds. Why Cleveland released a college sack leader and signed a man with zero career starts.

The surprise is not the signing.

Let us be precise about the headline, because the facts are smaller and the story is bigger.

Cleveland did not sign a veteran defensive lineman to its roster. It signed one to its practice squad, Eric Johnson II, at 9:34 on Wednesday morning, and released another man from the same practice squad to make room. Nobody joined the 53. Nothing was announced at a podium.

So why is Ktl Report writing a full article about two practice-squad transactions? Because the people doing the shouting have the story backwards. The surprise is not who Cleveland signed. It is who Cleveland let go, a rookie who tied for the national lead in sacks last season, and the reason has almost nothing to do with how good either man is.

It is about seventy pounds.

That is the whole article, and once you see it you will read every roster move differently for the rest of the season. Then we will go through Wednesday's practice report, where the genuinely important news is sitting, and the opponent, which contains a name that will stop every Browns fan cold.

The swap, stated exactly.

Out: defensive end Nadame Tucker. A rookie out of Western Michigan, 6-foot-3 and 250 pounds, who ran a four point seven three-second forty yard dash at the 2026 combine. His college path was anything but straight, junior college, then Houston, then Western Michigan, where he broke out. He went undrafted, signed with the Los Angeles Chargers, was released after training camp, and joined Cleveland's practice squad in September. He was never called up to the 53.

And the production everybody is talking about: last season at Western Michigan, Tucker recorded fourteen and a half sacks, tying for the national lead. The man he tied with was David Bailey of Texas Tech, who went on to be drafted second overall.

In: defensive tackle Eric Johnson II. Six-foot-four and 320 pounds, entering his fifth NFL season. Missouri State, then a fifth-round pick of Indianapolis in the 2022 draft. He has played 51 regular-season games with no starts, between the Colts and the New England Patriots, and in 2025 he recorded 41 combined tackles, a sack and a fumble recovery.

One man tied for the national lead in sacks. The other has never started an NFL game. Cleveland kept the second one.

And here is why it was never a judgment on Tucker.

Every reaction to this move has framed it as a verdict, experience beats production, the NFL does not care about college numbers, Tucker could not translate. All of that is a familiar argument and none of it is what happened here.

Look at the two body types again. Tucker is a 250-pound defensive end. Johnson is a 320-pound defensive tackle. Those are not competing players. They do not play the same position, they do not line up in the same place, and they are not asked to do remotely the same job. One rushes from the edge. The other occupies two blockers over the guard.

Now look at where Cleveland is actually hurt.

Mike Hall Junior has not practiced since the Week 4 win over Pittsburgh with an ankle injury. Kalia Davis has a quadriceps problem dating to training camp, has managed exactly one limited session all season, and did not practice Wednesday. Mason Graham, the fifth overall pick of 2025, missed the last two games with a sprained MCL and a sprained ankle.

Every one of those men is an interior defensive lineman. Cleveland's hole is at defensive tackle, and Nadame Tucker is not a defensive tackle.

This was not production versus experience. It was a shape decision. You cannot patch a leak at nose tackle with a 250-pound edge rusher, no matter how many college sacks he had. Cleveland needed seventy more pounds, and it went and got seventy more pounds.

What fourteen and a half college sacks is and isn't worth.

Now the fair version of the production question, because the video's instinct here deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal.

The video is right that fourteen and a half sacks is genuinely impressive, and right to note that the player who matched it went second overall. But the comparison is the lesson, not the argument. David Bailey went second overall and Nadame Tucker went undrafted. Thirty-two front offices watched the same two sack totals and reached completely different conclusions, which tells you the sack number was never the thing being evaluated.

What was being evaluated: length, weight, how he handles an NFL tackle's hands, whether he can hold an edge against the run, and whether 250 pounds survives Sunday. A four point seven three forty is respectable, not special, for that frame.

And we are not writing Tucker off, because nobody should. He is a rookie who has been in three organizations this year. Edge rushers develop late and bodies change. The honest sentence is the boring one: he had an excellent college season, he has not yet shown he can do it at this weight in this league, and Cleveland ran out of time to find out because its injuries were somewhere else.

And what fifty-one games with no starts actually buys.

Here is the part nobody explains, and it is the whole reason October practice squads look different from August ones.

A practice-squad player can be elevated to the active roster a maximum of three times per season. That is the currency. When you elevate a man, he plays that Sunday, no ramp-up, no learning week.

So ask what you want from that spot in late October, with three interior linemen hurt. You do not want potential. You want a body that can be elevated on Saturday and take twenty snaps on Sunday without getting anyone killed. Eric Johnson II has done exactly that 51 times. He has never started, which tells you his ceiling, and it tells you nothing about his usefulness, because nobody is asking him to start.

That is also why Cleveland released defensive tackle Chris Williams on Tuesday after he had already used two of his three elevations. The elevation count is a real asset, and a player who has spent his is worth less than a player who has not.

Potential is an August asset. Availability is an October asset. Cleveland is in first place in October.

And the genuinely big news nobody led with.

Buried under two practice-squad transactions is the most important football development of the week, and it should have been the headline.

For the first time since the season opener, Cleveland's full projected running back group was on the practice field. Quinshon Judkins, who missed the Baltimore win with an ankle injury, was a limited participant on Wednesday. So was Dylan Sampson, who has been on injured reserve since Week 1. Monken said he is optimistic about both for Tennessee.

Why this dwarfs everything else: Monken's own diagnosis of what is wrong with this offense is first-down efficiency. His words: "If you're not efficient on first downs, it makes it hard on a starting-level quarterback in the NFL... The more efficient you are on first down, the easier it is to call the game."

And the number behind it: Judkins has 73 carries for 221 yards, three yards a carry. Cleveland beat Baltimore with 93 net rushing yards produced by Jaleel McLaughlin and Audric Estimé, a man signed off Minnesota's practice squad five days earlier.

A healthy back room is the only thing on this list that attacks the root cause. Everything else is depth. This is the fix.

One caveat worth stating plainly: limited on Wednesday is not active on Sunday. Thursday and Friday decide it.

And Sampson has a clock of his own.

A detail almost nobody has reported: Dylan Sampson's twenty-one day practice window opened last week. He is on injured reserve with a designation to return, and Cleveland must either activate him to the 53-man roster or shut him down when it expires.

So Cleveland now has three of these clocks running at once, Sampson's, Kalia Davis's, which opened 15 days ago and is nearly up, and Dillon Gabriel's, which opened October 6 and closes within a week.

Gabriel, for the record, was a full participant in Wednesday's practice. Cleveland has carried four quarterbacks through this entire process. Something has to give, and with a running back room this thin, the math is getting harder to argue with.

Mason Graham, and the detail that tells you who he is.

Graham returned to practice Wednesday after missing the Jets and Ravens games with a sprained MCL and a sprained ankle, both suffered in the Week 4 win over Pittsburgh. Monken said there is a pretty good chance he plays at Tennessee.

But here is the detail Monken volunteered, and it is the best thing in the whole news cycle.

Graham was pushing to play against Baltimore. And he had a reason that had nothing to do with the standings: Jesse Minter, Baltimore's head coach, was Graham's coach at Michigan. That was his one chance this season to line up across from the man who coached him in college.

Cleveland told him no. He had managed one limited session on the Friday, so the staff gave him another week.

Think about what that costs a coaching staff. A first-round defensive tackle, desperate to play, with a personal reason, in a division game against the team in first place, and they held him out anyway. Then the defense went out and held Derrick Henry to 58 yards on 17 carries without him, and allowed Baltimore 72 yards in the entire second half.

That is the single most encouraging thing about how this team is being run. A staff that can tell its best young player no in the week he most wants to play is a staff thinking about January.

And the one that is not good news.

Not every update was positive, and this is the one to worry about.

Grant Delpit remains in the NFL's concussion protocol, and he is also dealing with a shoulder injury. He did not practice Wednesday and is the most uncertain name on the report. He was hurt on Baltimore's first drive, and the circumstance is the kind of thing that happens in football and nobody can coach away: he was struck accidentally by his own teammate, Derek Barnett, while making a tackle.

The protocol has stages. A player must pass each one, rest, then exertion, then non-contact work, then full contact, before returning to unrestricted practice. There are no shortcuts and no timetable.

The video makes a careful point here and it is the right one: KC Concepcion moved quickly through protocol after the Jets game and was back at full practice the following Wednesday, but every concussion is different and nobody should treat that as a template. Concepcion, for what it is worth, was himself limited on Wednesday with a calf and a quadriceps issue.

If Delpit cannot go, rookie Emmanuel McNeil-Warren, who played ninety-four point seven percent of the snaps after Delpit left on Sunday, and veteran Daniel Thomas, who recorded a tackle for loss in his first defensive snaps of the season, take the load again.

Schwesinger's walking boot.

This one keeps getting better the more detail comes out.

Carson Schwesinger spent much of last week in a walking boot with a sprained ankle. He missed most of that week's practice, was limited on the Friday, and then played every defensive snap against Baltimore, led the team with 13 tackles, and forced the fourth-quarter fumble out of Derrick Henry that produced the only turnover of the game.

He did not practice on Wednesday.

We will keep saying the careful thing: nobody should romanticize playing hurt, and Cleveland managing that ankle during the week is the correct call. But the record is the record. The reigning Defensive Rookie of the Year went from a walking boot to a hundred percent of the defensive snaps in a division game, and won it. Based on last Sunday, expect him to play.

And the offensive line item nobody flagged.

Here is the most underrated line on Wednesday's report, and it matters because of what is waiting on Sunday.

Elgton Jenkins played every one of Cleveland's 68 offensive snaps against Baltimore, and did not practice Wednesday with a knee injury. He is the center. Tytus Howard was limited with a knee of his own. Teven Jenkins has still not appeared in a game this season after hurting his back in practice before Week 1.

Now remember the number that defines this team: Deshaun Watson has been sacked 24 times, the most in the National Football League. Justin Herbert is second at 21.

A line that has already given up 24 sacks, missing one starter all year, with its center and its right tackle managing knees, is not a unit with room for another absence. Monken's own account of Sunday's five sacks was honest and three-part: behind schedule, receivers covered, and "a couple times where we need to get rid of the ball."

Now the opponent. The worst offense in football.

Cleveland travels to Nashville on Sunday, one o'clock Eastern, as a one and a half point favorite. And the opponent's profile is remarkable.

Tennessee averages fourteen point oh points per game, tied for last in the National Football League. The Titans are also last in yards per game, at two thirty-six point three.

Last in scoring. Last in yardage. That is the softest offense Cleveland will face all season, arriving in the week its three injured interior defensive linemen are trending back.

Tennessee's danger is on the other side of the ball, and it is pointed directly at Cleveland's weakness. Jeffery Simmons leads their pass rush with four sacks and 26 quarterback hurries. Put that against 24 sacks allowed, a center with a knee and a right tackle who was limited, and you have the matchup that decides the afternoon.

And the name that will stop every Browns fan cold.

Here is the part nobody in this news cycle has connected, and it is the real story of Sunday.

Tennessee's quarterback is Cam Ward.

Browns fans have spent the last month arguing about him. There has been a whole genre of content claiming Andrew Berry would have taken Ward first overall, that Cleveland is secretly "sniffing around" him, that he is the answer the Browns passed on. We dealt with those claims when they came up, and found no reporting behind any of them.

On Sunday, Cleveland will simply line up across from him. And the record is this: through six games Ward has the worst scoring offense in football. Against the Colts he completed 11 passes for 102 yards, both career lows. As the Tennessean's own scouting report put it, his limitations are clear enough that they give Cleveland's secondary close to a blueprint.

We are not going to spike the ball about that, and neither should anybody else. Ward is a young quarterback in his second season on a bad offense, which is a description that has applied to plenty of men who turned out fine, Cleveland has one of those on its own roster. One Sunday settles nothing about either career.

But it does settle one thing. The argument that Cleveland is one quarterback away from fixing itself gets harder to make while watching the quarterback some people wanted, on the league's worst offense, in a stadium where the Browns are favored.

And why last in scoring is a trap.

Now the cold water, because Cleveland fans have seen this movie.

Cleveland averages one point per game in the first quarter. That is thirtieth in the National Football League. Monken himself volunteered that his defense has conceded a scoring drive on the opponent's opening possession in each of the last three games.

Two bad offenses do not produce a comfortable win. They produce thirteen to ten. They produce seventeen to twelve in the rain at the Jets, which is exactly what happened two weeks ago against a team nobody feared.

Cleveland is four and two with three one-score wins. A one and a half point spread against the league's worst offense is the market telling you that the Browns are not yet a team anyone expects to win comfortably. Earning that expectation starts with a first quarter.

The corrections.

One real error and a pile of names.

The score was thirteen to ten, not thirty-two to ten. Cleveland beat Baltimore by three points on a field goal as the clock expired. A 22-point margin would have been a different season.

Names: Nadame Tucker. Eric Johnson II. Quinshon Judkins, not Quinchan or Quinnon. Dylan Sampson, not Samson. Todd Monken, not Monkin or Monin. KC Concepcion, not Casey Conpsion. Carson Schwesinger. Emmanuel McNeil-Warren. Grant Delpit. Elgton Jenkins and Teven Jenkins, two different men, not a typo of each other. Jesse Minter. Derrick Henry. And the reporter whose practice work this all rests on is ESPN's Daniel Oyefusi.

The clocks.

Kalia Davis: twenty-one day window opened 15 days ago. A decision is due within the week.

Dylan Sampson: window opened last week. Activate or shut down.

Dillon Gabriel: window opened October 6, closes within a week. Full participant on Wednesday. Four quarterbacks, one roster spot.

November 10: the trade deadline, arriving with Cleveland in first place and needing a lineman and a healthy back far more than it needs a fourth quarterback.

Where we stop.

We are not going to tell you a practice-squad swap wins or loses a game. We are not going to tell you Tennessee is a free win, because Cleveland's own offense scores one point a quarter until it panics. And we are not going to tell you Nadame Tucker cannot play, he was simply the wrong shape for the hole.

What we will tell you is that Wednesday's practice field held the single most important development of Cleveland's season: a full running back room, for the first time since Week 1, attacking the exact problem the head coach has named every time he has been asked.

The bottom line.

Out: a 250-pound rookie edge rusher who tied for the national lead in college sacks. In: a 320-pound interior veteran with 51 games and zero starts. That is not a verdict on talent. It is seventy pounds, aimed at a hole at defensive tackle created by Mike Hall Junior, Kalia Davis and Mason Graham.

Back on the field: Judkins and Sampson, limited, the full back room for the first time since the opener. Mason Graham, with a pretty good chance for Sunday, after Cleveland told him no in the one week he most wanted to play.

Still a worry: Grant Delpit in protocol with a shoulder on top of it. Schwesinger's ankle, after a walking boot and every defensive snap. Elgton Jenkins' knee, in front of a quarterback sacked 24 times.

And Sunday: Nashville, one o'clock, Cleveland by a point and a half, against the worst scoring offense in football, quarterbacked by the man half of this fanbase spent a month wishing was theirs.
