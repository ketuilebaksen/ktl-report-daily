Twenty yards a game. Where Harold Fannin's production actually went.

First. Monken did not call anybody out.

The headline says Todd Monken called out Deshaun Watson. He did not, and the proof is in the official transcript of Wednesday's press conference.

Asked about Harold Fannin Junior's vanishing production, here is what Monken actually said:

"We just, it's really nothing to do with Harold as much as it is we've got to become more consistent at throwing the football... At times we're not connecting with anybody, you know, coaching or playing. That's all of us. Protection, calls, players, quarterback, everybody."

Count the nouns in that last sentence. Protection. Calls. Players. Quarterback. Everybody. The quarterback is in there, fourth of five, in a list the head coach built specifically so that nobody could pull one name out of it.

Credit where it belongs: the video that prompted this article says the same thing, and says it well. It explicitly corrects other coverage: "One video called it the first time Monken openly admitted Watson needs to throw the football better. That's not quite it." That is honest work, and it deserves to be repeated.

So the headline is wrong, and the video knows the headline is wrong.

But the underlying question is excellent, and nobody has actually answered it. Harold Fannin Junior has lost roughly twenty receiving yards per game. Ktl Report is going to do something nobody else has bothered to do: find all twenty of them. Not "it's a bit of both." An actual accounting, cause by cause, with the evidence attached.

The size of the hole.

First, establish exactly what we are explaining.

2025, in the seven games Shedeur Sanders started: Fannin had 30 receptions for 335 yards and five touchdowns. That is roughly 48 receiving yards per game.

2026, through six games with Deshaun Watson: 19 catches for 166 yards and three touchdowns. Roughly 28 yards per game.

The gap is about twenty yards a game, and it is real. A tight end who was a primary target has become a secondary one.

One piece of context before we start, because it matters for the sample: Fannin's 2025 season ended early. He re-aggravated an injury in Week 17 against Pittsburgh after catching a touchdown, and did not finish the year. So those seven games are what we have.

Now let us go find the missing yards. There are four places they went, and only one of them is the quarterback.

Cause one. There are simply fewer plays.

The largest single share, and the video names it well with a phrase worth stealing: the starvation effect.

Receiving yards are not only a function of how open you get. They are a function of how long your offense stays on the field. When a drive dies on second-and-fifteen after a sack, the tight end's route is never thrown. He can be open, trusted and targeted, and still finish with three catches, because the offense ran 48 plays instead of 68.

Monken said exactly this, and the specificity is what makes it credible:

"First half the other day, we didn't have a lot of plays, didn't have a lot of production, so no one's going to touch it very much. The better we get on offense, the more we're able to stack plays and put drives together, you're going to see more production by our top-end guys."

The evidence is brutal. Against Baltimore, Cleveland had 46 total yards and three first downs in the entire first half. It finished with 237 total yards and went five for sixteen on third down. For the season, Cleveland averages eighteen point six points per game, twenty-seventh in the league, and, the number that explains everything, one point per game in the first quarter, thirtieth in the National Football League.

You cannot feed four pass-catchers on 46 first-half yards. You can barely feed one.

This is the biggest share of the twenty yards, and it has nothing to do with who is throwing.

Cause two. The game script flipped completely.

The second share is the one almost everybody forgets, and it is substantial.

In 2025, Cleveland was losing. Shedeur Sanders went three and four in his seven starts on a team that finished nowhere. A team that trails throws, and throws often, and throws underneath, which is precisely where a tight end eats. Trailing teams inflate tight-end numbers the way blowouts inflate backup running back numbers.

In 2026, Cleveland is four and two and in first place. Three of those four wins were by one score. This team protects leads, leans on a defense ranked second in the league in sacks, and plays for field position. Monken's own stated formula is "that's going to win if we end every possession with a kick."

A team built that way throws less. Fannin's share of a smaller pie, in a season where the pie is deliberately smaller, produces smaller numbers no matter who is at quarterback.

Fewer yards, more wins. That is a trade almost every tight end in the league would take, and Fannin said as much himself.

Cause three. The room got crowded.

The third share is simple competition, and Monken named it himself when he listed who will see more as the offense improves: Harold, along with Denzel and Casey, Denzel Boston and KC Concepcion, and he credited Jerry Jeudy for coming up big over the last few weeks.

Look at the room Fannin is sharing now. Denzel Boston leads the receivers with 16 catches for 330 yards and three touchdowns. KC Concepcion has 18 catches for 149 yards as a rookie first-round pick. And Jerry Jeudy just went four-for-four for 69 yards against Baltimore, including the 33-yard throw on third and five that set up the winning kick.

Last season Cleveland did not have those three producing. Now it does.

Fannin's share would have shrunk even with a perfect quarterback, simply because Cleveland spent premium draft capital on receivers and those receivers are now playing. That is not a problem. That is the plan working.

Cause four, and it is real. The throws.

Now the share that does belong to the passing game, because pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

A reporter at Wednesday's press conference made the point precisely: Fannin has been targeted around nine times in the last two games and the connection has not been there. That is not starvation. If the ball is going his way and the play is not being completed, something else is wrong.

And the mechanics point in one direction. Deshaun Watson has been sacked 24 times, the most in the National Football League, with Justin Herbert second at 21. He ranks twenty-eighth in time to throw at three point oh four seconds and thirty-fifth in average air yards per attempt at three point six.

A tight end lives in the middle of the field on quick, timing-based throws. Those are the first routes a quarterback abandons when he does not trust the pocket, and the first concepts a coordinator cuts when protection is failing.

Monken has been honest about the cause, and it is not what people assume. He says Watson's determination not to turn the ball over is itself producing the problem: it makes him hold the ball, work into tight windows and absorb sacks. His framing of the dilemma was exact: "How do you balance that early in the game of letting it rip and yet maybe putting the ball in harm's way?"

Watson's own answer was equally direct. The first-half problem, he said, was "just trying to get the ball out," and he should not be trying to read every disguise and rotation.

So here is the honest accounting.

Twenty yards a game, allocated. These are judgments, not laboratory measurements, and we will say so plainly, but they are judgments with evidence behind them.

Roughly half, about ten yards a game, is volume. Forty-six first-half yards, 237 total, five for sixteen on third down, one point a quarter. The offense is not on the field. Nobody eats.

Roughly a quarter, five yards a game, is game script and the crowded room combined. A winning, low-scoring, defense-first team with three other mouths to feed throws a tight end fewer balls by design.

Roughly a quarter, five yards a game, is the passing game itself. Nine targets in two games without connection is a real signal, and 24 sacks with a three point oh four-second clock is a real mechanical problem.

Which means the fair verdict is the one the head coach gave. It is mostly not the quarterback, and it is partly the quarterback. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing, and any version of this story that drops one of them is selling you something.

And the number that reframes all of it.

Here is the statistic nobody covering this story has put next to Fannin's, and it changes the picture completely.

Deshaun Watson's fourth quarter this season: forty of sixty-three, 488 yards, four touchdowns, one interception, a one oh one point eight passer rating.

His season overall: sixty-three point eight percent, eleven hundred seventy-four yards, seven touchdowns, two interceptions, a ninety-two point oh rating. Only three quarterbacks with at least five starts have thrown fewer interceptions.

So the man is genuinely good in the fourth quarter and ordinary before it. All four Cleveland wins came on fourth-quarter scoring drives he led, tying Josh Allen and Bo Nix for the most in the league.

Now connect it to the tight end. A tight end's volume is built in the first three quarters, on early downs, in rhythm, on the drives that sustain. That is precisely the stretch of football where this offense does not exist. By the time Watson starts playing like a one oh one point eight quarterback, Cleveland is in two-minute mode throwing to the sideline.

Harold Fannin is not being frozen out. He is being scheduled out, by an offense that only works in the last fifteen minutes.

And the fix is narrower than everyone thinks.

So what actually gets Harold Fannin back? Not a quarterback change, and not a plea for more targets. Two play types, both on first down.

The first is play-action off a real run game. Play-action works by making a linebacker take one step forward, and the space that opens behind him is exactly where a tight end runs. Cleveland has barely been able to use it, because a run game at three yards a carry does not make anybody take that step. This is the direct link between Quinshon Judkins' ankle and Fannin's stat line, and it is why Wednesday's practice, Judkins and Dylan Sampson both back, the full back room for the first time since the opener, matters more to the tight end than to anyone else on the roster.

The second is quick-game over the middle on early downs. Slants, stick routes, seams, throws that leave on the third step. Watson's time to throw is three point oh four seconds, twenty-eighth in the league. Those concepts cut that number by design, which fixes the sack problem and the tight end problem with the same call.

Notice that neither of these is a favor to a player. Both are the exact medicine for the 24 sacks and the one-point first quarters. Getting Harold Fannin the ball is not a side project. It is what fixing this offense looks like from the inside.

And Fannin's answer is the best thing in this story.

He was asked directly whether he is frustrated. His answer:

"I'm cool with it. It doesn't really bother me. Obviously happy that we're winning, and we're going to continue to keep coming out with wins whether I have catches or not. The main thing is to go out there and win."

And on why the opportunities have not come:

"Just how it plays out. I'm going to continue to go out there and do my job to the best of my abilities, and if it come my way, it come my way."

The video called that the most professional thing you will hear from a young player this week, and it is right. No complaints, no anonymous grumbling, no agent leaking frustration to a reporter. A second-year tight end watching his numbers halve, in a contract-relevant season, saying the main thing is to win.

Cleveland has now produced two of these in one week, Jerry Jeudy handling a reduced role and then catching everything, and Fannin saying this. Whatever else is wrong in that building, the locker room is not one of the problems.

The Shedeur comparison. Fair, but not clean.

The comparison everybody keeps making, Fannin with Shedeur Sanders versus Fannin with Deshaun Watson, needs handling with care, because it is being used as a weapon in an argument it cannot settle.

What it legitimately shows: with Sanders, Fannin was a primary target. With Watson, he is a secondary one. That is true and the numbers are the numbers.

What it cannot show: that Sanders is the better quarterback. Those seven games came on a team that was losing, throwing to catch up, with a thinner receiving room, under a different head coach, in a different offense. And the same stretch produced Sanders' sixty-eight point one passer rating on fifty-six point six percent passing, the lowest rating in the NFL among quarterbacks with at least 200 attempts.

You can have a tight end's best stretch and the league's worst passer rating in the same seven games. That is what a losing team throwing from behind looks like, and it is why volume comparisons across seasons prove so little.

One claim we could not verify.

A note on sourcing, because we hold ourselves to this.

The video says Monken told a live television audience at halftime that Watson was leaving the pocket too quickly, and treats it as the sharpening of a message. We could not find that comment in any transcript or report, and we are not going to repeat it as fact.

What is on the record is adjacent but different. After the Jets game, Monken said of Watson's scrambling, "That's an athletic quarterback issue and trying to make plays," and added, "You've got to get the ball out." On Monday he said the offensive problems "start with me, and then our offensive staff, and then the guys that we put on the field." And on Wednesday he said Watson can be better moving through his progressions and should avoid overthinking his reads.

That is a coach applying steadily increasing pressure in public. It is not a halftime callout, and the real version does not need the embellishment.

And the radio segment, handled fairly.

The local radio discussion in that video gets two things right and one thing wrong.

Right, and generously so: the Jets loss was not all on Watson. The hosts listed it honestly, Watson got Cleveland the lead, a runner went out of bounds and stopped the clock, a snap sailed over the punter's head, and Cleveland threw twice late when it could have run clock. That is a fair accounting of a team loss, offered by people who had every excuse to blame one man.

Right again: the plea for tempo. "I would still love to see us use some hurry-up, do some tempo, sugar huddle, something in the first half." That is the correct ask, and Monken has confirmed the option exists: "We've considered that a little bit... we certainly have that at our disposal." Against a Tennessee team allowing opponents to control games, Sunday is the week to find out.

Wrong: the idea that Watson plays only because of his contract, "the Haslam rule." That argument died in September. Four fourth-quarter game-winning drives, tied for the most in the National Football League, a one oh one point eight fourth-quarter rating, and a team in first place is not a payroll decision. The hosts half-admit it themselves: "the only difference this time is he's winning games." That is not a small difference. That is the whole thing.

What we are not carrying.

"Monken called out Watson." He listed five causes and put the quarterback fourth.

The halftime television quote, unverified.

"The Haslam rule." A team in first place with four game-winning drives is not starting a quarterback out of financial embarrassment.

And the implication that Fannin is unhappy. He said the opposite, twice, on the record, and nobody has reported otherwise.

Names and numbers.

Harold Fannin Junior, not Fannon or Finnon. Todd Monken, not Monin or Monkin. KC Concepcion, not Casey Conpsion. Denzel Boston. Jerry Jeudy, not Judy. Deshaun Watson. Shedeur Sanders. Travis Switzer is the offensive coordinator; Mike Bajakian is the quarterbacks coach.

What to watch at Tennessee.

Fannin's first-quarter targets. Not his game total, his first-quarter total. If the ball goes to the tight end on early downs in the opening drive, the fix has started.

Cleveland's first-quarter points. One a game, thirtieth in football. Anything above a field goal is news.

The snap count on designed runs. Quinshon Judkins and Dylan Sampson both practiced; Monken is optimistic for both. A functioning run game is what puts a tight end back in rhythm.

And one for the storyline file: Shedeur Sanders' best professional game came against Tennessee last December, twenty-three of forty-two for 364 yards and three touchdowns. Sunday, a different quarterback faces the same opponent, with the league's worst scoring offense on the other sideline.

Where we stop.

We are not going to tell you Harold Fannin is being wasted, because his head coach named him first among the players whose production will rise and the targets are already coming.

We are not going to tell you the quarterback is blameless, because nine targets in two games without a connection is a real number and 24 sacks is a worse one.

And we are not going to tell you this is a controversy. It is a tight end having a quieter season on a better team, and saying out loud that he is fine with it.

The bottom line.

Twenty yards a game, accounted for. About half is volume, 46 first-half yards, 237 total, one point a quarter. About a quarter is game script and a crowded room, a four and two team protecting leads with three other mouths to feed. About a quarter is the passing game, nine targets in two games without a connection, behind a line that has allowed a league-high 24 sacks.

And the number that explains the shape of it: Watson's fourth-quarter rating is one oh one point eight; his season rating is ninety-two point oh. A tight end's work happens in the first three quarters. This offense happens in the fourth.

Monken did not call out Deshaun Watson. He said "protection, calls, players, quarterback, everybody", and then described the fix in one sentence: stack plays, sustain drives, and the production follows.

Harold Fannin's answer to all of it was four words long: "The main thing is to go out there and win." Ktl Report will take that over a stat line every week.
