"Own it and fight like hell to fix it." Todd Monken's Week 2 press conference, line by line.
 
Before every game, the head coach stands at a podium and tells you what he's thinking, if you know how to listen.
 
Todd Monken's final press conference before Tampa Bay was more revealing than most. He addressed the run game, the snap count, Harold Fannin Junior's involvement, Deshaun Watson's decisiveness, the Buccaneers' pressure schemes, and, in the most interesting moment of the session, a change to how Cleveland practices that amounts to an admission that something was broken.
 
He also said something almost nobody picked up on. This game is a homecoming for him too.
 
Ktl Report is doing this one differently. First, the key parts of what he actually said, in his own words. Then our read on each one. What's convincing, what isn't, and what Sunday will prove.
 
Let's start with the press conference itself.
 
On Harold Fannin Junior getting only three targets in Week 1, Monken said this: "We have a number of guys you'd like to get the ball to, but we just didn't stack plays early enough in the game and ended up with 49 total snaps. So that's not going to nearly be enough to get the guys that we want to get the ball to. But he's certainly a weapon."
 
On what went wrong in the run game: "Some of it was that, some of it is they're really good, and some of it was our first game collectively. We certainly can do it better schematically, coaching and playing. I expect us as the year goes on to continue to get better with the offensive line playing together. We're new as a staff. These aren't excuses. We certainly have to do it better."
 
On how a young team handles a blowout loss: "I've seen it like they've handled every week that I've been here, and that's go to work. It wasn't about effort. It wasn't about the work that we put in. But how do we direct it? How do we continue to direct our effort in the right direction?"
 
On Deshaun Watson: "He was certainly more decisive. He was certainly more decisive in terms of throwing the ball down the field, which is what we want to see. And we're looking forward to building on that."
 
On Tampa Bay's defense: "They challenge all of your run rules and they challenge all of your protection rules with their ability to bring pressure from every edge. Whether it's your strong side edge, your weak side. They'll bring secondary pressures. It'll be a long day if you're not on top of your identifications."
 
On Bucky Irving: "He's explosive and he's got excellent contact balance. He's an explosive waiting to happen."
 
On whether the Jacksonville heat was the problem: "I don't think that the prep was the reason that we didn't play up to our standard. If I did, I think we would have tried some things differently. We just got to play more efficiently. It really came down to that, not really the heat."
 
And on changing practice to fix the pre-snap operation, shortening the play clock and running it off from the sideline, he said this: "Well, we're going to find out, aren't we? But that's what you do. If you want to change something, you've got to change how you do it. We weren't getting the desired results. And I thought we were fine, but obviously we weren't. So that's what you're going to do in life, right? You're going to own it and fight like hell to fix it."
 
Now, our read.
 
Number one. Forty-nine snaps.
 
Start with the most important number in the entire press conference, because it explains nearly everything else. Forty-nine total offensive snaps.
 
For context, a normal NFL offense runs somewhere in the mid-sixties per game. Forty-nine is what happens when you go three-and-out repeatedly and fall behind early.
 
And Monken identified the cause precisely. "We didn't stack plays early enough in the game."
 
That's coach language for exactly what we documented in our Jacksonville breakdown here at Ktl Report. Six consecutive empty possessions to open the game. Interception, punt, punt, fumble, punt, end of half.
 
Here's why the snap count matters so much for the Fannin question. Every weapon in an offense is rationed by snaps. You can't design touches for a tight end, feed a running back, and work in two rookie receivers when you only get 49 plays, and a third of them come after the game is decided.
 
So Monken's answer is honest and correct. But notice what it quietly concedes. Fannin's three targets weren't a design choice. They were a symptom. The offense didn't fail to use its best weapon because the plan ignored him. It failed because the plan never got far enough to reach him.
 
Which means the fix isn't "throw it to Fannin more." The fix is converting the first two third downs of the game. Everything downstream follows from that.
 
Number two. "These aren't excuses."
 
The run game answer is one of the better ones he's given since taking the job, and it deserves credit.
 
He named three causes, and he ranked them fairly. Game script, because falling behind limited the run. Opponent quality, because Jacksonville's front is legitimately good. And newness, because it was the first game together as a staff and as an offensive line.
 
Then he immediately closed the escape hatch himself. "These aren't excuses. We certainly have to do it better."
 
That's the correct structure. Context, then ownership, in that order, without letting the context swallow the ownership. Compare it to the Watson press conference we analyzed earlier this week, where the ratio ran the other way. One sentence of accountability, four of explanation.
 
And the offensive line point is genuinely true. George Warhop's group is rebuilt. Zion Johnson and Titus Howard in the starting mix, Austin Barber at guard, a rookie at left tackle. Cohesion up front is a skill of its own, and it improves with snaps together, measurably.
 
The honest question is simply how fast. Because the calendar doesn't wait for chemistry.
 
Number three. "More decisive, down the field."
 
Now the answer that deserves the most scrutiny, because it connects to a claim Monken has been making since August.
 
He said Watson was "more decisive in terms of throwing the ball down the field, which is what we want to see."
 
Remember the August version of this. "I'm confident in Deshaun and our ability to push the ball down the field. Otherwise, we wouldn't be starting him." We wrote at the time that he'd chosen to defend the exact category where the evidence was thinnest. The offense had finished thirty-first in explosive plays, at 8.2 percent, and Watson's preseason work in Chicago had produced no completion beyond ten air yards.
 
So is this new claim supported?
 
Partially. Watson finished Week 1 at 16 of 22 for 205 yards, which is meaningfully more aggressive than the preseason sample. If the coaching staff saw more willingness to attack downfield on film, that's plausible and worth taking seriously. Coaches see the full coaching film angle, and the broadcast doesn't show you which receivers were open.
 
But "decisive" and "effective" are different words. A quarterback can decide quickly and still produce ten points. And the staff has now tied its public case for Watson to the downfield game twice.
 
That makes Sunday a direct test of a specific claim, not a vague one. If Cleveland produces genuine chunk plays in Tampa, Monken's evaluation looks right, and we'll say so plainly. If the offense lives underneath again, this becomes the second time the stated reasoning didn't show up on the field.
 
Number four. The admission hidden in the operation answer.
 
This is the single most important moment in the press conference, and it's being underplayed.
 
Cleveland changed how it practices this week. A shorter play clock in practice. Running time off from the sideline. The goal is to fix the pre-snap operation. The choppy huddles, the slow personnel groupings, the clock burning before snaps.
 
And Monken's explanation was unusually candid. "I thought we were fine, but obviously we weren't."
 
Sit with that sentence, because it's a coach admitting his own evaluation was wrong. Not the players' execution. His read of where the team was.
 
That matters for two reasons.
 
First, it's the right response. "If you want to change something, you've got to change how you do it" is genuinely good coaching philosophy. Repeating the same preparation and expecting different results is the most common failure in the sport. He identified a problem, changed the process, and said so out loud. "Own it and fight like hell to fix it" is exactly the attitude this fan base has been asking for.
 
Second, it quietly confirms the operational problem was real. Remember the summer-long debate about pre-snap operation. The cadence narrative aimed at Shedeur Sanders, which a credentialed film analyst later found didn't match the tape, and his observation that the false starts and miscommunication he actually saw were more associated with Watson's side.
 
We're not claiming Monken's answer settles that debate. He didn't name anyone, and an operation problem belongs to the whole offense. But it does confirm that the thing the operation critics were pointing at in August existed on Sunday.
 
And his answer to whether it'll work was the most honest line of the day. "Well, we're going to find out, aren't we?"
 
Yes. We are.
 
Number five. The heat answer, and why it's good.
 
Small moment, but revealing. He was offered an easy excuse, the Florida heat in Jacksonville, and he declined it. Coaches take that exit constantly. Weather, travel, crowd noise, officiating. A coach who won't use the available excuse is a coach who expects to be judged on execution. That's a standard fans can work with.
 
Number six. The Tampa Bay problem.
 
His scouting of the Buccaneers was specific, and it should concern Browns fans.
 
Pressure from every edge. Strong side, weak side, secondary pressures. A defense designed to test every protection rule and every run rule an offense has.
 
Now put that next to what we know about Cleveland's front. A rebuilt line playing its second game together. A rookie at left tackle. Five sacks allowed in Week 1. An offense still fixing its pre-snap identification.
 
Monken said it himself. "It'll be a long day if you're not."
 
That's not a throwaway line. It's the head coach identifying the precise stress point, communication and identification up front, that his own team just struggled with. If Jacksonville's plan was "make it a drop-back game," Tampa's is "make you figure out who's coming before the snap." Both attack the same weakness.
 
And on the other side, Bucky Irving. "An explosive waiting to happen," with excellent contact balance. After a Week 1 where Cleveland's defense couldn't get pressure on Trevor Lawrence, a back who breaks first contact is a genuine nightmare. Tackling through the first level decides whether this defense recovers or repeats.
 
And now, the part nobody noticed. This is Monken's homecoming too.
 
Here's the detail that got buried under the Baker Mayfield storyline. Todd Monken used to work in Tampa Bay.
 
He credited Dirk Koetter, then the Buccaneers' head coach and someone he'd worked with previously in Jacksonville, with hiring him as offensive coordinator and giving him play-calling duties in 2018. In Monken's words, that "changed my track forever." A coach who'd called plays for thirty years, handing the keys to someone else.
 
He named the front office people he still considers friends, including general manager Jason Licht, and described those as three great years, adding one honest line. "We just didn't win enough."
 
He also spoke warmly about Tampa's receivers, noting he was there when they drafted Chris Godwin, and that his previous staff did research on Jalen McMillan. Players, he said, that any quarterback can trust.
 
So Sunday isn't one reunion. It's two. Baker Mayfield returns to face the team that drafted him. And Todd Monken returns to face the franchise that made him a play-caller.
 
That's a genuinely human detail in a week full of statistics.
 
So here's the bottom line. Here's the Ktl Report read on the whole press conference.
 
What was convincing: the snap-count diagnosis, the run game answer that named causes without hiding behind them, the refusal to blame the heat, and above all the operation admission. "I thought we were fine, but obviously we weren't." That's a coach correcting himself in public, which is rarer than it should be.
 
What needs proving: the claim that Watson was more decisive attacking downfield. It's now the second time the staff has tied its case to the deep game. Sunday tests it directly.
 
What should worry you: his own description of Tampa's defense. Pressure from every edge, testing every protection rule. It lands squarely on the exact weakness Cleveland showed in Jacksonville.
 
He ended on the right note. "We're going to find out, aren't we?"
 
That's the only honest answer anyone can give right now. Press conferences are promises. Sunday at one o'clock in Tampa, they get graded.
