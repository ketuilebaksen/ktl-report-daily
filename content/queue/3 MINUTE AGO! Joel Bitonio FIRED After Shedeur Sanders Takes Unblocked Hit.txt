What the tape would actually have to show. We are not running the sabotage story, so here is the evidentiary test it would have to pass, and why this video fails every part of it.

First, the title, and this one we have to stop on.

The title is "Three Minutes Ago! Joel Bitonio FIRED After Shedeur Sanders Takes Unblocked Hit."

Joel Bitonio has not been fired. No release, no waiver, no termination, no announcement, nothing. The video itself never claims he was, the word "fired" appears nowhere in the fifteen minutes of script. The title invents a firing of a named, living professional and puts it in capital letters.

And the body of the video goes somewhere worse. It accuses two named Cleveland Browns offensive linemen of deliberately sabotaging plays, of intentionally not blocking so that their own quarterback would get hit. The script uses the word "sabotaging" in its opening sentence and returns again and again to "is this negligence or is this something else entirely?", "zero effort," "refusing to do their jobs," and "an environment that appears to be actively working against him."

That is an accusation of deliberately losing football games on purpose. In this sport there is no more serious charge you can make about a player. It ends careers. It is the one allegation the league itself investigates rather than debates.

So we are not going to carry it, and we are not going to write the version of this article that treats it as an open question. Here at Ktl Report we have refused three stories in the last month, a trade argument built on a player's blood clots, a house-hunting vlog offered as proof of a trade, and a sideline clip sold as a locker-room collapse. This is the fourth refusal and it is the easiest one we have had to make.

But refusing is not the same as having nothing to say. The useful thing we can do is show you exactly what evidence a claim like this would require, because once you have that list in front of you, you will never be fooled by a video like this again. That list is this article.

The six things the tape would have to show.

If someone tells you a professional offensive lineman deliberately let his quarterback get hit, these are the six questions that have to be answered before the claim deserves one minute of your attention. Not four of six. All six.

One. Does the footage exist, and have you seen it?

The video does not show it. This is the single most remarkable fact about fifteen minutes of film analysis, and we want to be absolutely precise about it.

Twice, the script announces it is about to roll the evidence. The first time:

"Don't take my word for it. Here is the exact moment the film analysis describes what Jack Conklin does on that fourth and one play. Watch this."

And what plays is a clip of a quarterback saying: "Uh, really, everything happens for a reason. Um, God has a plan for myself. He has a plan for you. Um, at the end of the day, you just got to continue to prove yourself right."

That is Cam Ward of the Tennessee Titans talking about the draft. It is not film. It is not a block. It is not Jack Conklin. It is an unrelated interview answer, and the script even confirms the attribution twelve minutes later: "That is what Cam Ward told Shedeur Sanders when the draft was falling apart around him."

And it happens again. At the thirteen-minute mark, where the script promises to recontextualise everything, the audio that plays is the video's own opening monologue repeating itself, "What up guys? This your boy...", the intro, spliced in as evidence.

So the one piece of proof that is promised on camera, twice, is in both cases something else entirely. We did not have to hunt for that. It is in the transcript.

Question one: failed. The video never shows the footage it is describing.

Two. Who identified it, and are they accountable for being wrong?

The script answers this one honestly, and the answer destroys its own case:

"The film analysis that surfaced did not come from a team beat reporter. It did not come from an ESPN segment. It came from people on social media who were simply watching the tape and describing what they saw. Regular people, film watchers, fans."

The video presents that as a virtue, the authentic truth-tellers versus the compromised establishment. Read it again as a sourcing statement. An accusation that would end two men's careers rests on anonymous accounts, no named analyst, no credentialed film breakdown, and nobody who can be held responsible if it is false.

And note what is missing from that sentence: a single name. Not one. Fifteen minutes about jersey numbers being "visible" and not one identifiable human being willing to attach their own name to the claim.

Question two: failed.

Three. Is the alternative explanation ruled out, or even mentioned?

This is the one that matters most to anybody who actually watches offensive line play, and the video does not attempt it.

On a fourth-and-one near the red zone, two defenders coming through untouched is one of the most common outcomes in football, and almost none of the causes involve anybody's effort level. Among them: a run-pass option where the lineman is coached not to engage, because engaging downfield is a penalty. A slide protection where one man is deliberately left unblocked and the back or the quarterback is responsible for him. A blitz the centre did not identify, which sends two men to one blocker and leaves another with air. A pulling scheme where a guard's assignment is to get to a spot and the defender crosses his face. A quarterback keeping the ball on a read that the line was blocking the other way.

And the one the video actually describes without realising it. It says one lineman "gets into a motion in the backfield before the snap", a lineman moving in the backfield before the snap is not a man refusing to block. It is a man executing a designed movement, which is exactly what pulling, pre-snap shifting and fold blocking look like from the stands.

A film analysis that cannot distinguish a pull from a quit is not a film analysis.

Question three: failed, and not even attempted.

Four. Does the alleged pattern survive a check against the actual roster?

Here is where the story falls apart on facts that have nothing to do with effort or intent.

The video says the footage is from "the last time we seen him on the field going up against the San Francisco Forty-Niners." Cleveland's game against San Francisco, a twenty-six to eight defeat, was played in late November twenty twenty-five. That is roughly a year ago. It was Shedeur Sanders' second NFL start. Calling it "the last time we seen him on the field" in November twenty twenty-six, after he has just started and thrown for two hundred ninety-seven yards against New Orleans, is not a small slip. It is a year-old clip presented as current.

The video then compares the alleged pattern to "not Dillon Gabriel, not Joe Flacco." Both of those are twenty twenty-five quarterbacks. Flacco was the Week One starter that season, Gabriel was the backup and Sanders was third on the depth chart. The comparison set is a season old, which confirms what the game reference already told us.

And the names do not match this season's offensive line. The group that has been starting for Cleveland in twenty twenty-six, the one everyone has been scrutinising for the thirty-three sacks, includes Spencer Fano at left tackle, Austin Barber at right guard, Elgton Jenkins at centre, and Tytus Howard at right tackle, the man Cleveland traded for from Houston and signed to a sixty-three-million-dollar contract, with Dawand Jones as the designated backup at the position. We have written about every one of those men in the last three weeks, using the team's own injury reports and depth charts.

The right tackle the video spends four minutes accusing is not the right tackle who has been playing right tackle.

Question four: failed.

Five. Is the subject described accurately?

The video calls Shedeur Sanders a rookie at least six times, and refers to the twenty-two year old quarterback.

He is in his second NFL season. He started seven games last year. And on his age, we will use his own number, from his own mouth, in a press conference ten days ago: "I had a lot of ups and downs in my life so far, even though I'm only like twenty-four."

A video that cannot establish what season its subject is in, or how old he is, is not a video that has established what two linemen were thinking on one snap.

Question five: failed.

Six. Has anybody with standing said anything remotely like this?

No. Not one person.

Not the head coach, who has talked about the offensive line at length every week and whose language has been consistently and specifically about scheme, calls and execution, "certainly got to call it better, certainly got to scheme it better, got to protect better", and who, after the Saints game, declined even to blame a named player for a failed two-point conversion: "We didn't execute it."

Not the quarterback, who has gone out of his way to defend the people around him. Asked about the offense's slow starts, he said: "I don't think it been bad. How many wins we got?" Asked about communicating with the line, he said: "I think we all got a great relationship with them. I think to the outside world it's way more complex than what it is." Asked about Deshaun Watson, he refused three separate invitations to elevate himself. The man the video says is being sabotaged keeps publicly defending the men it accuses.

Not a single beat reporter, not the league, not a teammate, not an opposing player, not an agent.

Question six: failed.

Six questions. Zero passed. That is not a story with holes in it. That is not a story.

And the Cam Ward clip, restored.

One more thing, because a real person's words are being used to carry an implication he plainly did not make.

Cam Ward, the Tennessee quarterback, was asked about Shedeur Sanders and said this:

"Um, I don't know what's going on in their building. I don't know what he has to deal with on a daily basis in and out of the building. Um, so, you know, I just try to focus on the Titans and doing what I can do to help us get the wins."

The video turns that into evidence of a cover-up: "Even Cam Ward is acknowledging that there is something happening inside that Cleveland building that he cannot fully see from the outside... What does that tell you about the transparency coming out of that organization?"

Read the quote. A player on another team was asked about a friend's situation and said, in the politest possible terms, I don't know, that's not my business, I'm focused on my own team. That is the single most common answer in professional sport. It is what every player says about every other player's club, every week, in every league.

"I don't know what's going on in their building" means "I don't know." It does not mean "something is going on." Turning a refusal to speculate into proof of the thing being speculated about is the oldest trick in this genre, and it is being done here to a man who was being kind about his friend.

And the other Ward line the video uses, "everything happens for a reason, God has a plan", was something he said to Sanders during the draft to encourage him. It is being played as the sound of a lineman quitting on a block. That is not an interpretation. It is a substitution.

Now the real story, because there is one.

Here is the thing that makes this video so frustrating: there is a genuine, serious, well-documented problem with Cleveland's offensive line, and you do not need to invent a conspiracy to talk about it. We have written about it almost every week.

The actual numbers. Cleveland has allowed thirty-three sacks in eight games. Deshaun Watson was sacked eight times in one afternoon in Pittsburgh, in a game where the Browns ran for thirty-four yards and were flagged for four holding penalties. Against New Orleans they committed six penalties in the first quarter alone, offense, defense and special teams.

And the honest distribution of blame, which we did the arithmetic on at the time. Of the eight sacks in Pittsburgh, seven different Steelers got one, and three and a half of them came from a linebacker and two interior defenders, players who do not line up across from a tackle at all. That is not an effort story. It is a protection-scheme and interior-line story, which is exactly what the coaching staff has been saying out loud.

What the staff has actually said, in order, unprompted: "certainly got to call it better. Certainly got to scheme it better. Got to protect better." Scheme before execution. And on the first-quarter problem, Monken named four causes himself: turnovers, penalties, lost-yardage plays, and failing to stop the run early.

And the structural fact that explains most of it. Cleveland is starting seven offensive players in their first or second NFL season, including both starting tackles and a starting guard from the last two draft classes. That is a young offensive line learning protection calls in real time. Young lines give up pressure. They also get better, which is the entire reason you play them.

None of that requires anybody to be a villain. It is a hard, boring, real problem with a hard, boring, real fix: buy a lineman, keep playing the young ones, call it better. At Ktl Report we said that before the deadline and we are saying it now that the deadline has passed.

And a word about why this particular story is dangerous.

We want to be direct about this, because it is the reason we spent an entire article on a refusal rather than a quick dismissal.

An accusation of deliberately losing is not a spicy take. It is the most damaging thing you can say about an athlete. It goes beyond "he played badly" into "he betrayed his team," and it follows a man into every contract negotiation, every locker room and every job interview for the rest of his life. Two named men, with families, with careers, with reputations they cannot repair from a YouTube comment section, are being accused of it on the basis of footage the video never shows, identified by people it cannot name, with no alternative explanation considered, from a game played a year ago, against a roster it has not checked.

And the title adds a firing that did not happen.

We will criticise Cleveland's offensive line all day. We have. We will say the protection has been unacceptable, that thirty-three sacks is a season-defining number, that the front office should have bought help, and that specific units have played below the standard. All of that is fair, checkable and useful.

What we will not do is tell you that professional football players are losing games on purpose because an anonymous account said so and a video promised footage it never rolled. If that story ever becomes real, if a named reporter, the league, or the club itself produces evidence, we will cover it properly, in full, and say that we were slow to it. Until then the answer is no, and the answer will keep being no.
