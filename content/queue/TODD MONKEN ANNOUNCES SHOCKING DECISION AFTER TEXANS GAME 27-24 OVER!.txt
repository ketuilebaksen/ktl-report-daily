Things coaches don't say. There was no announcement and no decision. There were nine sentences a head coach is not supposed to say out loud.

First, the title. There is no decision in here at all.

The title is "Todd Monken Announces Shocking Decision After Texans Game twenty-seven to twenty-four Over!"

Monken announced nothing. We read every line of this press conference twice looking for it. There is no personnel decision, no depth-chart change, no quarterback declaration, no roster move, no scheme change and no plan for after the bye. The word "decision" does not appear in the transcript in any form.

What the twelve minutes actually contain is a head coach, forty-five minutes after winning a game his team was losing seventeen to nothing, talking almost entirely about character. The phrase he used was "they don't blink", and he used it five times.

So here is what we are going to do with it instead, and it is more useful than a decision would have been.

The reason this press conference is worth your twelve minutes is that Monken kept saying things head coaches are trained not to say. Not gaffes. Not controversies. Sentences that admit uncertainty, credit other people, refuse the credit offered to him, or simply sound like a person rather than a podium.

We counted nine of them. Here at Ktl Report that is the frame: nine sentences a coach is not supposed to say, and what each one actually tells you about this football team.

Number one. I don't know. If I knew that, then we would have had them dialed up last week.

The question was a compliment. A reporter noted that Cleveland had struggled in goal-to-go situations the previous week and this week dialled up a series of runs for different players that worked, and asked why they were so effective.

The answer a coach is supposed to give involves the word "preparation." Here is what Monken said:

"I don't know. If I knew that, then we would have had them dialed up last week."

That is a head coach declining an easy compliment by pointing out that if he had the answer, he would have used it seven days earlier.

And then he gave the real explanation, which is more interesting than the compliment:

"It's a little bit more in our wheelhouse. I mean, we've played two teams that we go against every week, and that's a little bit of an excuse. We got to be better against whoever we play. Can't sit there and say, 'well, you're going to suck against the teams that don't play what we go against every week.' No, that's not what I mean. Just, a little more comfort level there."

Read what he did in that answer. He offered a genuine explanation, the last two opponents play schemes Cleveland sees in practice every week, so the familiarity helped, and then he immediately took it away from himself: "that's a little bit of an excuse. We got to be better against whoever we play."

A coach handing you a legitimate reason and then refusing to hide behind it, inside one answer. That is the fifth consecutive press conference in which we have watched him do something like this.

Number two. I've never called a play I thought wouldn't work ever. Not one time in my life.

This is the funniest and most honest thing anybody in football said this weekend.

"And we don't always know they're going to work. I've never called a play I thought wouldn't work ever. Not one time in my life. My wife back there says, 'Why don't you just call the plays that work? It's so simple.' And so I get it."

Stop and consider what that admits. Every single play that has ever failed in his career was a play he believed in when he called it. Not one was a guess. They all looked right.

That is the most honest description of coaching anybody will give you, and it is why the "just call better plays" argument, which every fan base in the world makes every Sunday, is empty. His wife makes the joke, he repeats the joke against himself, and the joke contains the whole answer.

Number three. I wish him and I together would play better early and coach better early.

This is the one we would put on the thumbnail, and it is the single most important sentence in the press conference.

The question was pure praise: what is it about Deshaun Watson, a sixth fourth-quarter game-winning drive, how does he keep doing this?

The answer:

"I don't know. I wish collectively him and I together would play better early and coach better early, that we didn't have to get to this. And it's not just been them, it's been our team."

He was handed a victory lap and turned it into a self-criticism in eleven words.

And he is right, which is why it matters. We wrote yesterday that Cleveland is setting two opposite records at once, all six wins by game-winning drive, and the worst first-quarter differential of any team with a winning record in the history of the league through ten weeks. Our conclusion was that the two facts are connected: the clutch finishing exists to pay for a deficit the team creates every week.

Monken said the same thing from the podium, unprompted, in the middle of being congratulated: "I wish we didn't have to get to this."

That is not modesty. At Ktl Report we would call it a diagnosis, and it is the same one we published the day before he said it.

Number four. For one week, we solved a little bit of our penalty issue.

And here is the item that nobody in this video noticed, and it may be the most consequential thing on the tape.

"For one week, we solved a little bit of our penalty issue. One week, but we were cleaner that way. Gives you a chance."

Three weeks ago Monken volunteered that Cleveland was thirty-second in the NFL, dead last, in total penalties, and that they were overwhelmingly pre-snap. When a reporter offered him crowd noise as an excuse he refused it: "some of that is true and some of that is not true." Two weeks ago his team committed six penalties in the first quarter in New Orleans.

This week, by his own account, it got cleaner. And they won a game they were losing by seventeen.

Look at how carefully he said it, though: "for one week." "A little bit." "Gives you a chance." That is a man refusing to declare a problem solved on the basis of one afternoon, which is exactly the right posture, and exactly what we asked for yesterday when we printed our test for the rest of the season.

Our test was: does Cleveland start fast after the bye? His own framing of the same thing is: "we're going to have to figure something out about starting a hell of a lot faster."

Number five. Isn't that cool? Ain't that awesome? There's no other way to put it, man.

Asked what it says about his team that they are six and four going into the bye, he laughed and said that.

And then he said the thing underneath it, which is the line that explains why he keeps answering character questions instead of tactical ones:

"They've never blinked from the day I got here in the spring. They've never blinked in the fall from working. They've never blinked when our coaches coach them hard. They don't blink when we get down. They don't blink when things don't always go their way. And that says a lot about our roster and the character of our roster."

Five "blinks" in one answer. And then, immediately:

"And our coaches, our coaches deserve a lot of this credit and the way they coach our guys, and the way our guys accept coaching."

A head coach who credits his players and then credits his assistants and never once credits himself. We have now watched him do that in five consecutive press conferences, and the one time he mentioned himself was to say he wished he had coached better early.

Number six. I don't care what he says. This meant a lot.

Asked about Watson, he interrupted himself to check whether the quarterback was speaking to the media, and then:

"I don't care what he says. This meant a lot. It meant a lot to a lot of guys, that were with the Texans previously. We have a number of those guys on this team and it does mean a lot. Means a lot to me, 'cause it's an AFC game. It's a home game. We're now four and oh at home. And we get the bye. And being six and four, four and oh at home after they stuck us with six road games in the first nine. Pretty cool."

"I don't care what he says" is not a slight, it is the opposite. He is saying the result spoke for the quarterback regardless of how he phrases it afterwards.

And note what he reached for instead of a quarterback narrative: the roster. "A lot of guys that were with the Texans previously." He turned a story about one man beating his old team into a story about a group of men beating their old team, which is both more accurate and, we would guess, deliberate.

"After they stuck us with six road games in the first nine" is also the fourth time he has raised that schedule, and he is entitled to it: four and oh at home, which makes four consecutive home wins for the first time since nineteen sixty-three.

Number seven. Hell no. We're trying to win every play.

This is the exchange we would build a segment around, because it is a coaching philosophy delivered in two words.

A reporter asked whether there was any pause about sending a number one receiver out to return punts, whether the injury risk gave him second thoughts.

"Hell no. We're trying to win every play. That's a play, right? The field position battle, right? I don't look at it that way. I look at it like, I don't know what play is going to change the game. I don't know what play a player is going to get hurt. Hell, they lost Hunter in practice rushing the quarterback. I don't know when it's going to happen. I know that the next play is the most important play, whatever that is, and our best players are going to play. And he wants that. He doesn't want to shy away from that. Any of our players would balk if we said, 'we're not going to do this with you because of XYZ.'"

That is a complete, coherent, defensible position on risk, and the strongest part of it is the example: a player got hurt rushing the quarterback in practice. You cannot protect a man from football by using him less, because the danger is not located in any particular play.

And the last clause is the one players will remember: "any of our players would balk if we said we're not going to do this with you." He is arguing that protecting somebody from a role is itself a cost.

Number eight. I said, no, you got to let it eat.

This is the only genuine piece of tactical news in the whole press conference, and it is excellent.

Asked about Harold Fannin Junior's thirty-yard catch and run on the winning drive, Monken explained the call:

"Actually, we had it, we were going to check it versus cloud if it was a sky look. I said, 'No, you got to let it eat, man. We're not looking for a quarterback draw here at this time. So let's let it eat.' And Deshaun did a great job because we practiced it all week against one high. And I said, 'No, we got to let this thing rip.'"

Translating, because this is worth understanding. The play had a built-in alternative, if the defense showed a particular safety look, the quarterback could check out of the pass into something safer. Monken removed the alternative. He told his quarterback to throw it regardless, because the team had rehearsed that exact throw all week against that exact coverage.

"Let it eat" is the whole decision: trust the week's work over the safety valve. Thirty yards, and Cleveland scored the winning touchdown shortly after.

And then the part that is better than the play call:

"I'm happy for Harold. Harold was a captain this week, and he earned being a captain this week. Up until that point, he had not. He's not only earned it with his play, but with his diligence and his approach every day in terms of being a pro. I'm so proud of him because we know he's got the ability. And we didn't give him the ball enough last week early. We tried early this week. Didn't always work, but we tried, because he's such a weapon for us."

Three things in that, all of them unusual. He revealed that the tight end was named a game captain for the first time. He said plainly that he had not earned it before this week, which is a public standard, applied honestly. And he admitted a coaching error from the previous game: "we didn't give him the ball enough last week early."

That is a head coach grading his own usage of a player, by name, in a victory press conference.

Number nine. I'm done trying to plan out anything.

The last question was the best one: what would you have thought if somebody had told you at the start that you would reach the bye at six and four and that all six wins would come as fourth-quarter comebacks?

"I'm going to answer it like you would answer it. Like, what would that have been on FanDuel, right? And I don't gamble. I don't gamble, but, there's no way. There's no way. But I'm done trying to plan out anything. In all my years in the NFL, I'm done trying to plan out how games are going to go, the ebb and flow of a game, things that go your way that don't go your way. An interference call on third and fifteen, right? They go take the lead. Then we got to battle back, and then we turn it over. I don't have the end of the story. I just know if our guys keep fighting and keep battling, keep scratching and clawing, we always have a chance."

And then the sentence that is the actual answer to the question he was asked:

"I'm proud of our guys irrespective of the record, by the way they approach every day and the way they battle on game day, irrespective of the record. I was proud of them last week. It didn't go our way. It didn't. And I was proud of our guys in a lot of those games. That's what I want them to know. And then you let the results fall where they may, you don't always control that."

"I was proud of them last week. It didn't go our way." He went back to a one-point loss in New Orleans, in the middle of celebrating a win over Houston, to make the point that the standard is not the scoreboard.

That is the closing argument of this whole press conference, and it is why there was no shocking decision in it. The man was not announcing anything. He was explaining what he measures.

And the numbers he gave you, collected.

Buried in all that are five figures worth keeping, four of which he volunteered without being asked:

Six and four, going into the bye.
Four and oh at home, four consecutive home wins for the first time since nineteen sixty-three.
Six and two in one-score games. He said it twice.
Sixth fourth-quarter game-winning drive, which is all six of Cleveland's wins.
Six road games in the first nine, which he has now raised in four straight press conferences.
Down seventeen to nothing in this game, and the comparison he reached for was his own past: "I've seen it before, at Nashville, being down fourteen nothing and continuing to fight."

And the one he gave as a warning rather than a boast: "we're going to have to come back and play a hell of a lot better than we did after the Steelers game."

That is a coach using his own team's recent failure as the standard for the bye week, Cleveland came out of a short week against Pittsburgh and lost in New Orleans. He is telling his players, in public, that he has not forgotten it.

Two more things that deserve a mention.

Jerry Jeudy's sixty-five yard play, including the drop before it.

Asked about the lift from Jeudy's big gain, Monken gave the whole sequence rather than the highlight:

"It's not always that you dial up a play and you get the look you want and you get it protected. Could have thrown it better. But early he had a drop early in the game where we got a drive stopped. But he did a great job of hitting the safety, and Deshaun did a good job. First I thought he overthrew it. Jerry did a great job at tracking it and catching up to it."

A drop that killed a drive, and then a sixty-five-yard play he had to run under. Monken reported both.

And it closes a story we have been following for three weeks. The December twenty twenty-five sideline clip of Jeudy clapping in Shedeur Sanders' face, the "microwave" quote about chemistry taking time, and Jeudy telling a reporter this month that Sanders is "a baller, man." This week he ran under a sixty-five-yard throw from the other quarterback on a day Cleveland came back from seventeen down. The receiver is simply playing.

Denzel Boston returned punts off a toe injury.

"Denzel comes in for KC to return punts and it seemed like he gave you some hidden yardage, guy coming off a toe injury..."
"Man, I'm telling you, that kid personifies our team. Fighting to get back and play, the whole week he's like 'I'm going to play, I'm going to play,' and the medical staff's like 'I don't know, I don't know.' 'No, I'll take whatever I need to take that's legal, NFL standards.' Isn't that cool? And then he gets the punts, and awkward as it was, him catching it, for God's sakes, I'm like, okay. Guy's got great range and the guy loves to play football, man."

Note the correction buried in there: it was a toe, not the calf that appeared on an earlier injury report. And note that this answers something we flagged last week, the transcript of the Friday presser said "Denzel Austin is questionable" and we could not tell which player was meant. It was Denzel Boston, and it was a toe.

"The medical staff's like, I don't know, I don't know" is also a coach telling you his player pushed to be cleared and the doctors were the brake. Which is the correct order.

The bottom line.

Nobody announced anything. There is no decision, no depth-chart news and no plan for the bye in twelve minutes of this press conference. What there is, instead, is a head coach forty-five minutes after a seventeen-point comeback saying nine things coaches are trained not to say.

He refused a compliment, "I don't know. If I knew that, then we would have had them dialed up last week." He admitted the whole truth of his profession, "I've never called a play I thought wouldn't work ever. Not one time in my life," and repeated his wife's joke about just calling the ones that work. He turned a victory lap into a self-diagnosis, "I wish collectively him and I together would play better early and coach better early, that we didn't have to get to this." He reported progress and immediately limited it, "for one week, we solved a little bit of our penalty issue. One week." He credited his players five times for not blinking and then credited his assistants, and never once credited himself. He turned the only question about his quarterback into a sentence about every man on the roster who used to play in Houston. He defended using his best receiver on punt returns in two words, "Hell no", and explained it with a player who got hurt rushing a quarterback in practice. He revealed that he took the safety valve off the biggest throw of the winning drive, "no, you got to let it eat, man", because they had practised that exact throw all week, and that Harold Fannin Junior was a game captain for the first time and "up until that point, he had not" earned it. And asked what he would have predicted at the start of all this, he said "there's no way" and then "I'm done trying to plan out anything," before finishing with the line that explains the rest: "I was proud of them last week. It didn't go our way."

Six and four. Four and oh at home for the first time since nineteen sixty-three. Six and two in one-score games. A sixth fourth-quarter game-winning drive, which is all six wins. And the same warning from the man who just won: "we're going to have to figure something out about starting a hell of a lot faster."

This one had no decision in it and was worth every minute anyway, because the most revealing thing in a press conference is almost never the announcement. It is the eleven words a man says when he is handed credit and gives it back.
