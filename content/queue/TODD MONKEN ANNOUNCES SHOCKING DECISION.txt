Don't ever forget that you're a Mercedes. Todd Monken's Friday press conference, the decision he really announced, and the three defensive tackles he'll play without.

What he announced, and what he revealed.

Let's do the honest thing first, because that is the deal at Ktl Report and because the truth here is better than the headline.

Todd Monken did not drop a bombshell on Friday. He benched nobody, traded nobody, announced no quarterback change. He announced a decision, and he revealed a story, two very different things.

The decision: Cleveland will play the New York Jets without three of its defensive tackles, and the replacement will come off the practice squad. His words: "It's next man up. that's why you have practice squad players and the ability to elevate them, and we'll make a decision who that'll be later today."

The revelation: Monken interviewed to be the head coach of the New York Jets in 2019. They hired Adam Gase instead. And a member of that Jets interview panel told him something he has evidently never forgotten: "Don't ever forget that you're a Mercedes. When you look in the mirror, you can't control if they want a Lexus."

On Sunday he walks into that building as the head coach of a first-place team. That is the story of this presser, and no clickbait was required to make it good.

Here is the transcript, cleaned up, and then our read on each piece of it.

In his own words.

On replacing two starting defensive tackles: "Well, it's next man up. I think that's really what it is. That's why you have practice squad players and the ability to elevate them, and we'll make a decision who that'll be later today."

On whether the plan changes without two of his top three interior players: "I don't see it that way. I mean, we are who we are. We've got to play to our strengths and, obviously, not having Mike and not having Mason, but that's part of it. That's part of the NFL, and no one cares and we don't care. It's those guys got to go play and fight like heck for the Browns."

On the tight end room after another injury: "We're still kind of evaluating what we're going to do there on offense. it doesn't look like we'll have him."

On the Jets' 2019 head coaching search: "Man, we're going to the way-back machine, all the way to '19. First off, I was jacked. Who isn't excited to be interviewed, any position with any NFL organization? To be at the highest level of your work. I really enjoyed it. Didn't work out. That time. Every organization can just pick one. That's all you can do. I've never looked at it any other way, and I never looked at myself any differently."

On the advice he got from someone inside that process: "Don't ever forget that you're a Mercedes. When you look in the mirror, you can't control if they want a Lexus. That doesn't mean you're not capable. Ultimately they chose a different model, and that was their choice."

On a three and one start: "Who doesn't want to be told, man, it's pretty cool right now to be a Cleveland Brown? It's human nature. But that is our job, to bring us back down to earth. We've won three games and that doesn't get you anywhere. It's a nice start, but our direct focus has to be improvement, as coaches and as players."

On motivation: "I talked to the team a little bit this week about all the different levels of motivation. How would you prep this week if we're playing in the Super Bowl Sunday? How would it be if it was the last game you ever played? What if it was the only time your grandma or grandpa got a chance to see you play? We all have to find those tools that motivate us. I find that they all work. There is not one angle."

On a hurricane possibly reaching New York: "It's kind of weird to say that, right? The hurricane and New York. I don't know, rain, little bit of wind. What are you going to do? Spot the ball, let's go play. All we can control is what we do the next 48 hours. We've worked on wet ball."

Our read, number one. The decision he actually announced.

"Next man up" is a cliché until you look at who is not available, and then it becomes a genuine coaching problem.

Mason Graham, a sprained MCL and a sprained ankle from the Pittsburgh game, is ruled out, and the team says he is being evaluated week to week. Mike Hall Junior, who hurt his ankle in the first half against the Steelers, came back in the second half, and then did not practice a single day this week, is ruled out. And Kalia Davis, the veteran interior lineman who had only just been designated to return from injured reserve, was limited on Wednesday and then did not practice Thursday or Friday. He is out too.

That is three defensive tackles. The interior of this defense is being rebuilt on a Friday afternoon with practice-squad elevations.

And his phrasing on the elevation matters: a decision "later today." That is the practice-squad rule at work, the mechanic we have walked through with Jimmy Horn Junior and James Proche the Second, three standard elevations per player per season, after which he must be signed to the 53 to play at all. Cleveland spends one of those on Sunday.

Our read, number two. The casualty list is longer than the defensive line.

The full ruled-out list is heavier than the conversation has acknowledged.

Mason Graham (knee, ankle). Mike Hall Junior (ankle). Kalia Davis (quad). Carsen Ryan (calf), the rookie seventh-round tight end. Tylan Wallace (knee), a second straight game. Teven Jenkins (back), his fifth straight. And Dillon Gabriel (back).

Seven players out, on the road, in the rain, as a first-place team. The one piece of relief: Elgton Jenkins cleared concussion protocol and practiced fully all week, putting a veteran back in the middle of a line that has been without Teven Jenkins for over a month.

But look at the pattern. Cleveland's problems are concentrated in the two places where you cannot improvise: the interior of the defensive line and the interior of the offensive line. Those are the jobs where scheme cannot rescue you, either a man wins his one-on-one or he does not.

Our read, number three. No one cares and we don't care.

This was the most revealing football answer of the session, and it has two layers.

Asked whether the plan has to change with two of his top three interior players gone, Monken said flatly: "I don't see it that way. We are who we are."

The first layer is mindset, and "no one cares and we don't care" is what a locker room needs to hear on a Friday. Nobody is adjusting the schedule for Cleveland's MCLs.

The second layer is philosophy, and it is debatable in a useful way. Refusing to change identity because of injuries is a strength when the identity is sound and stubbornness when the thing that made it work is sitting at home. This defense is 18th in points allowed, giving up four and a half yards per carry (23rd), 13 runs of ten-plus yards (tied 30th) and 28 missed tackles (tied second-most in football), and what held it together was interior pressure, with Graham leading the team in sacks and tackles for loss.

So "we are who we are" is the right message and an open question at once.

Our read, number four. Gabriel practiced fully, and is still listed out.

This one deserves its own section because it is the quiet answer to a week of noise.

Dillon Gabriel was a full participant in practice all week, and he is still ruled out for Sunday.

Put that next to what Monken said on Wednesday about the twenty-one day window that opened October 6: "Not really. we opened up the three-week window to kind of see where we're at. I don't have to feel anyway at this point."

So the back is fine. The participation is full. And the designation is out, because being healthy enough to practice is not the same as being on the 53-man roster, activating him would cost a roster spot Cleveland would rather spend on a defensive tackle it urgently needs this week. That is the clock we flagged, working exactly as described: he can practice for 21 days without occupying a spot, and the decision comes at the end of it, not now.

Anyone selling you drama about Gabriel taking snaps in New York was guessing. The injury report answered it.

Our read, number five. The tight end sentence that hints at a move.

Small answer, real information. Asked about the tight end room, a reporter noted Cleveland would be down to two, Monken said: "We're still kind of evaluating what we're going to do there on offense."

With Carsen Ryan out with a calf injury, the room is Harold Fannin Junior and Joe Royer, both of whom Cleveland drafted. "Still evaluating what we're going to do" is coach-speak for a transaction being considered: an elevation, a signing, or a game plan that uses a sixth lineman or an extra receiver in the heavy packages.

It also underlines something from earlier in the week: Berry signed James Proche the Second and running back Jaret Patterson to the practice squad on October 6 and 7, before either need became an emergency. In a healthy organization the practice squad is not a parking lot, it is the bench you actually use, and Cleveland is about to use it in two rooms on the same Sunday.

Our read, number six. The Mercedes story is the real headline.

Now the part that will be quoted nationally, and it earns it.

In 2019, Monken was the Tampa Bay Buccaneers' offensive coordinator, coming off his third straight season in that job, and he interviewed for the New York Jets head coaching position. The Jets hired Adam Gase, who went nine and twenty-three over two seasons before being fired. Monken went to Cleveland as offensive coordinator that same year.

And someone inside that Jets interview process told him: "Don't ever forget that you're a Mercedes. When you look in the mirror, you can't control if they want a Lexus." Monken added the part that matters: "That doesn't mean you're not capable. ultimately they chose a different model, and that was their choice."

Think about how unusual it is for a head coach to tell that story publicly, two days before playing that team. There is no bitterness in it. He volunteered that he could have interviewed better, "there's things I certainly could have improved", and thanked them for the interview, even stopping mid-story to check himself: "I'm very humble, so please don't take this the wrong way."

That is a man with no score to settle and an intact sense of his own worth. Seven years, one rejection, one nine and twenty-three hire, and now he returns at three and one with the division lead. He does not need to say a word about it, which is exactly why he can afford to tell the story.

Our read, number seven. We've won three games and that doesn't get you anywhere.

Here is the cultural spine of the whole presser, and it is the opposite of what a three and one team usually sounds like.

Monken acknowledged the obvious: after a Thursday win and a few days off, "who doesn't want to be told, man, it's pretty cool right now to be a Cleveland Brown?" And then he put it straight in the bin: "We've won three games and that doesn't get you anywhere."

His framework is three words, mind, body and belief. Study the plan, take care of your body before the game, keep the belief in each other high, and you can play fast. "As long as we keep the main thing the main thing."

Worth saying: the head coach is the most sober voice in this entire conversation. Cleveland is three and one with 81 points in four games and three wins decided in the final minutes. A coach who tells his players that record means nothing has looked at the same point differential we have.

Our read, number eight. The motivation answer, and one honest caveat.

The longest answer of the day was about motivation, and it was more thoughtful than press conferences usually allow.

He laid out the two poles, the person who works for money and promotion, and the person who works out of fear of losing the job, then refused to choose: "I find that they all work. There is not one angle." Then the prompts he gave his players: how would you prepare if this were the Super Bowl? If it were the last game you ever played? If it were the only time your grandmother or grandfather got to see you play?

That last one lands in a locker room, and his closing observation is the sharpest line in the transcript: "that motivation isn't easy. It's easy to settle." One honest caveat: fear-based motivation works in bursts and corrodes over a season. Monken did not endorse it so much as note that it exists in every workplace, but a 17-game marathon runs on the other kind.

Our read, number nine. Quincy Williams is not allowed a revenge game.

A reporter tried twice to get Monken to call this a special game for Quincy Williams, and twice he declined.

Williams signed with Cleveland in March 2026, after Devin Bush left for Chicago, a former Jets All-Pro linebacker who spent years in that building, now returning to it. The revenge angle is right there. Monken's response: "I know it's a special game for him, but these are all special. Every game playing in the NFL is special. you'd have to ask him in terms of where it ranks. For me, it's just another one."

On the football, though, he was willing to be specific, and positive: "I think he's played better. I think he's gotten comfortable with what we're asking him to do. You can see his speed showing up on the field."

Note the structure, because it is the same one he used on the Mercedes story and the three and one start. Acknowledge the human thing, then refuse to let it become the plan. That consistency is what players actually trust.

Our read, number ten. He's a bull out in space. The Judkins point.

The most useful scheme answer of the day concerned Quinshon Judkins as a receiver, and it is worth translating.

Monken: "He's a bull out in space. Him and Harold are similar, their ability once you get them out in space, when you're fighting for yards, those guys are hard to bring down. They've got great contact balance. And when you talk about where teams are going to more and more of these zone looks, their ability to puncture and take a check-down and instead of getting three or four, getting seven, eight, nine, ten."

Here is what it means. Defenses are playing more zone, which takes away deep throws and concedes short ones. Against zone, a check-down to a back or tight end is supposed to be a four-yard play that keeps you behind schedule. If Judkins and Harold Fannin Junior turn those concessions into eight and nine, the whole bargain collapses, you cannot play soft zone against an offense that converts your giveaways into first downs.

That is not a throwaway compliment. It is the explanation for how a team completing 65 percent of its passes and scoring 20 a game keeps winning fourth quarters. On the road in wind and rain, that check-down game may be the entire offensive plan.

Our read, number eleven. A hurricane, in New York.

Monken on a potential hurricane reaching New York: "It's kind of weird to say that, right? The hurricane and New York. I don't know, rain, little bit of wind. What are you going to do? Spot the ball, let's go play." And the preparation: "We've worked on wet ball. You try to get out there when it's raining to get used to it."

Practical significance: wet games compress playbooks, raise fumble risk, and reward exactly the offense described above. They also punish a defense that misses tackles, and Cleveland is tied for the second-most in the league. If the weather is real, this is decided by ball security and tackling, an uncomfortable combination for a patched-together front.

Where we stop.

"Monken announced a shocking decision." He announced a practice-squad elevation to cover two injured defensive tackles and told a story about a 2019 job interview. Both newsworthy. Neither shocking.

The young player he praised. The question garbled the name badly enough that we cannot be sure who he meant, an undersized, explosive player who never misses a day. Better unnamed than attached to the wrong man.

"Monken wants revenge on the Jets." He said the opposite, twice.

Any timeline for Graham or Hall. Graham is week-to-week; nobody has said more, including the team.

"Gabriel is about to play." Full practice participant, still ruled out. The window runs to late October.

Any trade prediction before November 10. Cabot: no plans, eyeing all four quarterbacks. Fowler: no movement. Grossi: "I don't expect him to be traded."

What to watch on Sunday.

Cleveland is at the New York Jets, Sunday, October 11, one o'clock Eastern on CBS, the Browns' first game as a first-place team, with a win putting them at four and one before Baltimore visits on October 18.

The interior of the defensive line: who gets the elevation, and whether he can hold the point of attack. The Jets are one and three with a quiet offense, and Breece Hall (quad) sat out practice too, so New York may lean on short passing as well.

The check-downs: if Judkins and Fannin are getting seven and eight on balls thrown four yards downfield, Cleveland controls the game whatever the weather does.

The tackling: 28 missed tackles in three games, in the rain, with a replacement-level front. That is the losing scenario, and the only one that should worry you.

And watch Monken on the sideline of the building that picked a different model. He will not mention it. He does not have to.

The bottom line.

The decision Monken announced on Friday was "next man up", Mason Graham, Mike Hall Junior and Kalia Davis all out, the fix coming off the practice squad. Seven Browns are ruled out in total, including a tight end, a returner and a starting guard missing his fifth straight game. Gabriel practiced fully and is still out, which is the twenty-one day clock working as written.

The thing he revealed was better. In 2019 the Jets interviewed him, chose Adam Gase, and someone in that room told him not to forget he was a Mercedes even if they wanted a Lexus. Gase went nine and twenty-three. Monken took the Cleveland offensive coordinator job instead, and on Sunday he returns to New York as head coach of a three and one team leading the AFC North, having spent the week telling his players three wins "doesn't get you anywhere."

That is a coach who knows what he is worth and refuses to spend any of it on a grudge. Cleveland has had louder head coaches. It has rarely had a steadier one.
