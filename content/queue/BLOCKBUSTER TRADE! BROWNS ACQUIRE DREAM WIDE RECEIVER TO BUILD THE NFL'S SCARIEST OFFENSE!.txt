One Receiver In, One Receiver Out — And They Passed Each Other Going Opposite Directions

Days before a season opener, nobody expects a transaction wire to tell you anything.

But look closely at what Cleveland just did, because two moves happened at almost the same moment, they involve the same two franchises, and together they say more about what this organization believes than any press conference has all summer.

Bub Means is in. Signed to Cleveland's practice squad, coming from New Orleans.

Cedric Tillman is out. Released by Cleveland, and now on New Orleans' practice squad.

Two receivers, two organizations, crossing paths in opposite directions in the same week. One franchise decided a player wasn't worth a roster spot; the other decided he was worth a look. Both times.

Ktl Report is going to break down both players, what the new receiver room actually looks like, the injury situation nobody's talking loudly enough about, and the compounding risk sitting underneath all of it.

WHO BUB MEANS ACTUALLY IS 📋

Start with the arrival, because he's more interesting than a practice squad signing usually is.

Bub Means is 25 years old, 6-foot-2, 215 pounds — real NFL size at the position. He was a fifth-round pick by the New Orleans Saints in 2024, which means a franchise spent actual draft capital on him not long ago. His NFL production is thin: seven games, nine receptions, 118 yards, one touchdown.

Those numbers look unimpressive until you find out why they're small.

He's barely been available. An injury in November of 2024 ended that season. Then New Orleans placed him on injured reserve again in August of 2025, and he missed that entire season as well.

That distinction matters enormously, and it's the whole reason this signing is interesting. This isn't a veteran who had several healthy seasons and simply couldn't hold a roster spot — that player has been evaluated and the league reached a verdict. This is a young receiver whose career has been interrupted before it started. The evaluation was never completed.

Now go back to what he did in college, because that's the actual scouting report.

At Pitt in 2023, Means caught 41 passes for 721 yards and six touchdowns. He led his team in both receiving yards and touchdown catches, and earned honorable mention conference recognition.

But the number that should make Browns fans sit up isn't any of those.

17.6 yards per reception.

WHY 17.6 IS THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS 🚀

Sit with that figure for a second, because it connects directly to the single biggest problem on this football team.

We spent an entire piece at Ktl Report on Cleveland's explosive play rate — 8.2 percent, 31st in the NFL, bottom two in professional football. An offense that can't produce the one snap that changes a game, that needs twelve perfect plays to score, that leaves its own defense on the field too long.

Yards per reception is one of the cleanest indicators of what kind of receiver you're getting. A player averaging six or seven yards a catch is winning underneath — valuable, but not field-flipping. A player averaging 17.6 is catching the ball down the field. He's the guy running past a defender on a post, winning a contested ball on a deep comeback, and turning a first down into a scoring opportunity in one motion.

Pair that with 6-foot-2 and 215 pounds and you have a specific archetype: a big-bodied receiver who threatens vertically and can win a contested throw.

That is precisely the profile a team ranked 31st in explosive plays should be collecting lottery tickets on. Not because a practice squad signing fixes anything — it doesn't, and we're not going to pretend otherwise — but because it tells you what the front office is shopping for. You can read an organization's priorities more honestly in the players it takes flyers on than in anything it says at a podium.

Cleveland is shopping for size and vertical ability. That's not an accident.

Now the fair caveat, because we don't oversell flyers here: college yards per reception doesn't transfer cleanly to the NFL. Separation that works against ACC corners frequently doesn't work against professional ones, and a receiver who missed nearly two full seasons has speed and body control that nobody outside Berea has recently verified. Means might never take a meaningful snap in this uniform. That's the likeliest outcome for any practice squad signing, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

But the cost is essentially zero, and the profile fits the hole. That's what a smart depth acquisition looks like.

THE TILLMAN GOODBYE 👋

Now the departure, and this one deserves genuine respect rather than a shrug.

Cedric Tillman was a third-round pick in 2023. He started 19 games across three seasons in Cleveland and finished his time here with 833 receiving yards and five touchdowns in 38 games. Last season he appeared in 13 games, started 10, and caught 21 passes for 270 yards and two scores.

Nobody's calling that a great career. But nobody watching honestly would call it a failure either — and the reason matters.

Tillman had the size and the physical tools to win contested catches. There were genuine stretches where he looked like he could become a reliable possession receiver, the kind of player who converts third-and-seven and never makes a highlight show. What got in the way was a combination of things largely outside his control: injuries, inconsistent quarterback play, and limited opportunity.

That middle item deserves emphasis, because it's the honest context. A possession receiver is entirely dependent on a quarterback willing and able to throw into tight windows. In an offense that finished 31st in explosive plays and repeatedly failed to attack past ten air yards, a big-bodied contested-catch receiver is a weapon nobody picks up.

Then Cleveland rebuilt the room around him, invested premium draft capital in two rookies, and his path essentially closed.

New Orleans moved fast — signing him on September 1st after losing a young receiver to a hamstring injury expected to cost him the first couple of months. So there's a genuine path for Tillman to get called up if the Saints need bodies.

And let's say the honest thing: if he produces there, Browns fans are going to feel it. Nobody enjoys watching a former player find his footing somewhere else. But he also deserves a fresh start in a place where the depth chart isn't stacked against him before camp begins. Both of those things can be true.

THE ROOM THAT REPLACED HIM 🧩

So what does this receiver group actually look like now? Here's the projected picture.

Jerry Jeudy is the established starter — the veteran, the known quantity, the player this offense has to lean on.

Alongside him: rookies KC Concepcion and Denzel Boston.

Behind Jeudy: Tylan Wallace. Behind Boston: Malachi Corley. Behind Concepcion: Isaiah Bond.

That is a fundamentally different room than anything Cleveland has fielded in years, and the reason is straightforward. The Browns spent two of their first three picks in the 2026 draft on wide receivers — Concepcion and Boston.

You don't do that by accident. That's a franchise looking at a position group that finished last in the entire league and deciding the fix wasn't a free agent signing or a scheme tweak. The fix was demolition and replacement.

And Todd Monken has been publicly encouraged by the group, saying that from the moment they arrived, all they've done is work and continue to get better.

Coach-speak? Partly. But it's also consistent with everything we've watched all camp: this group has been visible, active, and involved. That's the honest read.

There's one more name worth remembering in this shuffle: Bryce Oliver, the receiver released to make room for Means. He'd only been in Cleveland since September 2nd — a stay measured in days. An undrafted free agent out of Youngstown State in 2024, he appeared in 13 games with Tennessee before landing here briefly.

That's the part of this business nobody makes documentaries about. A player arrives, learns a playbook, meets a locker room, and is gone before he's memorized the drive to the facility. Every roster move Browns fans celebrate or complain about has somebody's actual career on the other end of it.

THE INJURY NOBODY'S TALKING ABOUT LOUDLY ENOUGH ⚠️

Now the part of this story that deserves more attention than the transaction wire.

Isaiah Bond has been dealing with a concussion. He took a hit to the head in Cleveland's preseason game against Buffalo on August 22nd. The initial word was that he cleared the in-game evaluation — but symptoms developed afterward, and he entered the concussion protocol.

Two things need to be said here, and Ktl Report is going to say both.

First, the football consequence. Bond sits behind Concepcion on the depth chart, and he's the player we identified as the room's genuine vertical threat — the take-the-top-off speed that forces safeties backward and gives a coordinator a reason to call shot plays. In an offense desperate for explosives, he's not a depth piece. He's a functional need.

Second, and more important: nobody should be in a hurry here. Head injuries are the one category where "he's a competitor, he'll push through" is exactly the wrong instinct. The fact that he cleared an in-game check and then developed symptoms afterward is a reminder of how these injuries actually behave — they don't always announce themselves on the sideline. The protocol exists for reasons the sport learned the hard way, and the right outcome is Bond being healthy in November, not available in Week 1.

This also explains the transaction activity. When your vertical threat is in protocol and two of your top three receivers are rookies, you keep the bottom of the room stocked with options. That's not panic. That's arithmetic.

THE COMPOUNDING RISK 🎲

Here's where all of it converges, and it's the part most coverage skips.

Cleveland is asking a young receiver room — two rookies in the top three, a vertical threat in concussion protocol, and depth built from practice squad claims — to produce immediately.

At the same time, this offense is being run by a quarterback whose most documented weakness all summer has been attacking past ten air yards.

Now think about how those two facts interact, because they don't simply add. They multiply.

A veteran receiver room can partially compensate for a conservative quarterback — savvy route runners find soft spots, uncover early, and make the quick game work. Rookies can't do that yet; it takes a season or two of reps to develop that feel.

And a quarterback who pushes the ball downfield can partially compensate for a young room — raw speed and size win vertically before technique fully develops.

Cleveland currently has neither compensation available. Young receivers who need time, plus a quarterback profile that doesn't stress defenses vertically, is how an offense stays at 8.2 percent.

That's the risk. Not that the plan is wrong — collecting young talent at a position that finished last in football is completely defensible — but that the plan and the quarterback situation are pulling against each other in the exact same season.

And the upside case is real too, so let's state it fairly: if Jeudy stays healthy, if Boston and Concepcion contribute early, if Wallace and Corley hold up as depth, if Bond returns fully healthy and develops — this group could genuinely surprise people. That's a lot of ifs. But every one of them is plausible, and rookie receivers historically make their biggest jump between September and December.

THE BOTTOM LINE 🏈

So what do two small moves actually tell us?

They tell us Cleveland is still building. That the 53 set last week isn't the 53 anyone finishes with. That the front office is shopping specifically for size and vertical ability — exactly what an offense ranked 31st in explosive plays should be hunting.

They also tell us this franchise made a choice: the new young receiver room was worth more than keeping an experienced body around. That's a bet on development over familiarity, and it's the same bet showing up everywhere on this roster — the second-youngest in the NFL.

Cedric Tillman gets a fresh start in New Orleans. Bub Means gets a second chance in Cleveland. Both were told by one organization they weren't needed and by another that they were worth a look. That's the business, and it's happening to somebody in all 32 buildings this week.

And on Sunday, September 13th in Jacksonville, all of it stops being theoretical. Coaches can praise development. Players can look sharp in practice. Front offices can reshape a depth chart right up until kickoff.

Then the games start counting, and this offense has to actually work.
