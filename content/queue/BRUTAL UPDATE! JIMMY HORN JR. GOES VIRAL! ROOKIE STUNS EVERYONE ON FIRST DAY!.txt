Cut on Monday, Defended by Shedeur, Signed in Cleveland: The Colorado Story Behind the Jimmy Horn Move.
 
A sixth-round receiver muffs one punt in a blowout loss. Two days later, he's out of a job.
 
And the first person to publicly show up for him wasn't a teammate in his own locker room. It was his old college quarterback, hundreds of miles away, sitting on a bench in Cleveland, in the middle of the most scrutinized backup quarterback situation in football.
 
Days later, that receiver was signing with the Browns.
 
We covered the Jimmy Horn Junior signing last week at Ktl Report and asked the pointed front-office question: why sign a receiver for his chemistry with the backup quarterback? Today is the other half of that story, how the release actually happened, what Shedeur Sanders did about it, and the Colorado culture that ties all of it together.
 
What actually happened in Carolina.
 
Get the sequence right first, because it matters.
 
The Panthers opened their season getting blown out fifty-nine to thirty-seven by the Chicago Bears, a game in which Carolina's defense surrendered a franchise-worst point total.
 
Jimmy Horn Junior, a sixth-round pick out of Colorado the year before and Carolina's primary punt returner, muffed a second-quarter punt that Chicago recovered and turned into points.
 
Two days later, the Panthers waived him, using the roster spot to promote linebacker Tyrell Dodson from the practice squad.
 
His Carolina tenure ended with 11 receptions for 108 yards plus 68 rushing yards on eight carries, a small sample, used mostly in a specialist and depth role. Not disastrous. Not much of anything, really, because he was never given the volume to become anything.
 
Now the fair football read, because Ktl Report doesn't turn every transaction into an injustice.
 
The case for Carolina: a special teams turnover in a game that spiraled that badly is a legitimate factor. Coaching staffs weigh ball security on returns heavily, it's the one role where a single mistake directly hands points away. And after surrendering 59, adding immediate defensive depth was an obvious priority.
 
The case against: several outlets covering the move called it a somewhat surprising corresponding cut. Carolina had other options, healthy inactive players, a deep tight end room, that could have opened the spot for Dodson instead. When a defense gives up a franchise record and the roster casualty is a punt returner, it's fair to wonder whether one visible mistake absorbed the consequences of a much larger collapse.
 
Both things can be true. Horn made a real mistake, and he also paid a disproportionate price for a game that wasn't his fault.
 
And there's a structural reality here that fans rarely see. Returners are the most exposed players on any roster. A cornerback who blows a coverage can be hidden in a film session. A lineman who misses a block disappears into a pile. But a muffed punt happens alone, in the middle of the field, on national television, with the ball bouncing loose for everyone to see. It's the one mistake in football with no shared ownership, which is exactly why it gets punished out of proportion to its actual cost.
 
The first person to show up.
 
Now the part that deserves attention on its own terms.
 
By most accounts, Shedeur Sanders doesn't post much on social media outside of business-related content. He's not a constant presence. He doesn't react to everything.
 
Which is exactly what made it notable when, shortly after the release, he publicly reposted and tagged Horn. And Horn reposted it right back.
 
Small gesture? Sure. But context makes it meaningful.
 
These two have a real history. Horn's college career began at South Florida, where he earned first-team All-AAC honors in 2022, before transferring to Colorado. There, with Sanders throwing to him, he produced 1,008 receiving yards and seven touchdowns across two seasons, 58 catches, 567 yards and six scores in 2023, then 37 catches for 441 yards and a touchdown in 2024.
 
That isn't an incidental connection. That's a productive partnership that helped both careers.
 
And there's a quote that makes the gesture land harder. Sanders was previously asked who his best friend is on another NFL team, and he answered without hesitation: one of his Colorado guys. He said he stays in touch with all of them, wants them to succeed, and hopes they can play together again one day.
 
Put that quote next to what happened this week, and it stops reading like a social media reflex. It's someone doing exactly what he said he'd do, the first time it actually cost him something to say it, a young quarterback with his own situation to worry about, choosing to spend public attention on somebody else's bad week.
 
The hug in Jacksonville.
 
And it wasn't an isolated moment.
 
After Cleveland's thirty-four to ten loss in Jacksonville, Sanders made his way across the field, through reporters and camera crews, specifically to find Travis Hunter. The two shared an extended embrace and conversation before heading to their separate locker rooms.
 
Think about the timing. His team had just been blown out. He hadn't played. The storyline all week had been about him not playing. The easy move was to head straight for the tunnel.
 
Instead, he went looking for an old friend.
 
And the history between those two makes the moment heavier. Their first season together at Colorado was brutal: Hunter missed three games after a lacerated liver suffered against Colorado State, Sanders played behind a line that surrendered 52 sacks, and the team finished four and eight.
 
The next year, the same duo led Colorado to nine and four and an Alamo Bowl appearance. Sanders finished his Colorado career with 7,364 passing yards, 64 touchdowns and just 13 interceptions in 24 games, and was named Big 12 Offensive Player of the Year in 2024. Hunter became only the second Heisman Trophy winner in program history, playing full-time on both sides of the ball.
 
Hardship first, then triumph. That's how friendships like this get built.
 
And it runs both directions. On draft night, during the slide that sent Sanders out of the first round and all the way to pick 144, Hunter was there for him, a gesture widely described at the time as the ultimate act of friendship on what should have been one of the worst nights of Sanders' life.
 
A correction on Hunter's role.
 
Quick fix, because some of the coverage garbled this.
 
Per NFL Network's Ian Rapoport, Travis Hunter is now being used almost exclusively as a cornerback in Jacksonville, not as the true two-way player he was at Colorado. In the Week 1 win over Cleveland, he played 29 defensive snaps and just four on offense.
 
That detail adds something real to the reunion. As one outlet put it plainly: neither Hunter nor Sanders is where they want to be in the NFL yet. Both are former college superstars navigating a diminished or uncertain role relative to what they were in Boulder.
 
This isn't one player who made it checking in on one who didn't. It's two players in parallel uncertainty, choosing to keep showing up for each other anyway.
 
The pipeline nobody talks about.
 
Zoom out, because Horn's situation sits inside something genuinely remarkable.
 
Colorado had zero players drafted between 2022 and 2024, the stretch right before Deion Sanders arrived.
 
In the 2025 draft alone, four Buffaloes were selected: Hunter, Sanders, Horn, and receiver LaJohntay Wester, who went to Baltimore.
 
And heading into this season, 14 former Colorado players made a 53-man roster or practice squad across the league, nine of whom played their final college season under Sanders.
 
For a program that was irrelevant at the NFL level a few years ago, that's a complete transformation. It's also why every individual roster move involving one of these players draws outsized attention, there's now a closely watched cohort whose NFL fates Colorado fans track collectively.
 
And it isn't only the famous names. Some of that group made it the hard way, as undrafted free agents, no guaranteed spot, no draft-weekend spotlight, just grinding for a job and sticking. That's worth remembering whenever this story gets told as if it's only about a Heisman winner and a famous quarterback.
 
The continuity runs back to Boulder, too: Colorado is off to a two and oh start this season, its best opening since the Sanders-and-Horn era.
 
The theory we're not buying.
 
There's a version of this story circulating that ties Horn's release into a larger narrative, that NFL franchises are somehow targeting former Colorado players, connecting Carolina's decision to how Sanders has been treated in Cleveland.
 
Ktl Report isn't going there, and we'll say why plainly.
 
There's no evidence of anything beyond the football reasoning Carolina gave: a costly special teams mistake in a blowout, plus an urgent need for defensive depth after surrendering a franchise record. Those are two individually explainable decisions by two unrelated front offices.
 
It's understandable to feel the frustration. This group of players has faced an unusual amount of collective scrutiny since turning that program around. But noticing a coincidence isn't the same as proving a pattern, and presenting an unproven coordinated dynamic across multiple organizations as fact would be exactly the kind of shortcut we've refused all season, whether the target was a head coach, an agent, or an opposing quarterback's injury.
 
The real story here is good enough. It doesn't need a villain.
 
Now he's in Cleveland.
 
And here's where the story completes itself.
 
Days after that repost, Cleveland moved to sign Horn, the reunion Sanders publicly said he hoped for, now sitting in the same building.
 
We raised the front-office question last week, and it still stands as clearly labeled inference: a receiver's chemistry with the backup quarterback is only valuable if you think the backup might play. Nobody in Berea has connected this signing to any quarterback plan, and it could simply be a cheap depth move at a position where two rookies sit in the top three.
 
But the human side deserves its own weight, separate from depth charts.
 
A young quarterback in an isolating, hostile situation, booed by nobody but questioned by everybody, holding a tablet while the city argues about him every day, now has someone in his locker room who has been through the hard version of this with him before. Horn was there for the four and eight season. He was there for the 52 sacks. He knows what Sanders looks like when things are going badly, and what he looks like when they turn around.
 
That doesn't show up on a stat sheet. It shows up in how a person handles a long season.
 
And there's a practical football layer, too. Timing between a quarterback and receiver isn't abstract, it's thousands of repetitions of the same routes until both players know where the ball is going before the break happens. Horn and Sanders built that over two seasons in Boulder. If Sanders ever takes meaningful snaps in Cleveland, in relief, in garbage time, or as a starter, Horn is the one receiver on this roster who already knows his release point, his cadence, and where he likes to put the ball on a back-shoulder throw. For a depth signing, that's an unusually specific kind of value.
 
The bottom line.
 
Here's the Ktl Report read.
 
Horn's release was defensible football and a disproportionate consequence at the same time, one visible mistake absorbing the fallout of a far bigger defensive collapse.
 
Sanders' response was consistent, not performative. It matched exactly what he'd said publicly before anything happened: stay in touch, want them to succeed, hope to play together again. And it followed a postgame moment in Jacksonville where, after a blowout and a week of scrutiny, he went looking for Travis Hunter instead of the tunnel.
 
The Colorado pipeline is a genuinely underrated story, zero draft picks in three years, then four in one draft, then 14 players on NFL rosters, and it's outlasting any single player's roster status.
 
And now two of those players share a locker room in Cleveland. Nobody knows what that means for the depth chart. But it means something for the people in it.
