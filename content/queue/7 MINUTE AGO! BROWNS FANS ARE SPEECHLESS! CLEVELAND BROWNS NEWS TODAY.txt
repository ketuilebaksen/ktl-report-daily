With whom? Every argument to cut a player has to name the replacement, so let us name him, and then let us talk about the running back New Orleans just claimed.

First, the title, and an unusual amount of credit.

The title is "Seven minutes ago! Browns Fans Are Speechless! Cleveland Browns News Today."

There is no claim in there to fact-check, which is its own kind of problem: "speechless" promises nothing and "seven minutes ago" promises freshness it cannot deliver. A viewer who clicks that learns nothing from the headline about what they are about to watch.

But the script underneath it is one of the most careful we have audited in three weeks, and we are going to say so plainly here at Ktl Report before we say anything else. Three separate times it stops to tell you what it does not know:

"These are possibilities discussed in the analysis, not confirmed decisions by the Browns. Cleveland has not announced that Howard is being released or replaced."
"The precise internal reasoning behind the decision wasn't established in the report, so we shouldn't assume we know exactly why Cleveland released him."
"But again, this remains speculation. No reunion has been confirmed."

That last one is the interesting part, because it has now been confirmed, and the video was right. We will get to that.

First, the bigger story, and the question nobody asks about it.

The eight sacks, distributed, and why they do not land on one man.

The video's first story is that Tytus Howard is taking heavy criticism for Sunday, and that a Sports Illustrated analysis floated bringing in a free agent, exploring a trade, or moving on from him.

Before anybody argues about that, distribute the eight sacks. Here is who actually got them:

T.J. Watt, two. Patrick Queen, one and a half, with twelve tackles and a forced fumble. Cameron Heyward, one and a half. Nick Herbig, one. Kevin Jobity Junior, one, the first of his career. Keeanu Benton, half. Alex Highsmith, half.

Seven different players. And look at the positions: Queen is a linebacker. Heyward and Benton work inside. That is three and a half sacks, nearly half the total, produced by players who do not line up across from the right tackle.

Watt and Highsmith are the edges, and together they account for two and a half of eight.

So the arithmetic does not support "blame the right tackle." It supports what Monken said, in this order: "certainly got to call it better. Certainly got to scheme it better. Got to protect better. Got to work to get our guys open." Call it, scheme it, protect it, and the video, to its credit, reaches the same conclusion on its own: "eight sacks cannot automatically be blamed on one player."

And the fact the criticism keeps leaving out.

Tytus Howard did not practice on Wednesday, Thursday or Friday. He was in the concussion protocol after leaving the Tennessee game, was the only Brown listed as a full non-participant all week, and cleared the protocol on Saturday. Then he started on Sunday against T.J. Watt.

Whatever you conclude about his season, a man who cleared a head-injury protocol the day before kickoff and went out to face the player with the most career sacks against the Browns in NFL history is not the easy villain of that afternoon.

The honest version of the criticism is the season-long one, not the Sunday one. Through the first three games he had allowed ten pressures and committed five penalties, three of them in one game against Carolina. On Sunday he was flagged for an ineligible man downfield on the series Shedeur Sanders played. The pattern is real; the Pittsburgh game is the weakest possible exhibit for it.

Now the question nobody asks. With whom?

Here is the test we want to introduce, because it is the one that separates a real roster argument from a vent.

Every "cut him" argument has to name the replacement. Not a position. A person. If you cannot finish the sentence "and then ____ plays right tackle on Sunday in New Orleans," you have not made an argument, you have made a complaint.

So let us finish it. There are exactly three candidates.

Candidate one. Dawand Jones.

This is the actual answer, and it is not a guess, Monken confirmed it on the record back in September. Asked about the plan behind Howard, he said of Jones: "He's been at both [spots]. And lately he's been at right tackle."

So the depth chart already has an answer. The question is whether it is a better one.

Jones is a fourth-round pick from the twenty twenty-three class, in the final year of his rookie contract. He has never played more than eleven games in a season for Cleveland, and in twenty twenty-five he played only three before a season-ending injury. He is also the man who replaced Howard in the third quarter against Tennessee, and one game-grading service credited him with allowing at least two pressures in that stretch, the lowest offensive-line grade of the afternoon.

Verdict: available, experienced at the spot, and unproven over a full season. Playing him is a change, not an upgrade, and it is a change Cleveland can make for free at any time. Which is precisely why nobody needs to cut anybody to find out.

Candidate two. The mid-season free agent pool.

The video raises this one and then answers it honestly: "finding a reliable offensive lineman during the season isn't easy."

That is an understatement. In November, the available offensive tackles are the ones thirty-two teams have already declined to employ. A street free agent needs a week to learn the protection calls, and protection is the one job where a communication error is worse than a physical loss, a missed call lets a free runner through untouched.

Verdict: depth, not a solution. Worth a signing for the sake of bodies with an open roster spot sitting there. Not worth a headline.

Candidate three. A trade, in six days.

The deadline closes at four o'clock on Tuesday, November tenth. Cleveland has the currency, two twenty twenty-seven first-round picks, plus the Rams' twenty twenty-eight second and twenty twenty-nine third, and it has an open active-roster spot after releasing a running back this week with no corresponding move.

And the market price is cheap for a veteran rental: DeForest Buckner, a three-time Pro Bowler, went to San Francisco for one twenty twenty-seven fourth-round pick, while Jeffery Simmons, twenty-nine years old and under contract through twenty thirty, cost Chicago two firsts and a fourth. The premium is for contract control, not reputation. A nine-week lineman sits in the Buckner aisle.

Verdict: the only candidate that could actually be an upgrade, and the only one with a clock on it. Which is why we have said buy a lineman since Thursday, and said it louder after eight sacks.

So the verdict on Howard.

Put the three candidates together and the answer falls out.

Cleveland traded for Tytus Howard from Houston and gave him a sixty-three-million-dollar contract. You do not release that player in November to hand the job to a backup you could play anyway, with the deadline six days out and no replacement signed.

What you do is the thing the video lands on and the thing we agree with: add, do not subtract. Bring in competition at the position, keep Jones ready, and let the snaps sort it out. If Howard plays like Sunday for three more weeks, the decision makes itself in the off-season, when there is a market.

Cutting him this week would not fix a single one of the eight sacks. Three and a half of them came from a linebacker and two interior linemen.

The test, applied wider, and why it disqualifies most of November's anger.

The reason we like this test at Ktl Report is that it survives being pointed in every direction, including at us.

Apply it to the whole offensive line and the same thing happens. Thirty-three sacks in eight games is a unit number, not a man number, and the pressure that produced Sunday's eight came mostly from the inside. So "fix the line" has to name five people and then name their replacements, and the honest answer for four of those five spots is that there is nobody in the building who is clearly better and nobody on the street who knows the calls.

Apply it to the coordinator and it bites just as hard. Monken put scheme ahead of execution in his own ordering of blame, "got to call it better. Certainly got to scheme it better", but "replace the coordinator in Week Nine" has to finish the sentence too, and the person who would inherit the playbook is one of the assistants who helped write it. Nine weeks into a season, continuity is not loyalty, it is arithmetic.

Apply it to the quarterback and you get the most uncomfortable version. Deshaun Watson has taken thirty-three of those sacks. The men behind him are a rookie fifth-rounder with four career snaps and a third-rounder who has not played. We spent two articles last week arguing over who should finish games; the test says the argument is about snaps and development, not about an upgrade, because there is no upgrade available on a Tuesday in November.

What the test does not disqualify is buying. A trade is the only lever in this entire column where the sentence finishes with a name Cleveland does not currently employ. That is the whole reason the deadline matters more than the outrage does.

And what the open roster spot is actually worth.

One more piece of roster grammar, because almost nobody reads it.

When Cleveland waived Audric Estime on Monday, it made no corresponding signing. A fifty-three-man roster with fifty-two men on it is not an accident in the week of a trade deadline; it is a parking space. It costs nothing to hold and it converts instantly into either a claim off somebody else's waiver wire, a free-agent protection body, or the incoming side of a trade.

It is the cheapest form of flexibility in football, and it is the one concrete thing Cleveland did this week that points toward activity rather than away from it. It is not proof of a plan. It is the absence of a reason not to have one.

The other story, and the video called it.

Now the part where this script earns real credit, because it speculated carefully and then the speculation came true.

Cleveland waived running back Audric Estime on Monday, November second. The video noticed the storyline immediately: Estime used to play for New Orleans, and New Orleans is Cleveland's next opponent. It asked whether a reunion could happen, explained the mechanics, and then said plainly: "this remains speculation. No reunion has been confirmed."

It has now been confirmed. The New Orleans Saints claimed Audric Estime off waivers from the Browns, days before Cleveland visits.

So he gets an immediate shot at the team that just released him, and, as one write-up put it, with a fresh view of Cleveland's defense from the practice field he has just left.

His file, properly: drafted by Denver out of Notre Dame in twenty twenty-four, where he ran for three hundred ten yards and two touchdowns in thirteen games as a rookie. Then New Orleans late last season, seven games, forty-six carries, one hundred ninety-eight yards and a touchdown, plus twelve catches for one hundred three yards, a four point three average in Kellen Moore's offense, which is the same offense he is walking back into. Then one game in Cleveland: nine carries for nineteen yards and a touchdown in the thirteen-to-ten win over Baltimore.

And the rule the video got right.

Credit where almost nobody earns it: the video explained an obscure roster mechanic correctly.

It said players released with an injury settlement must wait until the settlement period expires plus an additional three weeks before rejoining the team that released them. That is the rule as it is commonly reported, three weeks after the settlement term ends, specifically to stop clubs using settlements to park players.

The video applied it in the right direction too: the relevant settlement would have been New Orleans', since the Saints released Estime from injured reserve earlier this season, Kellen Moore described that as roster management with other players returning. And in the end the question was moot, because a waiver claim is not a re-signing; Cleveland waived him and New Orleans claimed him off the wire.

Explaining a settlement rule accurately and then having the transaction resolve a different way is not a miss. It is what research looks like.

The Saints' run game, audited, and why it matters on Sunday.

Here is the context that turns this from a cute storyline into a real preview.

New Orleans fired run-game coordinator T.J. Paganetti, the video says "Peaganetti", and handed the responsibility to offensive line coach Brendan Nugent, rendered in the transcript as "Nent." The trigger was specific and brutal: in their most recent game, against Pittsburgh in Paris, the Saints ran for eighteen yards on seventeen carries.

Eighteen. On seventeen attempts. Cleveland managed thirty-four yards against the same defense on Sunday. Both teams got buried by the same front, three weeks apart.

The Saints' run game ranks twenty-ninth, and they have not had a one-hundred-yard rusher all season. Kendre Miller's best day is seventy yards against the Giants, and he described Pittsburgh's preparation bluntly: the Steelers "definitely had us schemed-up every run we had."

The backfield as it stands: Travis Etienne Junior suffered a Grade One left hamstring sprain in Week three and went on injured reserve on October first. He has met the four-game minimum, Moore said he would be looked at on Monday, and a Wednesday return to practice would put him in play for Sunday, expected to be the lead back if cleared, though likely on reduced snaps at first. Alvin Kamara is the top option until then; against Pittsburgh he caught all seven of his targets for fifty-seven yards and led the backfield in all-purpose yards. Kendre Miller and rookie CJ Donaldson fill out the committee. And Miller's name has come up in trade talk, with open questions about whether New Orleans would move Kamara before November tenth.

So Cleveland travels to face a twenty-ninth-ranked run game with a new coordinator, a lead back possibly returning from a hamstring, and a back the Browns themselves cut last week.

And Cleveland's run defense just allowed two hundred yards. That is the matchup, and it is a lot more uncomfortable than "they can't run."
