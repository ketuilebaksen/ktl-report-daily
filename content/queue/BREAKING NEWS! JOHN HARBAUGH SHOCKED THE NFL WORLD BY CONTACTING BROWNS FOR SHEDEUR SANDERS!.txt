If nobody wanted him in April, why would anyone want him in October?" The best question of the week, answered properly.
 
There are now two teams attached to the Shedeur Sanders conversation instead of one.
 
Washington has been in it since Jayden Daniels dislocated his elbow. Now New York joins, after Jaxson Dart went down in Week 2 against the Rams on a hit from linebacker Byron Young, an injury Adam Schefter reported could require season-ending knee surgery, with Ian Rapoport following up that it was worse than first believed.
 
And according to the reporting circulating this week, Giants head coach John Harbaugh, who ran Baltimore before taking the New York job, has circled back on a quarterback he wanted once before.
 
That's the story. But the sharpest thing anybody said about it this week came from a skeptic, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal.
 
Tony Grossi's pushback: Sanders fell to the fifth round because team after team passed on him, multiple times, Dallas included. So if nobody wanted him in April, why would there suddenly be a bidding war for him now?
 
That question is better than most of the arguments on either side. Let's answer it.
 
First, what actually changed in New York.
 
Dart is out. Jameis Winston took over and his first extended start went badly: 11 of 27 for 111 yards, an interception, and a passer rating of 37.7 on Monday night.
 
Harbaugh's response afterward was that the team is open to all options at quarterback, that evaluations are ongoing, and that he wasn't going to share more until decisions are made, decisions that, in his words, take more than one person.
 
Now, fair reading. That is not a signal. That's what every coach in America says in that spot. A head coach who has just watched a 37.7 passer rating is not going to stand at a podium and guarantee the job to the man who produced it, and he's also not going to announce a trade target. "All options" is the most standard sentence in football.
 
What is real is the situation underneath it: a team with playoff ambitions, a franchise quarterback gone for the year, and a bridge who just posted the worst start of the week. That's a front office that will take phone calls. Whether it makes them is a different question.
 
The draft slide paradox.
 
Now the good question. Why would a player 31 teams passed on repeatedly become a target six months later?
 
Because the draft market and the trade market are not the same market, and the difference is bigger than most fans realize.
 
One: in April you're buying a projection. In October you're buying tape. A fifth-round pick is a guess about who a player will become. Sanders has now started NFL games. Teams know how his arm plays against pro speed and how he handles a collapsing pocket. That doesn't guarantee he's good, but uncertainty is what makes a player cheap, and he's less uncertain now than he was.
 
Two: in April every team has alternatives. In October nobody does. A general manager in the draft room has a full board, a whole offseason, and the option to wait a year. A general manager in Week 4 whose starter is on injured reserve has none of that. Scarcity is the entire explanation. The same player is worth more to a team with no options than to a team with thirty.
 
Three: most of the teams that passed didn't need a quarterback. Dallas is the clearest case, Jerry Jones said publicly they evaluated Sanders highly and passed because Dak Prescott was locked in on a massive extension. That's not a negative evaluation. A team with a franchise quarterback doesn't spend a premium pick on another one.
 
Four: fit was part of it, and fit changes. Per the reporting, Baltimore had real interest and it died because Sanders' camp signaled he had no appetite for sitting behind a two-time MVP for years. Whatever you think of that stance, it's a reason unrelated to talent, and it doesn't apply to a team whose starter is out for the season.
 
And now the honest counterweight, because Grossi's point doesn't fully collapse. The slide is still evidence. Thirty-one teams' evaluations were lukewarm at the same time, and that many independent judgments can't be waved away with "they were all wrong." A player's market in October can be higher than his market in April and both markets can be telling you something true. One says: nobody was convinced. The other says: desperation pays a premium.
 
Both things are real. That's the actual answer.
 
What a coach's old interest is worth.
 
The Harbaugh history is the strongest part of the story, and it's still weaker than it sounds.
 
Coaches don't pay for players. Front offices do. A head coach's previously reported interest in a prospect is real information, he's seen the tape, he liked what he saw, and he'd know what to do with him. But draft capital comes out of a general manager's budget, and "our coach liked him a year ago" has never been the deciding argument in a trade room.
 
There's also an irony in that history nobody has pointed out. The reported reason Baltimore's interest died is that Sanders' side didn't want him sitting behind an established starter. He is currently sitting behind an established starter, in a different city, having taken zero snaps this season.
 
We're not using that as a criticism of him, a player is allowed to prefer a path, and his preference didn't cause Cleveland to bench him. But it's worth noticing that the thing his camp was trying to avoid happened anyway, which is a good reminder of how little control any young player has over this.
 
The Jerry Jones endorsement.
 
Then there's Jerry Jones, who defended Sanders publicly while his draft stock collapsed, pointing to his relationship with Deion going back to the 1995 free agent signing that helped Dallas to its last Super Bowl, and vouching for Shedeur's character while analysts were picking it apart to explain the slide.
 
Two honest things about this.
 
It was a genuinely decent thing to do, at a moment when very few people in power were saying anything kind, and it's worth remembering.
 
And it's the cheapest currency in football. "We had him evaluated very highly, but we already have our guy" is what every owner and GM says about every player they passed on. It costs nothing, it can't be checked, and it makes everybody feel better. Praise from a team that didn't draft him is not evidence of value. A draft pick is.
 
So what's the price with two suitors?
 
Here's where the week actually moved something.
 
Two interested teams change the math, if both actually bid. One buyer sets a price by what he feels like offering. Two buyers set a price by what the other guy might offer. That's the difference between a fifth-round conversation and a real one.
 
But notice the conditions. Neither team has been reported to have called. "Sources close to the situation" with no name attached is not a call. Stephen A. Smith campaigning on national television is not a call either, it's an opinion with a large microphone, and it moves fan pressure, not draft capital.
 
If both teams do call, our number from earlier in the week holds and probably rises: the conversation starts in the second round, with escalators tied to starts.
 
And the other half of the equation hasn't moved at all. Cleveland's own need for a backup went up this month, not down. Deshaun Watson has two Achilles surgeries behind him and just asked for more designed runs. Quarterbacks are going down across the league. The insurance is worth more now than it was in August, which is exactly why a team with no pressure to sell shouldn't.
 
The Daniels detail worth correcting.
 
One piece of this week's coverage is more precise than what's been circulating, and it's worth getting right because it changes how you read Washington's side.
 
Jayden Daniels' elbow isn't a vague "injury history." The specifics: he dislocated the same left elbow in Week 9 against Seattle last season, missed three games, then reinjured it in Week 14 against Minnesota, which ended his year. Sunday against Dallas was the same joint again, after a teammate stepped on his foot and he braced his fall.
 
That's three separate incidents involving one joint in under a calendar year. That's a genuine structural concern, not a narrative, and it explains why Washington's thinking would run past this season rather than just to Daniels' return date.
 
It also cuts against the cheap version of the story. "He's injury prone" as an insult is lazy, and one of those three came from a lineman's foot. But a front office planning its 2027 quarterback room has to price in a joint that has failed three times, and that's not cruelty, it's the job.
 
Where we stop.
 
Several things attached to this one.
 
"The front office used the Tampa game as validation to lock Watson in harder." Nobody reported that. A coaching staff saying its starter played well after he played well is not a conspiracy, it's a description.
 
"Cleveland never gave him a fair shot." This keeps getting repeated in a week when the head coach said, on the record, that splitting first-team reps in camp benefited Sanders and cost the eventual starter. You can argue the decision was wrong. The claim that he never got a chance keeps colliding with that quote.
 
And "sources close to the situation" as a substitute for a name. We've said it all week and we'll keep saying it: when this becomes real, a reporter with a track record will attach their name to it.
 
The question Browns fans should actually be asking.
 
Here's where we'd steer the whole conversation, because "will they trade him" is the less useful question.
 
The better one: what is Cleveland's plan to find out what it has?
 
Because both futures, keeping him or trading him, get better with information, and worse without it. A team that knows Shedeur Sanders is a starter keeps him and saves itself the 86.2 million dollar problem in 2027. A team that knows he isn't trades him at peak value to somebody who still believes. A team that knows nothing does both badly.
 
There are real ways to learn something short of benching a starter who just won a road game: specific packages, situational snaps, meaningful reps if a game gets out of hand in either direction. None of that destabilizes anything. All of it produces the one thing four days of rumors haven't: evidence.
 
That's the story worth pressing the Browns on. Not the trade.
 
The bottom line.
 
Here's the Ktl Report read.
 
The skeptic asked the best question of the week, and the answer is that draft value and trade value are different markets. Projection versus tape, thirty alternatives versus none.
 
But the slide still counts as evidence. Thirty-one teams were unconvinced at the same time, and October desperation doesn't retroactively make them wrong.
 
Harbaugh's old interest is real and weak. Coaches like players; general managers pay for them.
 
Jerry Jones' defense was decent and free. Praise from a team that passed is not a market.
 
Two suitors would raise the price, if either one ever picks up the phone. So far, nobody with a name has reported that one has.
 
And Cleveland's real problem is unchanged: the Browns still don't know what they have, and Sunday against Carolina won't tell them, because the man who'd answer it will be holding a play sheet.
