The scoreboard gets a vote. Cleveland is in first place, and one story from last week did not survive Sunday.

First, the result, because nothing else matters as much.

Cleveland Browns 13, Baltimore Ravens 10. Field goal as the clock hit zero.

The Browns are four and two. Baltimore is four and two. They are level at the top of the AFC North, and Cleveland holds head-to-head wins over both Baltimore and Pittsburgh. It is the first time this franchise has had a share of first place this late in a season since 2014.

Say the quiet part out loud. Three weeks ago this team was being written about as a twenty-seventh-ranked offense with a quarterback leading the league in sacks and a backup quarterback drama. Yesterday it beat the division leader, without allowing a point after the first quarter, with its third-string running backs, on a kick by a man who had just missed one.

Ktl Report is going to do something this morning that almost nobody in this corner of the internet does. Before we touch a single rumor, we are going to grade our own homework. On Sunday morning we published four specific numbers to watch. Three of them came in exactly as described. One of them did not, and we are going to say so plainly.

Then we will deal with the other story of the weekend, the agent, the agency, and a claim about "silent investors" that we are not going to carry, for reasons we will document.

How it actually happened.

The sequence matters, because the shape of this game is the story.

First quarter: Baltimore scored on its first two possessions. Tyler Huntley led an 11-play, 66-yard opening drive that finished with a two-yard touchdown to tight end Josh Cuevas. Tyler Loop added a 34-yard field goal. ten to nothing. Derrick Henry carried five times for 30 yards on that opening drive alone.

Then Baltimore never scored again.

Third quarter: Andre Szmyt, a 48-yard field goal. ten to three.

Early fourth quarter: Carson Schwesinger forced a fumble out of Derrick Henry in Baltimore territory. Tyson Campbell recovered it at the Ravens' 35. Audric Estimé, signed to the active roster on October 13, scored from two yards out. ten to ten.

Late fourth: Szmyt missed a 48-yard attempt wide left with 3:44 remaining, ending a streak of 25 consecutive made field goals.

Final drive: Jerry Jeudy caught a 33-yard pass on third-and-five to reach the Baltimore 29. Szmyt came back out and hit from 37 yards as time expired.

Ten first-quarter points, then forty-five minutes of nothing. Baltimore punted on seven of its last nine possessions.

The four numbers we told you to watch, graded.

On Sunday morning we published four things to watch. Here is the honest scorecard.

One: Derrick Henry's carries. We said hold him near fifteen and it means Mason Graham and Carson Schwesinger played, and played well. Henry finished with 17 carries for 58 yards, three point four a carry, against a season average above four, and he put the ball on the ground. Schwesinger did play, and he was the single most important defender on the field. Called correctly.

Two: targets to KC Concepcion. Jerry Jeudy was the one who delivered the throw that won it: four catches, 69 yards, including the 33-yarder on third-and-five. The receiver story broke a different way than we flagged. Partial.

Three: first-quarter drives dying on negative plays. They did not die, Cleveland simply did not score in the first quarter while Baltimore scored twice. Called correctly in spirit, and it cost them the lead.

Four, and here is the one we got wrong. We wrote that against Baltimore's twenty-third-ranked pass rush, anything above two sacks at halftime would mean the protection problem is structural and the excuses are finished. Baltimore recorded three sacks and five hits on Deshaun Watson in the first half alone.

So the alarm sounded. And Cleveland won anyway.

We were right that the protection is still broken. We were wrong to imply the game would turn on it. The game turned on a defense that refused to give up a point for three quarters. That is a lesson worth keeping: a bad offensive line can lose you a season, but it does not get to decide every single Sunday.

The real story. Forty-five minutes of nothing.

Everything else in this game is a footnote next to one fact. Cleveland allowed ten points in the first quarter and zero for the rest of the afternoon.

Against the team that entered the day leading the division. Against the running back who entered the day leading the National Football League in carries. Baltimore punted seven times in its final nine possessions.

The plays that built it, in order: Isaiah McGuire with a first-down sack. Maliek Collins with the second sack of the day. Denzel Ward breaking up a third-down pass intended for Mark Andrews to force a punt. Rookie Emmanuel McNeil-Warren breaking up a second-down throw late. Six pass breakups in all, with no interceptions, which tells you this was a coverage performance built on discipline rather than luck.

And then the one that decided it, which deserves its own section.

Carson Schwesinger decided this football game.

Cleveland's linebacker did not practice on Wednesday. He did not practice on Thursday. He returned Friday as a limited participant and was listed questionable with an ankle injury.

Early in the fourth quarter, with Baltimore in Cleveland territory and a ten to three lead, he put his helmet on the football and knocked it out of Derrick Henry's hands. Tyson Campbell recovered at the Ravens' 35. Six plays later it was tied.

It was the only turnover of the entire game.

That is the reigning Defensive Rookie of the Year, playing hurt, producing the single swing play of a division game. We wrote on Friday that Todd Monken's phrase "wired the right way" was a compliment and not a medical clearance, and we stand by the caution. But the football result is not ambiguous: Cleveland's best defender got on the field and won the game with one play.

And the Hall of Famer got held.

Derrick Henry came into Sunday with 113 carries, more than any player in football, for 476 yards and seven touchdowns. He had been handed the ball at least 23 times in every game but one.

He left with 17 carries for 58 yards and a lost fumble.

That is not a slump. That is a defense taking the thing an opponent does best and removing it, in a game where Baltimore had every reason to lean on him harder than usual because its most valuable player was in street clothes.

Give Henry his due as well: he opened the game with five carries for 30 yards and looked exactly like the problem everyone expected. The fact that Cleveland solved him between the first quarter and the fourth is the coaching story of the week, and almost nobody is telling it.

The kicker story, told properly.

Andre Szmyt walked onto that field for the final kick having just missed one.

He arrived at Sunday with 25 consecutive made field goals, the third longest streak in Browns history. He made a 48-yarder in the third quarter. Then, with 3:44 left in a tie game, he missed a 48-yarder wide left and the streak was gone.

Then he made the 37-yarder that won it. His fourth career game-winning kick.

His own explanation was the most professional sentence anybody said all weekend: he let the miss go, and he noted that the wind had shifted and adjusted for it.

That is the entire job in two clauses. Not confidence as a slogan, confidence as a procedure. Forget the last one, read the conditions, hit this one.

And the on time question got a partial answer.

We spent Sunday morning arguing that Cleveland's season rests on two words from the quarterbacks coach: getting the ball out on time. Here is where that stands after one game.

The good: Watson was sacked three times, down from eight at the Jets.

The bad: three sacks and five additional hits in the first half alone, against a pass rush ranked twenty-third in the league. If anything, the raw sack number flatters the afternoon.

And the honest part: Watson finished sixteen of twenty-six for 164 yards, plus eight carries for 29 yards. One hundred sixty-four passing yards wins you a thirteen to ten game. It does not win you many others. This is still, by every available measure, a bottom-tier passing offense, and Monken said so himself afterward, he wanted the offense better on second-and-short.

One win does not retire the problem. It just buys another week to work on it.

But that is now four game winning drives.

And here is the thing that has to go in the same paragraph as the 164 yards, because leaving it out would be dishonest.

Deshaun Watson has now engineered a fourth fourth-quarter game-winning drive this season. At Tampa Bay, against Carolina, against Pittsburgh, and now against Baltimore.

On the drive that won it, facing third-and-five, he threw a 33-yard completion to Jerry Jeudy to move Cleveland into field-goal range. That is not a checkdown. That is the throw the whole last month said he would not make.

You can hold two things at once. This passing offense is not good enough, and this quarterback keeps finishing games. Anyone telling you only one of those is selling something.

And the running back room is an emergency.

The least-discussed fact of the weekend is the most alarming one.

Cleveland finished with 93 net rushing yards, and did it with its fourth and fifth options. Jaleel McLaughlin led the team with 41 yards on nine carries. Audric Estimé, who was signed to the active roster on October 13, had 19 yards and the game-tying touchdown.

Quinshon Judkins, Dylan Sampson and Raheim Sanders were all unavailable. That is the starter and the two players behind him.

So Cleveland is four and two and in first place while running the ball with men who were on other rosters a week ago. That is a genuine achievement and a genuine warning at the same time. Dylan Sampson is practicing off injured reserve. Getting him and Judkins back is now the most urgent football business in the building, more urgent than anything involving a backup quarterback.

Now the other story, and what it gets right.

Which brings us to the weekend's loudest video, and the parts of it that hold up.

Shedeur Sanders did sign with an agency, and it is the first representation of his professional career. True, and reported by ESPN. He went through the entire 2025 draft process without an agent.

The agency does represent Travis Hunter. True. Hunter, his Colorado teammate, went second overall in the 2025 draft and won the 2024 Heisman Trophy. He signed with the agency before that draft.

Having no agent costs a player leverage. True, and well argued. An agent can call thirty-one other buildings, gauge a market, and bring a real offer back. A twenty-four-year-old cannot do that himself without it becoming a circus.

And "nobody hires an agent to sit on the bench" is a line worth taking seriously. We will come back to it, because there is a real answer.

And the claim we are not carrying.

Here is the part where Ktl Report stops and plants a flag.

The video says: "Reporting suggests there are ownership ties and silent investor connections that run through the same power networks this agent operates within."

No reporting says that. Not ESPN, not Cleveland.com, not any outlet we can find. The phrase "reporting suggests" is doing enormous work in that sentence, and underneath it there is nothing.

What is being described is a named, licensed, private individual being connected to unspecified "back channel infrastructure" and "silent investors" with no source, no document and no quote. That is not analysis. It is an insinuation about a real person's finances, and we will not repeat it, for the same reason we refused "Berry lied to Shedeur" last week. A claim that cannot be checked cannot be published.

And here is the irony: the actual, publicly reported facts about this agency and this agent are more interesting than the invention. So let us use those instead.

Who Young Money A P A A Sports actually is.

First, the name. It is Young Money A P A A Sports, not "Young Money Sports APA."

And the answer to "who is behind it" is not a shadow network. It is public, it has been reported for years, and it is genuinely fun: the agency comes out of Young Money Entertainment. It was established in 2016 after years of conversation between Lil Wayne, the label's CEO, and Cortez Bryant, his longtime manager and Young Money's COO.

It is not a boutique startup, that part of the video is correct. It has represented more than eighty athletes across multiple sports. Its roster has included Quinnen Williams, Mecole Hardman Junior and Jakobi Meyers, and one of its most prominent agents is Nicole Lynn, one of the most accomplished women in the business.

Bryant has also made a point that bears directly on this story: people wrongly assume Lil Wayne negotiates the contracts. He does not. The agency has professional agents who do that work.

So yes, Shedeur Sanders joined a real agency with real reach. That is true, documented, and does not require a single unnamed source.

And who Adie von Gontard actually is.

Now the man at the center of the insinuation, with his name spelled correctly: Adie von Gontard, NFLPA-certified since 2023.

The video calls him "Eddie Vanguard" and gestures at St. Louis money. Here are the facts, which have been publicly reported and require no whispering: his grandfather owned the St. Louis Cardinals, and his great-grandfather founded Anheuser-Busch. He was brought into the agency as a businessman to help grow the brand.

Read that again and notice what it is. Baseball and brewing. A family history in a different sport and a different industry, decades ago. It is a legitimately interesting biography. It is not evidence of NFL ownership influence, and nothing in it suggests anybody can make a football team start a quarterback.

This is what happens when a real fact gets dressed in ominous language. The fact was already public. The menace was added afterward.

Nobody hires an agent to sit on the bench.

Now the line the whole video rests on, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal.

It sounds airtight. It is actually incomplete, because it assumes the only reason to hire an agent is to leave.

Here are the other reasons, all of which apply to Shedeur Sanders this morning. He is in year two of a four-year contract that runs through 2028. Somebody has to handle the 2027 and 2028 conversation, and the earlier that work starts, the better it goes. He has marketing and endorsement business that has nothing to do with a depth chart. He needs someone to manage a public narrative that has been running without him for eighteen months. And if the day ever comes when a market does exist, the file has to already be built, you do not hire an agent the week you need one.

Hiring representation is not a trade request. It is the removal of an excuse for not having one. Those are different things, and the difference is the entire story.

And Sunday made his week worse, not better.

Here is the part the video could not have known when it was recorded, and it is the most important consequence of the whole weekend.

A thirteen to ten division win that puts Cleveland in first place is the worst possible outcome for a backup quarterback's market.

Think it through. Watson now has four fourth-quarter game-winning drives. The defense just held the division leader to ten points. The team is four and two with head-to-head wins over Baltimore and Pittsburgh. The one thing that reliably opens a quarterback job in October, a team falling out of the race, just got further away.

Monken said in August that the quarterback decision was "not a week-by-week call." Sunday is the strongest evidence yet that he meant it, and the strongest argument yet that he was right.

So the agent arrived in the same week the door got heavier. That is not a conspiracy. It is just timing, and it is brutal.

The two dates still on the wall.

Nothing about yesterday removed either deadline.

Late October: Dillon Gabriel's twenty-one day practice window, opened on October 6, expires. He has been practicing fully and was inactive on Sunday. Cleveland must put him on the 53-man roster or shut him down for the season. With the running back room in the state it is in, that roster spot is suddenly worth a great deal more than it was on Friday.

November 10: the trade deadline. Three weeks out, with Cleveland now in a divisional race it was not supposed to be in. Teams in first place on November 10 do not trade quarterbacks.

Where we stop.

We are not going to tell you Cleveland is a contender on the strength of a three-point win and 164 passing yards. We are not going to tell you the offensive line is fixed, because it gave up three sacks and five hits in a half to the league's twenty-third-ranked pass rush. And we are not going to tell you that an agent signing is a trade, because it is not one.

What we will tell you is this. This team is four and two, tied for first in the AFC North, with wins over both Pittsburgh and Baltimore, a defense that just pitched three shutout quarters against a Hall of Fame running back, a kicker who answered a miss with a game-winner, and a linebacker who played hurt and won it with one swing of his helmet.

That is a real football team. The arguments can wait a week.

The bottom line.

Browns 13, Ravens 10. Four and two. First place, shared, for the first time this late since 2014.

Henry: 17 carries, 58 yards, one fumble. Baltimore: ten points in fifteen minutes and nothing in the next forty-five. Schwesinger: one forced fumble that became the only turnover of the game. Szmyt: a miss at 3:44 and a game-winner at 0:00.

And on the other story: the agent is real, the agency is real and it belongs to Lil Wayne and Cortez Bryant rather than to a shadow network, the man's name is Adie von Gontard, his family's history is in baseball and brewing, and not one syllable of "silent investor connections" has a source attached to it.
