He graded the questions himself. Sixteen questions, one quarterback, and a press conference audited from the other side of the microphone.

First, the title, and the answer that demolishes it.

The title is "Shedeur Sanders Bombshell Just Got Some HUGE News Before Saints Game!"

There is no bombshell and there is no news. We can prove that with the first answer in the transcript, which is also the single best line in it.

A reporter asked him when he first learned he might be in line to start. He said:

"I would say the same time y'all got the news."

Then, when the room laughed, he was asked about his mindset:

"The same way how I've been preparing every week. No different in that regard."

So the quarterback found out when the press found out, and nothing about his week changed. Those two sentences are the entire content of a nine-minute session, and they are the opposite of a bombshell. The honest headline for this tape is "nothing changes," said by a man who meant it.

So we are going to do something we have never done here.

Normally we audit the video. Today we are going to audit the room.

Here is why. Three separate times in this press conference, Shedeur Sanders stopped and graded the question he had just been asked. Not rudely, flatly, and with a reason attached each time:

"We in like week seven and we talking about the offensive system fitting me. We could have figured that out, you know, in training camp."
"What that got to do with this game? Nothing."
"Y'all asking questions I probably didn't even think about to be honest. So I really don't have an answer for that."

When the subject of a press conference grades three of the questions on the record, the fair thing to do is grade all of them. So at Ktl Report we counted: sixteen questions were asked. Below is the scorecard, and then we examine the four exchanges that matter.

We are grading on one criterion only: could this question have produced information a viewer does not already have?

The scorecard. Sixteen questions.

One. When did you first get the news you might start, and how are you approaching the week? Grade: A. The only question in the session that produced a fact. More on this below.

Two. Last year you went through a similar thing. How much did that experience help? Grade: B. A real answer: he thanked the coaching staff and the general manager by name, then described seeing what he needed to improve and working on it. The premise, though, contains an error we fix below.

Three. You've had two instances of hopping into a game, one play, then four plays. What do you take from that? Grade: B minus. Precise and factual in its setup, which is rare. Got him to "I'm comfortable when the ball in my hands in general" and "play the long game."

Four. How does this offensive system fit you? Grade: D. He graded this one himself, and he was right.

Five. KC told us about the reps he got with you in the off-season. How valuable is that going into this week? Grade: A minus. The best-constructed question in the room: the reporter had done reporting elsewhere and brought it back. It produced the most useful line of the day, "tapping back into that memory bank" and "I don't think we lost a step."

Six. There was a lot said in camp about how you'd improved. How has that development continued in the regular season? Grade: C plus. Fair, and unanswerable in advance. He said so: "I guess we'll see. I guess we'll see."

Seven. Is this the first time we've talked to you since you hired an agent? Was that a natural development? Grade: F. On the Wednesday before his first start of the season. He graded it himself.

Eight. Have you had a chance to study the Saints defense? What are you seeing? Grade: B. Legitimate, and it got the most-clipped non-answer of the week. The full version is below and it changes the meaning.

Nine. You've seen this offense for eight games. What do you have to do for it to be successful? Grade: C. Boilerplate: "trust my teammates, trust the game plan and do everything I can."

Ten. How have you watched the offensive line improve, and what do you have to communicate given the differences between your game and Deshaun's? Grade: B plus. Two questions in one, but the second half was sharp: "to the outside world it's way more complex than what it is."

Eleven. What can you do to get this offense going earlier in the game? Grade: B. The most important football question in the room, and it produced the most revealing answer. Below.

Twelve. How do you establish your own tempo, pace and rhythm early in the game? Grade: C minus, and he graded it himself, but we are going to defend the reporter here.

Thirteen. How does this week change for you from a mindset perspective? Grade: D. Question one again, asked ninth. "Nothing changes."

Fourteen. Does it change from an excitement perspective? Grade: B minus. Asked after a near-duplicate, and it worked anyway: the longest and most personal answer of the day.

Fifteen. How difficult is the backup quarterback role for a competitor like you? Grade: C. Reasonable, produced "I just come and do my job and learn everything I can."

Sixteen. How have you been able to support Deshaun this season? Grade: A minus. The last question was one of the two best, and the answer is the one we would put on the thumbnail.

Room average: roughly a C plus. Four good questions, two that should not have been asked, ten that were fine and produced little. For a Wednesday before a backup's first start that is an ordinary day, which is the point, because an ordinary day does not contain a bombshell.

The one question that produced news, and nobody led with it.

Go back to "I would say the same time y'all got the news."

Think about what that sentence actually tells you. Nobody sat Shedeur Sanders down on Tuesday and told him he was probably starting on Sunday. He learned it the way everyone else did, when the team announced that Deshaun Watson had entered the concussion protocol.

That is not a scandal and we will not dress it up as one. It is how a protocol works: Watson passed his evaluations after the game and again on Monday morning, reported symptoms on Tuesday that had begun Monday night, and the club announced the protocol on Wednesday. Until Tuesday there was nothing to tell.

But it is informative, and it is the thing to take from this tape: Cleveland did not make a quarterback decision and then inform people. A medical process ran its course and the information reached the quarterback and the press at the same moment. Monken's words that day were "We'll let it play out. But Shedeur, again, he'll take the reps today with the ones."

A nine-minute press conference produced exactly one piece of new information, and it was in the first ten seconds.

The three he graded himself, and whether he was right.

Question four. How does this offensive system fit you?

"We in like week seven and we talking about the offensive system fitting me. We could have figured that out, you know, in training camp."

He was right, and the point is better than it sounds. "Does the system fit you?" is a question for March, or for camp. Nine weeks in, the system is the system and the quarterback's job is to run it. Asking on Wednesday whether the offense suits him invites him to either criticise the scheme or flatter it, and neither answer is information.

Grade upheld: D. And note what he did not say: he never said the system doesn't fit him. He said the question's clock had run out.

Question seven. The agent.

"Um, what that got to do with this game? Nothing. I'm focused on this game. Anything else really don't matter at this point, you know."

He was right, and we will take the next section to explain why we are not building anything on it.

Grade upheld: F.

Question twelve. How do you establish your own tempo, pace and rhythm early in the game?

"Y'all asking questions I probably didn't even think about to be honest. So I really don't have an answer for that."

Here we disagree with him, mildly, and side with the reporter. This was a real football question, maybe the most technical one asked, and the honest answer is more valuable than a confident one would have been. A quarterback who says "I haven't thought about that" four days out has told you something true about where his preparation sits.

Grade revised upward at Ktl Report: C minus becomes B. Not because the question was elegant, but because it worked.

The smile, with the clause everybody cuts off.

Here is the exchange that has been running in circles all week, and here is the complete version from the full transcript rather than the clipped one:

Q: "Have you had a chance to study the Saints defense yet a little bit? And what are you seeing so far?"
A: "Um, yeah, I've seen a lot. When I smile, just know that means something. I'm maturing. You know what I'm saying? I'm maturing. Like, I could have gave that, you know, a decent answer, but now I'm just going to smile whenever it's just like some questions, you know?"

Read the last six words: "whenever it's just like some questions."

That clause is almost always cut, and it is the one that settles the argument. He is not describing the Saints. He is describing a policy about certain kinds of questions, that from now on, when a question invites him to say something he should not say, he will smile instead of answering it.

So "did Sanders send New Orleans a warning?" has an answer, and the answer is no. The smile is not a message about a defense. It is him telling the room he has stopped taking the bait, which, for a twenty-four-year-old who has spent two years being quoted, is the most professional thing in the transcript.

Is it cheeky? Yes. Will a two-and-five defense use it anyway? Probably. But what he said and what he was reported to have said are not the same thing, and the gap is six words wide.

The agent question. What is true, and what we will not build on it.

Here is what is verified, because it is the only actual news item anywhere near this press conference and we are not going to pretend it does not exist.

Shedeur Sanders hired an agent, reported on October fourteenth, twenty twenty-six, three weeks before this session. He signed with Young Money APAA Sports, represented by its chief executive, Adie von Gontard. He had previously had no agent at all, handling his own representation through the draft and the start of his career.

That is the fact. It is three weeks old. It is not "HUGE news before the Saints game."

And here is what we are not carrying. Several outlets have taken that signing and spun it into trade speculation, including comparisons to another young player who hired an agent shortly before being traded. We are not running that. Not because it is impossible, but because the chain is: a player hired a representative, an ordinary step most players take before entering the league at all, and nobody has reported a request, a conversation or an interested club. A hire is not a signal. A signal requires a source.

He was asked about it on the Wednesday before the biggest start of his career and he said it had nothing to do with the game. He was right, and so we are leaving it exactly where he left it.

Watson, three separate times, unprompted each time.

This is the thread through the whole transcript, and it is what nobody is clipping.

Once, on the locker room:
"Deshaun led us this far and it's unfortunate he wasn't able to go out there, you know, but on my end, you know, I just got to step up for the quarterback room, for the team and do what I have to do."

Once, when handed a chance to criticise the offense:
"I don't want to just come in here and just try to discredit, you know, what Deshaun done and everything he done. That don't make me feel good as a person, you know."

And once, when invited to take credit for helping him:
"He's a vet, so if he asks any questions, he got another set of eyes, you know, that could validate certain things. But I think that's just something for him to speak on. I don't really want to input what I have done because then I'll be taking a credit. You know what I'm saying? Like that don't make sense."

Three invitations to put himself above the man whose injury gave him the job. Three refusals, and one of them turned down credit he was being offered for free.

That is the story in this transcript, better than a bombshell, because it is real, and because it is the thing that would actually matter if this were your quarterback room.

And the answer that will get him criticised, which deserves a closer read.

Asked what he could do to get the offense going earlier in games, he said:

"Uh just my job. I don't think it I don't think it been bad. How many wins we got? It can't be that bad, you know."

This is the line people will fight about, so let us handle it properly.

The case against him: Cleveland has been outscored sixty-five to thirteen in the first quarter, the worst figure in the NFL. Against Pittsburgh the Browns scored on two of twelve possessions and had no points in three of four quarters. By those numbers, "I don't think it been bad" is wrong.

The case for him: he is five and three, and he was asked on the Wednesday before his first start to agree in public that his own offense is bad, about teammates he has to lead on Sunday. The correct answer for a quarterback is the one he gave. "How many wins we got" is a defense of the room, not a denial of the tape, and he followed it immediately with the refusal to discredit Watson.

Our verdict: the number is against him, the instinct is for him. If he says it again after Sunday we will hold him to the number. On Wednesday, with a locker room listening, we would not have wanted a different answer.

The error in the question, not the answer.

This is the catch of the day, and it belongs to the room rather than the player.

As transcribed, a reporter set up question two like this: "last year you kind of went through a similar thing, you know, Winston's backup and then, you know, thrust into the into the starting job."

That is not what happened. In twenty twenty-five, Cleveland's quarterback depth chart out of camp was Joe Flacco as the starter, Dillon Gabriel as the backup, and Shedeur Sanders third. Sanders was not anybody's backup at the start of that season; he was Q B three. And the man whose injury eventually opened the door was Dillon Gabriel, concussed in Week eleven against Baltimore, after which Sanders made his first start in Week twelve against Las Vegas.

Jameis Winston was Cleveland's quarterback in twenty twenty-four, not twenty twenty-five.

So the analogy the reporter was reaching for is actually stronger than the one he said, Sanders has now been promoted past two different men by two different head injuries, from two different places on the depth chart, but the name and the role in the question were both wrong. If you quote that question in your video, correct it on screen, because viewers will take the premise as fact.

And the two small things he got wrong.

"We in like week seven." It is Week Nine. Cleveland has played eight games.

The offensive line question also says "the first seven games," which the reporter got wrong too, so he was answering the number he was given.

Neither matters. We note them only because this is a transcript piece and we check everything that is checkable. In nine minutes of unscripted speech, two loose week numbers is a clean tape.

What this tape says about Sunday.

On Thursday we printed four things we would call success on Sunday, before kickoff, so nobody could move the standard afterwards. For the record, they were: one turnover or fewer; any first-quarter points at all; four sacks or fewer; one real connection with a rookie receiver.

Nothing in this transcript changes them, but two things in it bear on them.

On the rookie connection: he confirmed the thing the criterion rests on. "Tapping back into into that memory bank, you know, having those reps... so I don't think we we lost a step." The chemistry with KC Concepcion and Denzel Boston is the one asset he brings that the starter does not, and he says it is intact.

On the sacks: his answer about the line was "I think we all got a great relationship with them." He is not planning to communicate differently. Given thirty-three sacks allowed in eight games we would have liked something more specific, but "more specific" from a quarterback four days out usually means telling the opponent something.

And on the first-quarter criterion, he gave the one answer that makes it harder to grade: he does not accept the premise that the starts have been bad. Sunday settles it either way.

The bottom line.

There is no bombshell on this tape and the player said so himself in the first ten seconds. He found out he might be starting at the same moment the press did, and his week did not change, and that is not a scandal, it is how a concussion protocol works when a player passes two evaluations and then reports symptoms honestly the next day.

What is on the tape is sixteen questions and a quarterback who graded three of them while answering the rest.
