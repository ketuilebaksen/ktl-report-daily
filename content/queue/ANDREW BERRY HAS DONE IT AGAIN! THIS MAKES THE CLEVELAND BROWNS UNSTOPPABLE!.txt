Andrew Berry did it again, but not the way you think. The four moves he refused to make, and the trade that already paid for 2029.

First, let's be honest about the headline.

If you came here for a blockbuster Andrew Berry just pulled off this week, we will be straight with you, because that is the deal at Ktl Report: Berry did not complete a splashy trade in the last few days. The Browns' only transactions this week were a practice-squad signing and two injured-reserve return designations.

And yet the headline is still true, and the reason is better than a blockbuster.

Berry has done it again, he has produced a second consecutive draft class that plays as rookies, he has a trade on the books that funds this franchise through 2029, and this week he did the hardest thing a general manager does in October: nothing. Four separate moves the internet demanded, and he declined all four.

Is the team "unstoppable"? No. We are going to audit that word honestly below, with the defensive ranks attached, because this team allowed twenty-three point seven points a game through three weeks and that is not an unstoppable defense. But the better word for what Berry has built is funded, and funded is how a three and one team in first place stops needing a miracle.

The trade that already paid for 2029.

Start with the biggest thing Berry has done, because the emotion around it buried the terms.

On June 1, 2026, Cleveland traded Myles Garrett to the Los Angeles Rams. What came back:

DE Jared Verse
A 2027 first-round pick
A 2028 second-round pick
A 2029 third-round pick

Berry's own words at the time: "We recognize the unexpected nature of this trade, but it opens up great opportunities for our franchise." He added that the organization had long hoped Garrett would be a one-helmet player for his entire career, but that this was best for the long-term future.

Set aside how it felt and look at what it does. A 2027 first, a 2028 second and a 2029 third means Berry walks into the next three drafts with extra ammunition on top of his own picks. That is the structural reason this front office can keep saying no in October: a general manager with surplus future capital never has to accept a bad price today. Every decision below flows from that trade.

The 2026 class, four games in.

Now the part that actually justifies "done it again."

Cleveland's 2026 draft was a ten-player class with two first-round picks: offensive lineman Spencer Fano and receiver KC Concepcion at number twenty-four. Then Denzel Boston at number thirty-nine and safety Emmanuel McNeil-Warren in the second, Austin Barber in the third, Parker Brailsford and linebacker Justin Jefferson in the fifth, tight end Joe Royer and quarterback Taylen Green in the sixth, and tight end Carsen Ryan in the seventh.

Four games later, here is what those picks are doing. Against Pittsburgh, Boston played 61 of 64 offensive snaps, 95 percent, with four catches for 89 yards including a sixty yarder, and he has now led the team in receiving in back-to-back games. Concepcion played 59 snaps, 92 percent, catching six of seven targets. Fano and Barber are both drawing praise for their work on a retooled offensive line.

Two rookie receivers taking nine of every ten snaps for a first-place offense is not normal, most high picks need a year. Berry's two 2026 receivers are already the top names on the depth chart, which is also why a veteran on a 52.5 million dollars contract is down to 3seven and a half percent of the snaps. That is not a scandal. That is the draft working.

And the 2025 class just took its leap.

This is the piece almost nobody is connecting, and it is the strongest evidence of all, because one good draft is luck and two in a row is a process.

Mason Graham, the 2025 first-rounder, had half a sack as a rookie. Through three games this season he led the team with four sacks and six tackles for loss, including a two-sack, three-tackle-for-loss afternoon against Carolina. That is a textbook year-two defensive-line leap, and it is the single biggest reason Cleveland's front still functions without Garrett.

Carson Schwesinger has played every defensive snap and leads the defense with 26 tackles. Harold Fannin Junior has caught the go-ahead touchdowns in two of these wins. Ronnie Hickman and Myles Harden have the team's interceptions. Grant Delpit has played every snap at strong safety with a sack to go with it.

A front office whose 2025 picks are now starting and whose 2026 picks are already starting has solved the one problem money cannot solve. Credit where it belongs, and the name is Andrew Berry, not "Barry," the eighth consecutive transcript in which we have had to fix it.

The four moves he refused to make.

Here is the real "again," and it will never trend, because restraint has no highlight.

One: he did not sell Shedeur Sanders into a buyer's market. Six teams have a reason to call and the floated price is a fifth-round pick. A general manager with a 2027 first in his pocket does not need that fifth.

Two: he did not dump Jerry Jeudy at his lowest moment. Adam Schefter said on October 4 that Jeudy is "being squeezed out" and "could be had." Mary Kay Cabot reported the Browns have no plans to move him absent an overwhelming offer. Jeudy is at 3seven and a half percent of the snaps, the exact point at which his trade value is lowest, and therefore the exact wrong time to sell.

Three: he did not rush Dillon Gabriel back. The practice window opened October 6. Todd Monken, asked if he felt urgency: "Not really. We opened up the three-week window to kind of see where we're at." Twenty-one days of free evaluation, taken in full.

Four: he did not expose Taylen Green to waivers. Cabot reported that trying to slide the rookie sixth-rounder to the practice squad would be risky, because any team can claim him. So he stays.

Four invitations to act. Four declines. That is the skill, and it is only available to a GM who is not desperate, which brings us back to the Garrett haul.

And the unglamorous ones he did make.

Two small moves this week, both of which look like nothing and are exactly the job.

Tylan Wallace, the receiver who earns his spot in the return game, missed the Pittsburgh game with a knee injury. Rather than wait to find out on Sunday, Berry worked out and signed James Proche the Second to the practice squad on October 6: 30 years old, 71 NFL games, two prior seasons in Cleveland, 22 punt returns for 197 yards here, and 68 career punts fielded. A plug-and-play returner, in the building before the emergency instead of after it.

And on the same day Cleveland opened the return window for defensive tackle Kalia Davis, a 28-year-old who started all 17 games for San Francisco last season, precisely as Mason Graham was expected to miss time with a sprained MCL and a sprained ankle.

Nobody makes a thumbnail out of that. It is also the difference between surviving October and panicking in it.

Now the honest part. The Watson contract is also his.

We are not going to write a tribute article and skip the bill, because you would be right not to trust us afterward.

The same general manager authorized the Deshaun Watson contract: 230 million dollars fully guaranteed, the most aggressive quarterback commitment in the history of the sport. It is in its final year, it carries an 86.2 million dollars charge against the 2027 cap through dead-money acceleration, and The Athletic's Zac Jackson has reported the chances Watson is on this roster next season are "slim."

That is not a footnote. It is the largest single decision of Berry's tenure and it has not worked out the way the franchise hoped.

So the fair version of "he did it again" is this: Berry is out-drafting his own worst contract. Two straight classes of immediate contributors, a trade that funds three future drafts, a roster in first place, all while absorbing the heaviest cap consequence in the league. You can hold both thoughts at once, and anybody selling you only one of them is selling you something.

So is this team unstoppable? The audit.

No. Here are the numbers, and they are the reason we would not put that word in a headline without this section under it.

Through three games, Cleveland's defense allowed twenty-three point seven points per game, 18th in the league. It allowed four and a half yards per carry, 23rd, and 13 runs of ten or more yards, tied for 30th. It had 28 missed tackles, tied for the second-most in football. It had seven sacks and two interceptions.

The good news in the same file is real: ninth in completion percentage allowed, eighth in red-zone defense, 13th on third down, and it had not allowed a single rushing touchdown, one of only five teams in the league. That is a defense that bends badly between the twenties and then slams the door, which is precisely what the Carolina and Pittsburgh fourth quarters looked like.

The offense tells the same story. Point totals of 10, 23, 21 and 27, 81 points in four games, about 20 a game. Watson is completing sixty-five point oh percent of his passes, not the "nearly 70" being quoted: 80 completions on 123 attempts, which anyone can check.

That is a three and one team that wins close games, not an unstoppable one. The honest adjective for October is durable.

And Jared Verse hasn't arrived yet.

One more piece of discipline, because it cuts against the argument we just made.

Jared Verse, the player half of the Garrett trade, has not recorded a sack as a Brown. He has two quarterback hits, eight tackles and two tackles for loss on roughly two-thirds of the defensive snaps. With the Rams in 2025 he had seven and a half sacks and 11 tackles for loss.

So the trade's win, so far, is entirely in the picks. The player is still pending. If you want to argue that Berry did it again, you have to argue it on the 2027 first, the 2028 second and the 2029 third, not on Verse's stat line, which is a 24-year-old pass rusher learning a new scheme. That may change by December. It has not changed yet, and we are not going to pretend otherwise just because it fits the story.

What that draft capital actually buys.

Here is the part of the Garrett trade nobody has walked through properly, and it is the key to every "why won't they just trade him" argument this autumn.

Barring further moves, Cleveland is in line for two first-round picks in the 2027 draft, its own, plus the one acquired from the Rams. Then an extra second-rounder in 2028 and an extra third in 2029, again on top of its own selections.

Now put that next to the quarterback question. Deshaun Watson is in the final year of his contract. The most likely version of Cleveland's 2027 offseason is a team with two first-round picks, a young roster, and a decision to make at the most important position in sports.

A front office in that position has three real paths: draft a quarterback high with its own pick, package the second first to move up for one, or sit on the picks and let the market come to it. All three of those are open because of one June trade. None of them require getting a fifth-round pick for Shedeur Sanders in November.

That is why "keep all four quarterbacks" is not indecision. A team that can buy a quarterback in April does not need to sell one in October. The internet keeps reading Berry's inaction as confusion; it is more likely a man who has already paid for his next option and sees no reason to take a discount on this one.

The inverse is the warning: capital only works if you hit on it, which is exactly why the 2025 and 2026 classes matter beyond this season.

The five things that could still undo all of it.

Any article with "unstoppable" in the headline owes you the risk list. Here it is, and it is not short.

The run defense. four and a half yards per carry allowed, 23rd in the league, with 13 runs of ten or more yards, tied for 30th. That is a leak, and in December, in Cleveland, against Baltimore and Pittsburgh, teams will run at it.

The missed tackles. Twenty-eight through three games, tied for the second-most in football. Missed tackles are the statistic that turns a ten-yard run into a forty-yard run, and they are the thing most likely to turn one of these three-point wins into a blowout loss.

The pass rush without Garrett. Seven sacks in three games, with the team's leader being a second-year defensive tackle and the secondary contributing two of them. Verse has none yet. If Graham's MCL keeps him out and the interior pressure goes with him, the whole bend-don't-break structure gets harder to sustain.

The 2027 cap. An 86.2 million dollars charge for a quarterback who may not be on the roster. That number will shape free agency whether the team is good or not, and no draft class erases it.

And the margin itself. Cleveland has won three straight games in the final minutes. The 1980 Kardiac Kids won a division that way, twelve of sixteen games decided by seven points or fewer, and that season ended with an interception in the end zone in four-degree weather. Living on margins is a skill right up until the week it is a coin flip.

None of that cancels the case for Berry. It just means the correct tense is building, not built.

And about that house video.

Since it is everywhere this week: the claim that Shedeur Sanders "unofficially confirmed" a trade to Miami by buying a house in Fort Lauderdale does not survive one check.

The apartments were for Shilo Sanders, and Shedeur was the one helping. They were rentals, one at eight thousand nine hundred and fifty dollars a month, another at nine thousand nine hundred dollars, with a broker telling them approval would take about 72 hours. Shedeur offered to cover the deposit: "If you just let me take care of the down payment. You just pay per month." Shilo's reply, joking: "We got to wait till your next contract." Bleacher Report wrote the video up on October 6 and drew no trade connection, because there is none.

Shilo is in South Florida because he was an undrafted free agent with Tampa Bay who did not make the 53, and because his Chapter 7 case over an 11.89 million dollars default judgment has final arguments in November. And the "I'll be having security in my house" clip being used as atmosphere is from a conversation about the burglary at Shedeur's Ohio home in November 2025, when roughly two hundred thousand dollars was taken while he played his first NFL game.

We are not going to map where a 24-year-old is apartment hunting, and we are not using footage of a robbery victim discussing home security to sell a trade theory. Compensation or it isn't a trade.

This week's real news, and a correction of our own.

The actual headline from the beat on October 8: Cabot reported the Browns are eyeing keeping all four quarterbacks, Watson, Sanders, Gabriel and Green, with no plans to trade Sanders or Gabriel.

And we owe you a fix. Yesterday we wrote that Philadelphia was the one team this season carrying four quarterbacks. Cabot's piece names three: the Falcons, Eagles and Steelers. That changes the argument, one team doing it is an oddity, three teams doing it is a normal roster choice. Ours was incomplete, and we would rather say so out loud than quietly edit it.

It still costs a roster spot Cleveland would rather spend on the defensive line while Graham heals. But it is no longer the unheard-of thing everyone has been calling it.

Where we stop.

"Unstoppable." Eighteenth in points allowed, 20 points a game on offense, two one-score wins. No.

"Berry just pulled off a blockbuster." This week's moves were a practice-squad signing and two IR designations.

"Berry is a genius / Berry is a fraud." Neither. He authorized the Watson contract and he has drafted two straight classes that play. Both are true and the record is still being written.

"Shedeur confirmed a trade." He helped his brother look at two rental apartments.

Any trade prediction. Cabot: no plans. Fowler: no movement. Grossi: "I don't expect him to be traded."

Grades on the Garrett trade. A deal built on 2027, 2028 and 2029 picks cannot be graded in 2026, by us or anybody.

Sunday at the Jets.

Cleveland is at the New York Jets on Sunday, October 11, one o'clock Eastern on CBS, the Browns' first game as a first-place team. Aaron Glenn's Jets are one and three with an offense that has gone quiet around Geno Smith.

Three things to watch, all of them Berry's handiwork. Whether Boston and Concepcion hold those ninety plus percent snap shares on the road, which is where rookie receivers usually get exposed. Whether Mason Graham's absence shows up in the run defense that was already allowing four and a half a carry. And whether Shedeur Sanders takes a snap in a game still being decided, because every week without one is a week the asking price stays a fifth.

Then Baltimore comes to Cleveland October 18 with Lamar Jackson's ankle an open question. New Orleans at home November 8, the deadline November 10, the bye in Week 11.

The bottom line.

Andrew Berry did do it again. He just did it in April, in June, and in the four phone calls he declined to return.

The 2026 class is playing. The 2025 class took its leap, with Mason Graham turning half a sack into four. The Garrett trade stocked 2027, 2028 and 2029. And this week, with six teams circling his backup quarterback and the most-read insider in the sport saying his veteran receiver could be had, he did not blink.

He is also the man who guaranteed 230 million dollars to Deshaun Watson and will pay 86.2 million dollars for it in 2027. That is the whole picture: a general manager digging out of his own biggest hole with the one tool that actually works, picks that hit.

Not unstoppable. Not yet. But for the first time in a long while, Cleveland is a team that does not have to do anything desperate, and that, not a blockbuster, is what Berry did again.

So here's our question for you: which was the better Berry decision, the Garrett haul of a 2027 first, a 2028 second and a 2029 third, or spending picks 24 and 39 on two receivers who are now playing 92 and 95 percent of the snaps?
