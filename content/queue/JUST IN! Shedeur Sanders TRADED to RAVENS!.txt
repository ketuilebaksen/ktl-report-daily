We tried to draft you. The Ravens angle is real, it just points the opposite way.

The claim at the center of this is backwards.

There is a theory going around that deserves a careful answer, because the underlying facts are fascinating and the version being told has one of them pointing the wrong way.

The theory: Lamar Jackson is hurt, Baltimore is a win-now franchise, and, the load-bearing claim, "Todd Monken is now the offensive coordinator for the Baltimore Ravens. That connection exists. It is documented."

It is documented. It is also the reverse of what is being claimed. Todd Monken was Baltimore's offensive coordinator for three seasons. In January 2026 the Browns hired him as their head coach. He is not in Baltimore. He is standing on Cleveland's sideline, holding the play sheet, and he is the man who decides whether Shedeur Sanders takes a snap.

So the "connection" is real and it is remarkable. It just means something close to the opposite of what the video says. At Ktl Report we are going to lay the whole sequence out, because once you see it in order, it is one of the better stories in the league, and it argues against a Baltimore trade rather than for one.

What actually happened in April 2025.

Here is the chain of events, all of it reported at the time.

Going into the fifth round of the 2025 draft, the Baltimore Ravens planned to take Shedeur Sanders. ESPN reported it. And then, per that same reporting, Sanders told them he did not want to be drafted into a depth chart behind Lamar Jackson. He wanted a chance to compete, and competing behind a two-time MVP was not a realistic version of that.

Baltimore moved on. With pick number 141, the Ravens selected Alabama A&M offensive lineman Carson Vinson instead.

Three picks later, at number 144, the Cleveland Browns took Shedeur Sanders.

Three picks. That is the entire margin between Shedeur Sanders being a Raven and being a Brown, and it broke that way because he asked it to. Not an agent's leverage play, not a slide, not a snub. A 23-year-old looked at a roster and said he would rather go somewhere he could compete.

Hold onto that. It becomes the most interesting fact in this whole conversation.

And then the man who tried to draft him became his head coach.

On January 30, 2026, Monken met Sanders at the Browns facility, with cameras rolling for the team's own channel. What he said:

"We tried to draft your ass last year for God's sake."
>
"It all worked out. You remember that, right? Some day we'll get a chance to talk about that."

That is Baltimore's former offensive coordinator, now Cleveland's head coach, telling the quarterback he once tried to draft that it all worked out.

Everyone has been reading that clip as a trade clue. It is the opposite. The person with the most professional investment in Shedeur Sanders succeeding is the man who tried to acquire him before Cleveland did. The Ravens' interest did not stay in Baltimore, it walked into Berea with the head coach.

And here is the irony nobody named.

Now put the two halves together, because this is the part that stopped us.

In 2025, Shedeur Sanders declined Baltimore for one specific reason: Lamar Jackson was there. That was the whole objection, not the city, not the scheme, not the coaching staff he liked enough that its coordinator now coaches him. The obstacle was the quarterback.

And the only reason anybody is floating Baltimore in October 2026 is that Lamar Jackson got hurt.

So the scenario being sold as destiny is this: the one team he turned down becomes an option again precisely because the one thing he objected to is temporarily unavailable. That is not fate. That is a coincidence with a short shelf life, because ankle sprains heal and two-time MVPs come back.

We are not going to predict anything from it. We just think anyone excited about the Baltimore angle should know they are excited about the removal of the exact condition that made him say no the first time.

The quarterback injury wave, with the real details.

The premise underneath all of this is sound and worth stating properly, because a league short on quarterbacks does create a market.

Lamar Jackson sprained his ankle, left in a walking boot, underwent an MRI, and was ruled out of Week 5 against Atlanta, with Tyler Huntley (Huntley, not "Hunley") starting in his place. The range being discussed publicly is one to three weeks depending on severity.

Marcus Mariota suffered a significant right MCL sprain, a re-injury of a leg that has already had multiple surgeries, with a timeline running anywhere from one week to potentially the rest of the season. That matters for Washington, though the same reporting indicates Jayden Daniels is likely back, which largely closes the hole Mariota was filling.

Dak Prescott played with a wrap on a hand laceration and appears to be fine, on a day Dallas and CeeDee Lamb (not "CD Lamb") put up big numbers.

So: one franchise quarterback out for at least a week, one backup possibly out for the season on a team getting its starter back, and one minor hand injury. A real injury wave, and notice that only one of those three situations even creates a buyer.

What Daniel Oyefusi actually said.

The beat reporter in this transcript is Daniel Oyefusi of ESPN, rendered as "Yuzi" and "Yafuzi" in the captions, and he is one of the best-sourced people on this team. Here is what he said, including the parts the video skipped.

On a trade: "I still am standing by my report from last week that they have no plans of trading Shedeur Sanders or Dillon Gabriel at this time." And: "I just don't really think that they want to trade either of those guys. So I don't want to go down that road. at this point I would be surprised."

On the caveat, which is fair and which he volunteered: "Everyone has a price. Everyone has a price. We found that out with Myles Garrett. So if someone comes calling and makes the Browns a nice offer that they can't refuse and they feel good about what they have in their room, then I'm sure they would probably think about doing something."

And then the answer that is the actual news in this interview, which we will come back to: asked which quarterback would have the most value to a team that needed one, Oyefusi said "if you were really looking for someone that could step in and start some games for you, perhaps you might want to think about a Dillon Gabriel."

The part of his answer the video left out.

This is the single most useful sentence anybody has said about the October trade market, and it got cut.

Oyefusi: "You can't just pick up a quarterback at this point in the season and plug him in and have him start, unless he's like maybe a Joe Flacco or a longtime veteran who knows your system, or knows some facsimile of your system."

That is the whole thing. A mid-season quarterback trade almost never produces a starter. The playbook is different, the protection calls are different, the terminology is different, and there is no offseason to learn any of it. Which is why desperate teams in October trade for veterans, Brissett, Minshew, Dalton, the names on every deadline list, and why they wait until April to acquire young quarterbacks they intend to develop.

A team whose starter goes down in Week 5 does not fix 2026 by acquiring a 24-year-old who has thrown one pass this season. It fixes 2026 with a 35-year-old who has run the scheme. Every "quarterback injury crisis means Shedeur gets traded" argument runs straight into that wall, and the reporter being quoted in the video is the one who put the wall there.

But the roster squeeze is genuinely real.

Where the video is right, and it is right, is the arithmetic.

Cleveland has four quarterbacks: Deshaun Watson, Shedeur Sanders, Dillon Gabriel and Taylen Green. Gabriel's twenty-one day practice window opened October 6 after his back injury, and at the end of it the Browns must either activate him to the 53-man roster or lose him for the season.

And the week could not be worse for spare roster spots. Mason Graham (sprained MCL and ankle), Mike Hall Junior (ankle) and Kalia Davis (quad) were all ruled out, three defensive tackles, along with Carsen Ryan, Tylan Wallace and Teven Jenkins. Monken's answer was "it's next man up" and an elevation off the practice squad.

The Taylen Green problem is also real, and Oyefusi framed it honestly: they like Green, this is effectively a redshirt year for him, and trying to slide him through waivers to the practice squad is risky because a quarterback-desperate team would claim him. His telling observation: if that had been the plan, "maybe they would have done that from the beginning."

For the record: three teams are currently carrying four quarterbacks, the Falcons, Eagles and Steelers. It is possible. It is just expensive in a week like this one.

And the real news is about Gabriel, not Sanders.

Here is the finding that should have been the headline, and it has now come from two independent directions.

Oyefusi, asked who holds the most trade value for a team that needs a quarterback to start, named Dillon Gabriel. And ESPN's Jeremy Fowler reported weeks ago that if Cleveland moved Sanders specifically, the front office would need to be genuinely comfortable that Gabriel could handle being the primary backup, the clear implication being that Gabriel is the one whose role is replaceable, not Sanders.

Two ESPN reporters, two different conversations, same direction. The market's logical target in Cleveland is the third-string quarterback on a twenty-one day clock, not the backup who has the job.

That also squares with everything else on the record: Mary Kay Cabot and Ian Rapoport have both reported the Browns will not trade a quarterback even after Gabriel's return, and Cabot has reported Cleveland is eyeing keeping all four. Tony Grossi: "I don't expect him to be traded." The only disagreement in the entire corpus is how open the door is, not who is standing nearest to it.

And Baltimore, specifically, is the one team that cannot do this.

Set aside everything above for a second and just look at the matchup, because the Ravens are the least plausible trade partner on the entire board for four separate reasons.

One: they play each other. Baltimore comes to Cleveland on October 18, and these teams meet twice a year, forever. Nobody hands a division rival a quarterback mid-season, the radio host in this very transcript raised that objection himself and could not make it work either.

Two: Baltimore already has the Flacco answer. Tyler Huntley has been in that building, knows that offense, and started Week 5 in Lamar's place. He is the living example of the principle Oyefusi described: the useful mid-season backup is the one who already knows the system. Acquiring a quarterback who has never run a snap of it would make Baltimore worse for the three weeks it matters.

Three: Baltimore's own evaluation was a fifth-round grade. This is the detail the Cleveland radio voice got exactly right and the viral version ignores: "Yes, the Ravens wanted to draft him, but that was in the fifth round. It's not like they were going after him in the second round. They were viewing him as more of a developmental quarterback, too." A team that valued a player as a late-round project in April 2025 does not spend meaningful draft capital on him as an in-season rescue in October 2026.

Four: Lamar Jackson is coming back. He was ruled out for one game. Trading real assets for a quarterback you will not need in three weeks is how front offices get fired.

The Baltimore story is a great piece of history. It is not a transaction.

The three picks that decided a career.

One more thing worth sitting with, because it reframes the whole "he's being buried in Cleveland" complaint.

Baltimore was going to take him at 141. He said no. Cleveland took him at 144. Three picks.

Now run the other version. In the Baltimore timeline he spends 2025 and 2026 behind a healthy two-time MVP in his prime, in an offense built around that quarterback's legs, with no realistic path to a start and no meaningful passes thrown. There is no "he should be starting" conversation there, because there is no argument to have, nobody in America demands a team bench Lamar Jackson.

In the Cleveland timeline he got seven starts last season at 24, roughly 1,400 yards and seven touchdowns, two straight wins to close the year, and he is the listed number two on a team leading its division, with a national argument raging about whether he deserves more, which only exists because the job above him is contestable.

So when people say Cleveland wasted him, the honest comparison is not Cleveland versus a fantasy where he starts. It is Cleveland versus the team he turned down, where he would be invisible. He made a judgment at 23 about where he could compete, and on the evidence he was right. Which is why this is frustrating rather than tragic: he chose the room where the door could open, and the only thing missing is somebody opening it three plays at a time.

How Shedeur is actually being developed.

Oyefusi also gave the clearest description yet of what the backup's week looks like, and it is worth reading honestly rather than as a grievance.

"The plan all along, as Monken said, has been to kind of play the long game. they were pleased with what he did in the spring and the summer, almost kind of making it a competition, in Monken's words."

And then the mechanics: "It's tough honestly for any backup, let alone a young backup quarterback, because these guys aren't getting really any type of number-one reps during the week, all that is going toward the starter. He's running scout team in moments, and the hope is that behind the scenes he's doing the work. A lot of the onus is on that individual player to make sure they're getting the extra reps and the extra mental reps."

That is a straight answer, and also the strongest argument for the thing we have asked for since September: three to five designed snaps in neutral situations. If the plan depends on a 24-year-old self-administering mental reps while never taking a meaningful snap, the organization is outsourcing its own evaluation to hope. A package costs almost nothing and converts hope into information.

And the durability point, stated fairly.

One more argument from the transcript deserves a fair hearing, because it was raised by the interviewer rather than invented by a fan: "Very few quarterbacks anymore make it through 17 games. We want to be confident that he's able to take over and play winning football."

That is a reasonable thing to say about any team, and Deshaun Watson's availability history is a matter of public record. We are not going to speculate about anyone's body or predict an injury, that is where we draw the line in this series. But the general point stands on its own and it cuts hard against every trade scenario: a team one snap from needing its backup does not sell the backup in October.

Which lands where everything else here lands. The injury wave does not pressure Cleveland to sell. It pressures Cleveland to keep what it has, the quarterbacks getting hurt around the league being the reminder of why you carry three.

Where we stop.

"Todd Monken is the Ravens' offensive coordinator." He was, for three seasons. The Browns hired him as head coach in January 2026.

"Baltimore is pursuing Shedeur Sanders." No reporter has written that. The Ravens' documented interest was a fifth-round plan in 2025, and Sanders himself declined it.

"The injury wave means a trade." Oyefusi, in the same interview, explained why mid-season quarterback acquisitions almost never start.

Any prediction about Lamar Jackson's recovery. He was ruled out for Week 5 and Tyler Huntley started. Everything past that is other people's guessing, and we are not doing injury speculation.

"Shedeur is being buried." The reporting describes a long-game development plan with scout-team reps. We think that plan is incomplete without live snaps. "Incomplete" and "buried" are different words.

Any trade prediction before November 10. Oyefusi would "be surprised." Cabot and Rapoport report no quarterback trade. Fowler senses no movement. Grossi does not expect one.

What's next.

Cleveland went into Sunday at three and one, alone in first place in the AFC North, playing at the New York Jets without three defensive tackles.

Then the schedule turns: Baltimore comes to Cleveland on October 18, and whether Lamar Jackson's ankle allows him to play in that game is now one of the most consequential questions in the division. After that it is New Orleans at home on November 8, the trade deadline on November 10, and the bye in Week 11.

Two things to watch that bear on everything above. Cleveland's decision at the end of Gabriel's twenty-one day window, in late October, activate, keep four, or let him revert to injured reserve, which will tell you how this front office truly ranks the room. And whether Shedeur Sanders takes a snap in a game that is still being decided, because every week without one is a week the development plan runs on faith.

The bottom line.

The Baltimore connection is real, documented, and almost exactly backwards from how it is being sold.

In April 2025 the Ravens planned to draft Shedeur Sanders in the fifth round. He told them he did not want to sit behind Lamar Jackson. Baltimore took Carson Vinson at 141; Cleveland took Sanders at 144. Then, in January, Baltimore's offensive coordinator became Cleveland's head coach and told him on camera, "We tried to draft your ass last year for God's sake. It all worked out."

So the man who wanted him in Baltimore already has him. And the only thing that makes Baltimore interesting again is a temporary absence of the very quarterback he cited as his reason for saying no.

Meanwhile the actual reporting is boringly consistent. Daniel Oyefusi would be surprised by a trade. Cabot and Rapoport say it is not happening. Fowler senses nothing. And when Oyefusi was asked who a quarterback-needy team should actually want from this room, he said Dillon Gabriel, not Shedeur Sanders.

Everyone has a price, as Oyefusi said, and Myles Garrett proved it. But a three and one team one snap away from needing its backup, missing three defensive tackles, with a head coach who personally tried to draft the kid, is not a team looking for a reason to say yes.
