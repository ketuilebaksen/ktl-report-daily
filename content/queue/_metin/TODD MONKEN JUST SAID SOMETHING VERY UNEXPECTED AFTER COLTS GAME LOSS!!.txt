Monday in Berea. Seven decisions on Todd Monken's desk, and the one he already made.

First, a correction before we start.

Quick housekeeping, because accuracy is the whole product at Ktl Report.

Cleveland did not lose to the Colts. The Browns lost seventeen to twelve to the New York Jets at MetLife Stadium on Sunday, in rain that never let up. Cleveland has not played Indianapolis this season.

And Monken has not announced a shocking decision. He announced two words, "next man up", and a promise: "We're going to own it and fight like hell to fix it."

Here is why this is still the most important Monday of Cleveland's season. By the time that plane landed, Monken had seven real decisions on his desk, all of them due before Baltimore arrives Sunday. We are going to work through them the way the building will: what happened, what it costs, the options, and what we would do.

New format this week. Less essay, more war room.

The Monday ledger.

What broke. The offense produced 198 net yards, a season low, and 13 first downs. Deshaun Watson went 15 of 33 for 155 yards with one touchdown and took eight sacks, a season high. Cleveland fumbled five times and lost two. Punter Corey Bojorquez let a punt snap slip through his hands inside his own territory with just over four minutes left; the Jets recovered at the Browns' 23 and Braelon Allen ran 22 yards for the winning score at three oh one. Andre Szmyt missed an extra point. A long kick return was surrendered in the same sequence. Braelon Allen finished with 116 rushing yards.

What held. The defense, without its top three interior linemen, sacked Geno Smith eight times and held the Jets to 276 yards. Quincy Williams had three forced fumbles, 11 tackles, a tackle for loss and a pass defensed against his former team. Maliek Collins had a sack and a half, Sam Kamara got the first of his career, Adin Huntington shared one, and Ronnie Hickman recovered the fumble Williams knocked loose off Kenyon Sadiq. The Jets were flagged ten times for 102 yards; Cleveland six times for 30.

And what got worse after the whistle. Four more names joined the list: KC Concepcion (head injury, Minkah Fitzpatrick's penalized helmet-to-helmet hit), Quinshon Judkins (left in a walking boot), Carson Schwesinger (seen in a boot after the game) and Tytus Howard (helped off in the first quarter).

Cleveland is three and two. Baltimore is in six days.

The decision he already made.

Before the six, credit the one he made on the podium.

Handed every available scapegoat, a punter who dropped a snap, a kicker who missed a point, a quarterback who took eight sacks, an opponent whose safety concussed his first-round rookie, Monken took none of them.

On the eight sacks: "It's everything. It's coaching, it's playing, it's getting the ball out of our hands, it's protection, it's all of the above. It's rarely just one person. And so we all got to take a big freaking bite of this."

On the rain: "Was that a part of it? Probably. But we both had to play in it. And that's it."

On Fitzpatrick: "Minkah's been in this league a long time. He's not a dirty player."

And the line that is really a management philosophy: "If you want the credit when we win, we got to own this when we don't. You can't be this when we lose and this when we win."

That is the decision. No scapegoat. Everything that follows gets made inside that frame.

Decision one. The running back room, which is now an emergency.

What happened. Judkins walked out of the locker room in a boot after limping through the fourth quarter. He carried 14 times for 44 yards, three point one a carry, and caught three balls for six. Backup Raheim Sanders was also hurt. Dylan Sampson is already on injured reserve after knee surgery.

What it costs. That is potentially the entire depth chart. The only healthy back with NFL carries left standing is Jaleel McLaughlin, who joined the practice squad on September 1 and has one touch this season, a twenty-eight yard touchdown.

The options. One: elevate Jaret Patterson from the practice squad, where Andrew Berry put him on October 7. Two: sign Patterson to the 53 outright, which costs a roster spot. Three: lean on McLaughlin and a heavy-personnel run game. Four: sign somebody off the street this week, which is the worst option because nobody learns an install in four days.

Our call. Elevate Patterson, and do it Tuesday rather than Saturday so he gets a full week of practice. Notice the timing, because it is the argument we made five days ago when everyone called that signing boring: we wrote that Patterson "plays if somebody in front of him gets hurt." That took 48 hours. The unglamorous move is the one that saves your Sunday, and it only works if the body is already in the building.

Decision two. Who plays receiver, and the Jerry Jeudy reversal.

What happened. Concepcion, the 24th overall pick, took a helmet-to-helmet hit from Fitzpatrick while reaching for a pass in the first quarter, stayed down, walked off under his own power, and was ruled out with a head injury. Monken afterward: "I saw him afterwards, he was in good spirits. he missed the game, so I would assume he's in concussion protocol." Tylan Wallace has already missed two straight.

What it costs. Cleveland's receiver room was Boston, Concepcion, Jeudy, Corley and Bond, with Concepcion playing 92 percent of the snaps a week ago. Take him out and one of the two rookies carrying this offense is gone.

The options. There are not many. Denzel Boston stays at 95 percent. Isaiah Bond and Malachi Corley see real snaps. And the man who gets the biggest share back is Jerry Jeudy, who nine days ago his own head coach publicly described as "a guy that really isn't starting now, he's a rotational player."

Our call. This is the week Jeudy's snap count goes back up, and it reframes the entire trade conversation we have spent two weeks on. Adam Schefter said on October 4 that Jeudy was "being squeezed out" and "could be had." Mary Kay Cabot reported no plans to trade him. A room that just lost a first-round rookie is not a room that sells a 27-year-old with a 1,200-yard season on his résumé. Watch his percentage against Baltimore. It is the cleanest read available on whether that story is alive.

Decision three. The offensive tackle spot, and why there were eight sacks.

What happened. Tytus Howard went down in the first quarter and was helped off the field. He came back. Then in the second half, Dawand Jones replaced him. Teven Jenkins missed his fifth straight game at guard. Elgton Jenkins was back at center after concussion protocol, which was the week's one genuine piece of good news.

What it costs. Eight sacks. Monken refused to isolate it, and he was right to, but the sequence is not a mystery: a tackle leaves in the first quarter, a guard has been out since Week 1, two rookies are starting, the team cannot run, "we were behind the sticks the whole day", and then you have to throw in a downpour from your own end zone.

The options. If Howard cannot go, Dawand Jones starts. If Teven Jenkins is still out, the interior stays patched. The scheme option is the real one: more quick game, more play-action off the run, fewer five-step drops behind a line missing two starters.

Our call. Monken said it himself about the run game, "it's coaching, it's scheming it, it's playing, it's doing the things our players can do well." That last clause is the answer. Against Baltimore, build the game plan around what this line can actually do, not around what it was supposed to do in August.

Decision four. The twenty-one day clock, which just got easier.

What happened. Dillon Gabriel's practice window opened October 6. He was a full participant all week and was still ruled out on Sunday. The window closes in late October.

What it costs. Activating him costs a spot on the 53-man roster. Two weeks ago that was a philosophical argument about carrying four quarterbacks. Today it is an arithmetic problem, because the spots are needed for a running back, possibly a receiver and possibly an offensive lineman.

The options. Activate him and cut somebody Cleveland needs. Keep four quarterbacks, as the Falcons, Eagles and Steelers are doing. Or let the window expire and lose him for the season.

Our call. This is why we flagged this clock two weeks before anyone else was talking about it. Monken's own words on urgency were "not really, we opened up the three-week window to kind of see where we're at." He has now seen. And an injury list with four new names on it answers the question for him: you do not spend your scarcest asset, a roster spot in October, on a third-string quarterback. We expect four quarterbacks to be the thing Cleveland cannot afford, and we would not be surprised if Gabriel's season ends on the IR designation he is currently on.

Decision five. Special teams, and what a coach actually does here.

What happened. Monken counted it himself, in order: "You miss the extra point, you give up the long kick return, defense comes up with a big stop, and then we mishandle the snap." Three disasters in one sequence of a five-point game.

What it costs. The game. Bojorquez's dropped snap handed the Jets a touchdown drive that started at the Cleveland 23. Szmyt's missed extra point meant the Jets needed only a field goal to lead rather than to tie, which changes how both coaching staffs play the last five minutes.

The options. Fire somebody. Bring in competition. Or fix the operation.

Our call. Fix the operation, and say so publicly. Szmyt was two for two on field goals in a monsoon, including from 41. One wet afternoon is not a body of work, and a coach who just told his team "it's rarely just one person" cannot then cut one person to make the point. Rep the wet-ball punt operation every day this week, Monken says they already do, and accept that the margin for a 198-yard offense is one special-teams mistake, which is an argument about the offense, not the kickers.

Decision six. The defensive tackle rotation, which just earned something.

What happened. Cleveland played without Mason Graham (sprained MCL and ankle, week-to-week), Mike Hall Junior (ankle) and Kalia Davis (quad), and the defense recorded eight sacks.

What it costs. Nothing, this week. It gained something: Sam Kamara has his first career sack, Adin Huntington has a half, and Maliek Collins played like the veteran anchor on a front with nobody else left.

The options. When Graham returns, snap out the replacements entirely, or keep a rotation that now has four or five men with real October reps.

Our call. Keep the rotation. And here is our own correction, happily made: on Friday Monken praised an "explosive as heck. a little bit undersized" young player whose name came through the transcript so badly mangled that we declined to guess. It was Adin Huntington, and 48 hours later, with the room in front of him empty, he got into the quarterback. We should have chased that name harder. The quote stands up.

Decision seven. The one nobody is asking about. Who returns punts.

What happened. This is the decision hiding behind the other six, and it is the one most likely to decide a close game in Baltimore.

Tylan Wallace, the receiver whose roster spot is the return game, has missed two straight with a knee injury. Cleveland's punt returns this season have been split between Raheim Sanders, Malachi Corley and KC Concepcion, and as of Monday, Wallace is still out, Sanders is hurt, and Concepcion has a head injury.

What it costs. Potentially everything, as Sunday just demonstrated in the other direction. A five-point game in bad weather is decided by the punt operation, and Cleveland lost one on exactly that.

The options. Put Corley back there. Put Isaiah Bond back there. Or elevate James Proche the Second, who has 68 career punt returns and 22 for Cleveland across 2023 and 2024, and whom Berry signed to the practice squad on October 6, the day before Wallace showed up as a non-participant.

Our call. Elevate Proche. That is the second time in one article that a boring practice-squad signing from last week turns out to be the answer to this week's emergency, not a coincidence, a method. And whatever else happens, do not put a receiver you cannot afford to lose back there. We said that before the Jets game, when Concepcion was taking 92 percent of the offensive snaps and still fielding punts. He is now in protocol from a different play entirely, and the principle holds anyway.

The scenario board. Three ways Sunday goes.

Since every decision above depends on information Cleveland does not have yet, here is the board as it stands.

SCENARIO A, Lamar plays, Cleveland is healthy-ish. Judkins and Concepcion clear, Graham returns, Howard starts. This is the game everybody wanted: two four and two or three and two teams, a full-strength division decider, and Cleveland's defense gets to find out whether eight sacks against Geno Smith means anything against Baltimore. Cleveland's path is the one Monken described, run the ball, stay on schedule, win the fourth quarter again.

SCENARIO B, Lamar sits, Cleveland is patched. Huntley starts for Baltimore; Cleveland is down a back, a receiver and possibly a tackle. This is the strangest version, and the one where special teams and turnovers decide it, exactly like Sunday. A Browns defense that just produced eight sacks without three interior linemen against a backup quarterback is a real edge. A Browns offense at 198 yards gives it all back.

SCENARIO C, Lamar plays, Cleveland is not close to whole. The nightmare. Baltimore's offense at full strength against a defense whose best interior rusher is week-to-week, while Cleveland tries to run with a practice-squad back behind a line missing two starters. This is the version where three and three happens and the conversation turns ugly fast.

How you will know which one you are getting: Wednesday's practice report. Not a tweet, not a rumor, the official participation list. Full, limited or did-not-participate next to Judkins, Schwesinger, Howard, Concepcion and Graham tells you which game Cleveland is actually playing.

The forty-eight hour clock.

The calendar does not care about any of this. Here is what is actually due.

Monday,Tuesday: Monken watches the tape (he said four separate times that he had not yet) and the medical staff puts real labels on Judkins, Schwesinger, Howard and Concepcion. Practice-squad elevation decisions start here.

Wednesday: the first official practice report of Baltimore week, the day you learn whether "boot" meant a precaution or a month.

Thursday,Friday: the run-game plan gets built around whoever is standing, and Concepcion's protocol progression either begins or does not.

Late October: Gabriel's twenty-one day window expires.

November 8: New Orleans at home, the last game before the deadline. November 10: the deadline. Week 11: the bye.

The one number that should drive all seven decisions.

Forget the punt snap for a second. 198 net yards.

That is the number that made every mistake fatal. A team that gains 198 yards has no margin for a dropped snap, a missed kick or a long return, because it cannot score its way out of any of them. Judkins at three point one a carry. Eight sacks. Thirteen first downs. Three straight possessions backed up inside their own end, by Monken's own description.

The pattern: Cleveland is 25th in rushing, the quarterback is at 65 percent for the season, and the offense has scored 10, 23, 21, 27 and 12. The defense has been good enough to win five games. The offense has been good enough to win three of them in the last two minutes.

That is the real decision on Monken's desk, and it is not one of the six. It is whether an offense built on fourth-quarter escapes can be rebuilt into one that leads.

Where we stop.

"The Colts beat the Browns." It was the Jets, seventeen to twelve. Cleveland has not played Indianapolis.

"Monken announced a shocking decision." He said "next man up" and took responsibility. Both real, neither shocking.

Any timeline on Judkins, Schwesinger, Howard or Concepcion. A boot is not a diagnosis and a head injury is not a schedule. Wednesday's report will say more than anybody can today.

"Cut the punter / the kicker." One wet game. Szmyt was two for two from the field.

"Fitzpatrick is dirty." Penalized, twice on that drive. Monken declined to go further and so do we.

"This loss proves they should make a trade." Monken had not watched the tape when he was asked, and we have not watched it either. One rainy five-point road loss is not an evaluation.

Baltimore, Sunday.

The Ravens come to Cleveland on October 18 with first place in the AFC North on the table, and Lamar Jackson's sprained ankle is the question hanging over it, he was ruled out of Week 5 with Tyler Huntley starting in his place.

A Cleveland team that just lost four more players, hosting a division rival that may or may not have its MVP quarterback, with the loser falling behind in October. If you wanted the week that tells you what Todd Monken's first season is actually going to be, this is it.

The bottom line.

Seven decisions, one week, and a head coach who spent Sunday night refusing to blame anybody for any of them.

The running back room may be down to a practice-squad call-up and a man signed five days ago. The receiver room lost its first-round rookie to a penalized hit, which hands snaps back to the veteran his own coach called rotational nine days ago. A tackle went down in the first quarter and eight sacks followed. A twenty-one day quarterback clock is burning against roster spots Cleveland suddenly needs elsewhere. Special teams had the worst sequence of its season. And the defense, missing its three best interior linemen, was the best unit on the field.

None of that is a crisis. All of it is a Monday, which is what Monken meant when he said "this happens to every team in the NFL. It really does. It's a marathon."

The team that handles this week well is four and two and still leading its division on Sunday night. The one that does not is three and three and explaining itself. Same roster, same rain, different Monday.
