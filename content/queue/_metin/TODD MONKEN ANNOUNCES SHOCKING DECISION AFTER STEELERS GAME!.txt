Todd Monken cut off a reporter, said "that's on me" four times, and never mentioned Shedeur Sanders once. The full press conference, line by line.
 
A head coach stopped a reporter in the middle of a question on live television, told him "I'm not done yet," said he was on a little bit of a roll, and kept talking.
 
That's not a man managing a message. That's a man who just watched his season get handed back to him after three quarters of trying to give it away, and who wasn't going to be hurried off the stage before he'd said what he came to say.
 
And he said a lot. Over roughly twenty minutes after Cleveland's twenty-one to eighteen win over Carolina, Todd Monken praised his defense, indicted his own offense, took personal blame four separate times, put his hat on backwards for his defense, told the fan base he understands the booing, blamed a reporter for making him emotional, and called his quarterback's legs a "superpower."
 
He also never said Shedeur Sanders' name. Not once.
 
A lot of people are treating that silence as the story. We're going to deal with that head-on, including the reason it's weaker evidence than it looks, and then go through the entire press conference properly, because there is more real football information in it than in anything else that's come out of Berea this season.
 
This is the Ktl Report breakdown: what he said, what it means, and the three places where the reporting around this game has the numbers wrong.
 
First, the silence.
 
Let's start where everybody wants to start.
 
Monken spent a long press conference talking about Deshaun Watson, Mason Graham, Denzel Boston, Harold Fannin Junior, his offensive staff, the fans, the penalties, a fourth-and-one, and a kickoff fumble. Shedeur Sanders' name never came up.
 
The loud reading: that's the loudest silence in Cleveland. Ten straight minutes of praise for the man ahead of him on the depth chart, and nothing for the backup. The message writes itself.
 
Here's the honest version, and it's less dramatic in both directions.
 
Reason one: nobody asked. A press conference is reactive. Coaches answer what's put in front of them, and after a come-from-behind division win the questions are about the man who threw the winning touchdown. Silence nobody prompted isn't a statement.
 
Reason two, and this is the part the "loudest silence" take leaves out entirely: Monken has been talking about Sanders. On 92.3 The Fan, he said he's been super impressed with Sanders' improvement, that he feels really good about where he's at and about his future, and that Sanders understands the team is playing the long game.
 
So what does the silence actually tell you? One real thing: the depth chart is no longer a topic the head coach feels obliged to address. After Week 1 it was the only topic. Two wins later it's off the table, genuinely meaningful, and not a secret message. Silence in a press room doesn't change the one thing that matters: if Cleveland is keeping him, it owes itself information about him. Snaps would.
 
The shape of the press conference.
 
Notice how he structured it, because the order tells you what he values.
 
He led with the defense, first sentence, first subject: they played their tails off. Then he indicted his own side of the ball. Cleveland can "certainly play a heck of a lot better on offense," the pieces are there, and "the problem is coaching and playing." Coaching first: a head coach who calls his own plays putting himself in the sentence ahead of everyone else.
 
Then the accountability, over and over. On Denzel Boston's quiet first three quarters: "That's on me. Got to throw it to him, don't you?" On the offense: "We've got to coach better, and that starts with me and our offensive staff."
 
Three press conferences in a row, Jacksonville, Tampa, Carolina, and the pattern hasn't varied once. The run game is "not a negative, it's a fact." The challenge flag was wrong. The offense isn't good enough and it starts with him. In a city that spent years listening to coaches explain why things weren't their fault, a coach who points at himself after a win has nothing to gain from it except the trust of the room.
 
The defense, and Mason Graham's arrival.
 
Now the best football story of the week, and it has almost nothing to do with quarterbacks.
 
Mason Graham's line against Carolina: seven tackles, two sacks, three tackles for loss, two quarterback hits. Per Dawgs By Nature, that gives him four sacks through three games, from the interior of the defensive line.
 
Stop and consider what that means in context. Cleveland traded Myles Garrett this offseason. You do not replace a player like that, and nobody honest pretends a rookie-contract defensive tackle is going to match him snap for snap. But the Browns didn't need to match him. They needed pressure to come from somewhere else.
 
Go back to what Monken told us the day after Tampa about this defense's identity: when an offense slides protection away and leaves a defender one-on-one, that's "the ultimate sign of disrespect." He was describing a unit built for its interior players to win those matchups.
 
Mason Graham winning a fourth-down rush and finishing with two sacks is that philosophy turning into points on a scoreboard. In Jacksonville the interior lost and the defense gave up 34. In Tampa it won more. Against Carolina it decided the game.
 
Monken also flagged a pattern only the man watching all the tape would catch. Two weeks running, the offense or special teams dug a hole and the defense refused to let it become a grave, in Tampa a failed fourth down deep in Cleveland's own territory, on Sunday a lost fumble on a kickoff return, and both times the Browns held the opponent to a field goal instead of a touchdown. That's complementary football, and it's the difference between two and one and oh and three with the same yardage. It's also a margin of almost zero: Pittsburgh turns a short field into seven.
 
And when a reporter asked when he knew Graham could be special, Monken gave an answer that doubles as this team's mission statement. He talked about how the NFL wants everything immediately, how flashes eventually turn into consistency, and then:
 
"We're going to be less flashy and more consistent."
 
Write that one down. That is the entire identity of a two and one team whose offense produces 144 passing yards and whose defense has allowed one touchdown in eight quarters. It isn't pretty. It's repeatable, which is more than most of what Cleveland has fielded in the last five years.
 
"There was no flow." The three three-and-outs.
 
Monken was asked about the second half, and his answer was the most technically useful thing in the whole press conference.
 
Cleveland opened the second half with three straight three-and-outs. Three possessions, no first downs, no clock management, and a ten to three lead handed back to a team that had been held to 113 net yards in the first half.
 
His description, nearly word for word: when you don't stack plays and you're choppy, it's hard. There was no flow. Run, pass, third down, punt. Run, run, third down, punt. And then the sentence underneath it all: getting the first down and getting it going is the whole key to being good on offense.
 
This is not coach-speak, and it's worth explaining why.
 
An NFL offense is built in sequences, not plays. The second-down call depends on what first down produced. Play-action only works once the run has been established on that drive. And tempo, running a play before the defense has settled, only exists if you're moving the chains.
 
A three-and-out doesn't just fail to score. It erases the coordinator's entire plan for that drive. Monken said it himself later: they never got to much of the game plan, because of the fumble and the three straight drives to open the second half.
 
So when you hear that this offense "stalled," understand the mechanism. The calls weren't the problem; the offense never reached the part of the script where calls start working together. That's fixable, and it starts with converting second-and-manageable, exactly where Monken said they screwed up.
 
The flag that saved the season.
 
And now the moment this game will actually be remembered for, which almost nobody is talking about honestly.
 
Carolina had gone ahead eighteen to thirteen with four twenty-five left on an eight-yard touchdown pass from Bryce Young. Cleveland started moving. And then, with two twenty-nine on the clock, Watson threw into the end zone for KC Concepcion, and Carolina intercepted it.
 
That's the game. Right there. In a stadium that had been booing for an hour, with a quarterback whose history in that building is what it is, a red zone interception inside two and a half minutes ends the afternoon.
 
Except there was a flag. A Carolina linebacker was called for roughing the passer, and the interception came off the board.
 
Ktl Report will say this plainly, because a standard only means something when it costs you something: nobody in a Browns jersey earned that. Probably a legitimate call, hits on quarterbacks get flagged in 2026, but a gift all the same. And it's the third straight week this team's result turned on a margin that had nothing to do with execution.
 
Week 2: Baker Mayfield's receiver dropped a potential game-winner in the Tampa end zone. Cleveland got a win it could easily have lost.
 
Week 3: an early throw toward Concepcion should have been intercepted and was dropped, Watson later explained the two weren't on the same page on the route, sharing the blame instead of dumping it on a rookie. Then, in the fourth quarter, an actual interception was erased by a penalty.
 
Add those up and you get the real portrait of this two and one team: competitive, resilient, and living on a knife's edge. The resilience is real, you still have to go score after the flag, and Cleveland did. But a team banking on bounces eventually gets the other kind.
 
Knocked down, and "I'm good, I'm good."
 
Then came the moment that made the press conference.
 
Somewhere in the middle of the go-ahead drive, an eight-play, 55-yard march, Watson took a hit and had to come off. He got up slowly. Trainers came out. The staff was being told he had to leave the field.
 
And Monken, who was in the middle of calling the next play, described what happened next: Watson was jabbing at him on the sideline saying "I'm good, I'm good." Monken said he didn't know at first whether Watson would be able to go back in, and then delivered the funniest line of the day, that by that point there's nothing you can do about it anyway, and that it's amazing how many people want to talk to you while plays are going on.
 
Watson went back in. And then he delivered.
 
Here's Monken's full assessment, as reported by ESPN's Daniel Oyefusi: Watson probably didn't play as well as he wanted, but to get knocked down, then to come back in and make a play, and then the two-point conversion, this is about him.
 
One housekeeping note: these quotes, the knocked-down story, the two-point conversion, the "superpower" line, are about Deshaun Watson. A breakdown circulated this week attributing all of it to Shedeur Sanders, whose entire afternoon was one pass for one yard. Oyefusi's reporting confirms the attribution; we flagged it before the receipt arrived.
 
And the moment deserves its full weight with the right name on it. This is a man 22 months removed from real football, with two Achilles surgeries on the same leg, who had been booed by his own crowd an hour earlier, taking a fourth-quarter hit and arguing with his head coach to get back on the field.
 
Whatever anyone thinks about the contract, the summer, or the depth chart, that is what competing looks like.
 
The real stat line, and a correction.
 
Now the numbers, with a fix that's been circulating all week.
 
Watson finished 16 of 30 for 144 yards, two touchdowns and no interceptions, plus 11 carries for 45 yards.
 
You'll see "16 of 34" in a lot of places. The arithmetic settles it: his yards-per-attempt was reported at 4.8, and 144 divided by 30 is exactly 4.8. Thirty attempts. Two of the breakdowns floating around this week also printed the final score as twenty-one to eight instead of twenty-one to eighteen, and Tampa's score as twenty-three to nine instead of twenty-three to nineteen. Small things, but if a number is going to carry an argument, the number has to be right.
 
So what does 144 passing yards mean? It means this offense did not function as a passing attack. A quarterback can produce 144 in a half of garbage time; Cleveland needed sixty minutes, while leading at halftime, with a receiver room it spent two early picks rebuilding. And both touchdowns came inside a five-minute window, very good for five minutes, inconsistent for the other fifty-five.
 
Which, notably, is nearly exactly what Monken himself said. He didn't claim the passing game was fine. He said the run game was better and the pass game was not, that they have a ways to go, and that they haven't played their best football on offense yet.
 
That's not the language of a man who thinks he's fixed anything. It's the language of a man who knows exactly how fragile two and one is.
 
"One of his superpowers."
 
Asked about Watson using his legs, Monken used the word superpower, and then immediately said something more revealing.
 
He said that as Watson continues to learn the system, gets his eyes right and gets his drops right, all of which he's still working on, the staff will build the offense better around him.
 
Read that carefully, because it's two messages in one sentence.
 
Message one: the mobility is real and it's now a designed part of the plan. Eleven carries for 45 yards, including a 21-yard run late. And per the Dawgs By Nature breakdown, the effect goes beyond the yardage: defenses now have to assign a spy to Watson, which pulls a defender out of coverage. On Fannin's first touchdown, a long over route, the spy on Watson took a defender out of the play.
 
That's a schematic advantage, and the most encouraging thing about this offense: a quarterback who must be accounted for as a runner makes every underneath route easier for everyone else.
 
Message two, delivered gently: the passing fundamentals aren't there yet. Eyes. Drops. A coach saying that about his starter in Week 3 is being honest, not disloyal, footwork is the first thing to go after two years away and the last thing to come back.
 
And the part we'll keep saying every time we praise the legs: eleven carries a game across a full season is roughly 180 hits on a body that has already broken down twice. The superpower and the risk are the same play, and Cleveland's insurance behind him is a quarterback who has thrown one pass all year.
 
"When you don't, you don't."
 
The most emotionally interesting stretch of the press conference was about the fans.
 
Monken got genuinely fired up. He talked about how Browns fans want to be part of something special. He said play tough, play physical. And then the line of the day:
 
"The better we play, the more we deserve to be cheered. When you don't, you don't. That's what we live by."
 
That is a head coach publicly accepting the boos. Not complaining about them. Not calling them unfair. Treating them as a verdict the team earns or doesn't, week to week.
 
There's a contrast worth handling carefully. In the preseason Watson said the booing felt personal and brought up fans cheering his 2024 injury, and Ktl Report defended him on that in August and still does. But the two responses differ in kind, and Monken's is the one that buys a franchise room: "we owe you a better product" can't be argued with.
 
And the broader context makes it sharper. Owner Jimmy Haslam, at the league meetings in March 2025, described the Watson acquisition as a "big swing and miss", saying they thought they had the quarterback and they didn't. The ownership of this team has already publicly conceded the error. That's the backdrop every boo in that stadium is sitting on.
 
Then, late in the press conference, Monken lost the thread of his own good intentions in the best possible way. He said what fans want is to see the team score a damn touchdown and not commit stupid penalties, and then, catching himself: I said I was going to be positive. You got me going, Scott. It's your fault.
 
Ten penalties will do that to a man. You could hear the weight of the franchise coming out of him in bursts, which is probably the most human thing a Cleveland head coach has done at a podium in years.
 
Where we stop.
 
And for the record, the full state of that room: Cabot reported no plans to trade Sanders or Gabriel before November 10; Jeremy Fowler said Cleveland is one of three teams with four quarterbacks that would welcome a deal at the right price for one of its backups, naming nobody; Zac Jackson of The Athletic called Watson's chances of being here next season, and of still starting at the Week 11 bye, slim. Nothing in that changes our month-old position: if the Browns are keeping him, they have to learn something about him.
 
A few things attached to this one that Ktl Report won't carry.
 
"The loudest silence in Cleveland." It's a great line and it's thin evidence. Nobody asked, and the same coach praised Sanders on the radio in the same news cycle. Reading a message into an absent question is the same move as reading a mutiny into "above my pay grade."
 
"That's classic quarterback protection." Maybe, and it's a reasonable read, clearly labeled as opinion by the person who said it, which we respect. But it's a claim about motive. The simpler explanation is that a coach who calls the plays believes the play-calling was part of the problem, which is also what he said after Jacksonville and after Tampa, when there was no quarterback to protect.
 
And the numbers. 16 of 30, not 16 of 34. twenty-one to eighteen, not twenty-one to eight. twenty-three to nineteen in Tampa, not twenty-three to nine. If the facts are the foundation of the argument, check the facts.
 
Thursday night.
 
Pittsburgh, in Cleveland, Thursday, with first place in the AFC North on the line. Both teams two and one. Four days of rest. And Aaron Rodgers at 42 on the other sideline, a reminder, for a 31-year-old quarterback who said this week that he wants to play another twelve years, that the runway exists and is earned one year at a time.
 
What decides it: the penalties, ten is unsurvivable against a disciplined division opponent. Second-and-manageable, where the whole "no flow" problem starts. Whether 4.8 yards per attempt survives a better front than Carolina's. And whether the interior keeps winning, with Mason Graham at four sacks in three games, Cleveland can win this kind of game scoring 17.
 
The bottom line.
 
Here's the Ktl Report read on the whole press conference.
 
Monken's accountability is the most consistent thing about this team. Three press conferences, three times putting coaching first, four separate "that's on me" moments in one night. After a win.
 
The defense is the identity, and Mason Graham, seven tackles, two sacks, three tackles for loss, four sacks in three games, is becoming the answer to the Garrett trade that nobody expected this fast.
 
"Less flashy, more consistent" is the mission statement, and it's the right one for a two and one team with 144 passing yards.
 
The flag was a gift, and the margins have gone Cleveland's way three weeks running, resilience and luck sharing a seat.
 
Watson earned the moment, knocked down, back in, 14-yard touchdown, two-point conversion, and his own coach said the performance still wasn't what he wanted. Both are true.
 
And the silence about Shedeur Sanders isn't a message. The real story is that nothing about his situation has changed: no trade before November 10, no snaps, no information.
