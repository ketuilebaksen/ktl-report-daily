The design flaw. Monken just explained why his own defense keeps getting trapped, and why that is a trade-off, not a failure.

First, the title. There is no announcement. There is something better.

The title is "Todd Monken Huge Announcement After The Browns' Humiliating Loss To The Steelers."

No announcement was made. Nothing was declared, nobody was benched, no roster move was revealed. Eleven minutes of postgame questions produced zero news of that kind, and we will say so first as always here at Ktl Report.

"Humiliating," on the other hand, is a word Monken himself would not fight you over. His opening sentence was: "Well, we certainly earned that." Later: "that was just one piece of the humble pie we got to eat." And on the sack total: "I'm sick that we have that."

So the headline is half honest, which is better than most weeks. The problem is that it promises a declaration and delivers something far more useful: a head coach explaining, in public, a structural trade-off inside his own defense.

That answer is the best football anybody in Cleveland has said out loud this season, and almost nobody will clip it, because it does not fit in a thumbnail. So that is what this article is about.

The answer that explains the whole defense.

A reporter asked a very good question: what is it about the way you play defense that lends itself to those schematic opportunities?

Monken's answer, in full:

"Well, I think when you're the attack style front, I think as you work to, you're going to get schemed runs. You are going to get your trap plays, your wham plays, you're going to get the edge plays, try to pin and get to the edge. And there's certain things you've got to do to combat that. With your ability to move the front, with your ability to pressure off edges, with your ability to get over blocks."

Read that twice, because it is a coach telling you that the thing that makes his defense good is the same thing that makes it vulnerable. That is not an excuse. It is a design brief.

What a trap and a wham actually are.

Let us teach it properly, because the words get thrown around and almost never explained.

An attacking front asks its defensive linemen to penetrate, to get upfield fast and disrupt, rather than hold a gap and wait. That is how you generate sacks and tackles behind the line. It is also why Cleveland has twenty sacks through eight games, top six in football, with eleven different players recording at least one.

A trap weaponises that. The offense deliberately leaves a defensive lineman unblocked. He surges upfield, pleased with himself, into a space the design has already conceded, and a guard pulls across the formation to hit him from the side, in the ear hole, while the ball runs through the hole he just vacated. The faster he gets upfield, the bigger the hole.

A wham is the same idea with a tight end or fullback as the hammer instead of a pulling guard.

So the cruelty is exact: the more aggressively a lineman attacks, the more violently a trap punishes him. You cannot coach "penetrate hard" and "stay home" into the same player on the same snap.

And that is precisely what happened on the play Monken named without being asked.

Exhibit A. Fifty-one yards on third and thirteen.

Monken brought it up himself: "one of them was a fifty-yard run or something on third and thirteen, you know, a trap play... a good call by them on third and thirteen."

The Browns' own game story confirms it. Jaylen Warren's fifty-one-yard run came on third-and-thirteen, on a trap.

Think about the situation. Third and thirteen is a passing down. Cleveland's front is in its most aggressive posture, ears pinned, rushing the quarterback, which is exactly when an attacking lineman is least interested in staying square. Pittsburgh called a run. The trap found the man who was already gone, and Warren went fifty-one yards.

Monken gave Pittsburgh the credit without being prompted: "a good call by them." He is right. On third and thirteen that call is close to free money against this front, and Pittsburgh knew it.

The two sides of the same ledger.

Here is the trade-off, with both columns, because a trade-off with only one column is just a complaint.

What the attacking front has bought Cleveland: twenty sacks through eight games, sixth in the league, spread across eleven players. Two hundred one passing yards allowed per game, seventh. Twenty point nine points allowed per game, twelfth. On Sunday it held Aaron Rodgers to thirteen of twenty-five for one hundred seventy-two yards, got him pulled for Mason Rudolph, and Denzel Ward took his third interception of the season. Through six games it had allowed one rushing touchdown.

What it has cost: Tony Pollard ran twenty-five times for one hundred twenty-nine yards and three touchdowns a week ago. On Sunday Pittsburgh put up two hundred net rushing yards, three rushing touchdowns and fourteen rushing first downs, including one hundred seventeen net rushing yards in the first quarter alone. Warren finished with one hundred forty-three on seventeen carries, tying his career high. Rico Dowdle, back from the knee injury that kept him out of the first meeting, added fifty-six and a touchdown.

Same scheme. Both columns. The pass defense is seventh in the league and the run defense is the hole, and they are the same decision.

And note what Monken said about the space rather than the effort: "there was a lot of space... it's not that our guys weren't running the ball. I mean our guys will run to the ball. They play hard, but there was a lot of space, and when there's a lot of space with those backs, those two backs that run hard downhill, you're going to have that."

That is a coach distinguishing between effort and structure. The effort was there. The structure gave up the space.

And the three counters he named.

This is the part that makes the answer useful rather than fatalistic, and it is why we think the segment deserved a whole video rather than a clip.

Monken named three specific fixes, and they are real coaching solutions rather than platitudes:

One: "your ability to move the front." Stop lining up in the same place. If the front shifts late, the pulling guard does not know who to trap.

Two: "your ability to pressure off edges." Bring the heat from outside rather than straight up the middle, so the interior linemen are not the only engine and do not have to over-commit.

Three: "your ability to get over blocks." Teach the technique, hands, leverage, flattening, so that a trapped lineman survives the block instead of being removed by it.

None of those require a trade. All three are available to defensive coordinator Mike Rutenberg this week, and the fourth option, asking an attacking front to stop attacking, is the one that would cost Cleveland its twenty sacks and its seventh-ranked pass defense.

That is the actual decision in front of this defense, and it is a lot more interesting than "they need another edge rusher."

But the other number is not a trade-off.

Now the part where we will not let the structural argument do work it cannot do.

The run defense is a scheme trade-off. The sacks are not.

Deshaun Watson was sacked eight times for forty-one yards. His season total is now thirty-three in eight games, still the most in the National Football League. Seven different Steelers got home: T.J. Watt two, which gives him twenty and a half career sacks against Cleveland, the most by any player in NFL history against the Browns, Patrick Queen one and a half with twelve tackles and a forced fumble, Cameron Heyward one and a half, Nick Herbig one, Kevin Jobity Junior one for the first of his career, Keeanu Benton a half, Alex Highsmith a half.

Cleveland finished with thirty-four net rushing yards and went four of thirteen on third down.

And Monken, asked about protection, did something worth noticing: he put himself first, in order.

"Certainly got to call it better. Certainly got to scheme it better. Got to protect better. Got to work to get our guys open."

Call it. Scheme it. Then protect it. Two of those four are his job and he listed them before the players'. Left guard Zion Johnson said the same from the other direction: "At the end of the day, it's on us."

There is no design brief that explains thirty-three sacks in eight games. That is the thing we have said is item one on this roster's shopping list since Thursday, and nothing on Sunday changed it.

The youth answer, which is the best thing he has said all season.

A reporter offered him the softest possible exit: does the youth of your team factor into a loss in that environment?

"Oh, I think that's just an excuse. That's an excuse. Do we have a young team? Of course we have a young team. Does that exonerate ourselves from two turnovers the first two drives and keeping our poise and playing at a high level? No, of course not. Otherwise what were the other five wins, right? Were we going to take the ones that we win and say it's not the youth, and then when we lose it's the youth? That's a freaking excuse."

That is not just refusing an alibi. That is a logical argument, and a tight one: an explanation that only appears after losses is not an explanation, it is a convenience. If youth explains the twenty-one-point loss, it has to explain the five wins too, and it cannot, so it explains neither.

We have written in these pages that Monken keeps declining excuses that are handed to him. This is the cleanest example yet, and he built the reasoning himself rather than just saying no.

He said something similar about practice, by the way, and it cuts the same way: "We had a great week of practice leading up to Jacksonville... We've had eight great weeks of practice. We had a great training camp. We had a great offseason. We just didn't play well today." Cleveland lost Week one to Jacksonville thirty-four to ten. Good practice is not a receipt.

Everything starts with me.

Asked whether he considered taking Watson out late, Monken said no and explained why: "I want to see him play. I want to see him continue to play and battle." Then he kept going, unprompted:

"There's things that come up in the game and you're like, man, I could have done it better as a coach. I mean, that's, who are you if you don't say that? Who you are as a man, if you just say no. Starts with me. Everything starts with me. And we can certainly be better how we do it offensively, and then how we get our team prepared as a total team in terms of our poise."

For a coach whose team had just been beaten by twenty-one in a division game, that is a straight answer. He blamed the call sheet before the line, the preparation before the players, and the poise of the team before anybody's individual effort.

And his sign-off is now a standing promise: "We're going to get on the bus. We're going to look at that tape and we're going to own it and we're going to fight like hell to fix it. You're going to get tired of me saying that as long as I'm the head coach here."

And the Verse answer, which is a rebuke in a suit.

The last substantive question was about Jared Verse's week of talk, he spent the build-up saying he was not worried about anybody on Pittsburgh's roster.

Monken's answer: "I can't speak for them. I can only say that our guys fight. They compete. But certainly, as we all know ultimately, whether it's me or our team, it's not what you say, it's what you do and how you play. And that's in life. That's not a Jared Verse thing. That's everything. It's what we do, not what we say. And we did not do the things today that give you a chance to win."

That is a rebuke delivered without naming a crime. He did not disown his player, did not endorse the talk, and used it to make a point about the whole team.

The scoreboard argument is harsher than anything Monken said. Verse finished with one tackle and still has no sacks as a Brown. To his credit he did not hide afterwards: "We didn't do enough on our end to come away with a win. That's completely on us, offense, defense, special teams. Nobody did enough." And he did not retreat either: "I stand by everything I say."

We will leave that there. A man is allowed to mean what he said; the tape just gets a vote too.

The failure checklist, in his own order.

Monken's opening statement was a list, and it is worth preserving because the order is his:

Didn't start fast. Two early turnovers. Then a three-and-out. Never got the run game going. Couldn't stop the run early. Gave up a touchdown right before the half. Three-and-out twice to start the second half. Penalties. The sacks. "Didn't coach well enough, didn't play well enough."

Every item checks out. Fumbles on each of the first two drives, a three-and-out on the third, Pat Freiermuth's one-yard sneak with one second left in the half, three-and-outs opening the second half, three punts and two turnovers on downs after the break.

One wrinkle reflects well on him: Monken said ten penalties. The Browns' own game story lists seven enforced, three pre-snap, three post-snap and one taunting. He over-counted against his own team, which is the opposite direction from how most coaches misremember a Sunday. Penalties also turned two of three second-quarter drives into field goals instead of touchdowns, which is where the six points came from, and sorting that kind of thing out is most of the job at Ktl Report.

Two things that deserve better than this scoreboard.

Harold Fannin Junior had ten catches for one hundred fifty-four yards, the best day of his career, in a game where the offense totalled two hundred eighty-five net passing yards. Nearly half the passing output went to the tight end.

Andre Szmyt made both his kicks, from thirty-two and forty-nine, and those six points were Cleveland's entire total. He is sixteen of eighteen this season with three game-winners, and has now made twenty-five consecutive field goals dating back to twenty twenty-five, the third-longest streak in Browns history. Monken called him one of the best kickers in the league; on the evidence that is not flattery.

And Carson Schwesinger is playing hurt. Asked whether the ankle is affecting him: "I think he's a tough sob, man. He just likes to play. There's a couple weeks ago I didn't think he played. He's down, it's the other ankle. He's an absolute stud. Absolute warrior." Both ankles, in other words, and he keeps dressing.

Eight days, and New Orleans first.

Cleveland is five and three. Pittsburgh is six and two and has the division. Monken put it plainly, twice: "They've earned being six and two. We've earned being five and three."

Next: at New Orleans, Sunday November eighth, one o'clock, against a two-and-five team, two days before the trade deadline closes at four o'clock on Tuesday, November tenth.

And the line that should frame the whole week came when Monken was asked how much Sunday resembled the Tennessee run-defense problem: "You're going to see the similarities each week until you get it fixed, right? It's not just defense, it's offense, it's everywhere. You're going to see the same stuff till you get it fixed."

That is the honest version of a mid-season diagnosis. Two weeks, two games where the run defense leaked and the protection failed. The scheme explains part of one of those. Nothing explains the other.

The names and numbers to fix.

"Huge announcement", there wasn't one. If you want the real headline, it is the attack-front answer or the youth answer.
"Jerry versus" is Jared Verse. "Deshan" is Deshaun. "Carson" is Carson Schwesinger. The penalty count, Monken said ten; the team counted seven enforced. Say both, and say who said which.
The fifty-yard run was fifty-one yards, on third and thirteen, on a trap. Use the down and distance; it is the whole point.
Cleveland is five and three; Pittsburgh is six and two. The transcript has Monken saying both "five and three" and, once, "five and two", he corrected himself; use five and three.

What we are not carrying.

We are not carrying "announcement." None was made.

We are not carrying youth as an explanation, because the head coach dismantled it better than we could.

We are not carrying the scheme argument as a defence of the sacks. Thirty-three in eight games is not a trade-off, it is a problem.

We are not carrying blame aimed at Tytus Howard, who cleared a concussion protocol on Saturday without practising all week and played anyway, or at any single lineman. Seven different Steelers recorded a sack.

And we are not carrying panic. Five and three with eight games left, a kicker on a twenty-five-kick streak, a tight end having a breakout year and a defense ranked seventh against the pass is not a crisis. It is a team with one enormous, specific, fixable problem and eight days to decide whether to buy a solution.

Four titles you can actually use.

One. "MONKEN EXPLAINS THE TRAP: WHY AN ATTACKING FRONT GIVES UP FIFTY-ONE YARDS ON THIRD AND THIRTEEN"
Two. "'THAT'S A FREAKING EXCUSE', MONKEN DESTROYS THE YOUTH ARGUMENT"
Three. "HE SAID TEN PENALTIES, THE BOX SCORE SAYS SEVEN: MONKEN COUNTED AGAINST HIMSELF"
Four. "THIRTY-THREE SACKS IN EIGHT GAMES, THE ONE NUMBER NO SCHEME EXPLAINS"

Your turn.

Monken named three counters to the trap: move the front, pressure off the edges, get over blocks. There is a fourth he did not name, tell the front to stop attacking, and give back the twenty sacks and the seventh-ranked pass defense.

Which do you want? Keep the aggressive front and accept that good teams will trap you for fifty-one yards on third and thirteen, or dial it back and win uglier. You cannot have both, and that is the actual question facing Mike Rutenberg this week.

And the deadline one, with eight days left: thirty-three sacks allowed in eight games. Do you spend a pick on a lineman now, or trust the fix is coaching? We have said buy since Thursday. Make the other case.

The bottom line.

There was no announcement. There was a head coach who opened with "we certainly earned that," called the loss humble pie, said he was sick about the sacks, listed his own failures before his players', and then, asked a smart question about his defense, explained the structural trade-off at the heart of it better than any analyst has managed all season.

An attacking front buys you twenty sacks and the seventh-best pass defense in football. It also hands a well-coached opponent a trap on third and thirteen worth fifty-one yards, and two hundred rushing yards on an afternoon when one hundred seventeen of them came in the first quarter. Both columns, same decision, and three named counters that cost nothing but coaching.

The sacks are different. Thirty-three in eight games has no design brief behind it, and eight days before the deadline that is the only item on the list that money can touch.

New Orleans on Sunday, the deadline on Tuesday. Here at Ktl Report we would rather bring you the eleven minutes where a coach told the truth about his own scheme than a thumbnail promising an announcement nobody made.
