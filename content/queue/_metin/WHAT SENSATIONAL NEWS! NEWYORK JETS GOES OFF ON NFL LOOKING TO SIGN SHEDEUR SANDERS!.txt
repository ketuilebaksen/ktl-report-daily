Six teams, one fifth round pick. What the Shedeur Sanders market actually looks like from the buyers' side.

The phone should be ringing. So why is the price still a fifth?

Here is a question that sounds like it has an obvious answer and does not.

Six NFL teams currently have a defensible reason to call Cleveland about Shedeur Sanders. The Jets are one and three and their offense has gone quiet. Miami is oh and four and its quarterback just went 9 of 17. Tampa Bay lost Baker Mayfield to a dislocated thumb. Arizona has a brand-new head coach and an unsettled quarterback room. Tennessee is still looking for its first win with the player it drafted first overall. Washington has been one elbow away from chaos for two seasons.

That is six buyers. And the price being floated for Sanders, everywhere, by everyone, is a fifth-round pick. Maybe a fifth and a sixth if somebody gets competitive.

Six interested teams and a fifth-round price is not a contradiction. It is the most instructive fact in this whole saga, and it proves the argument we have been making at Ktl Report for a month, from the other direction. So: team by team, with real numbers, and then who Shedeur Sanders is actually competing against on a general manager's whiteboard. It is not Geno Smith. It is the 2027 draft.

First, a receipt. The file everyone is quoting is from July.

Before any of this, a correction that matters more than any of the individual team notes.

The team-by-team breakdown circulating this week, the one being read aloud on shows as current intelligence, with specific pick packages attached, traces back to a FanSided piece published July 18, 2026. That is before the season started. Three months ago.

And you can tell, because its roster assumptions are already dead. It lists Quinn Ewers as Miami's number two, Jake Browning as Tampa Bay's backup, and Gardner Minshew in Arizona's room. Its Arizona proposal is not a draft pick at all, it is a player swap sending Jacoby Brissett to Cleveland, which is an odd thing to suggest about a man who is currently Arizona's starting quarterback.

None of that makes the piece bad; it was a reasonable July exercise. It makes it July. When a show reads a preseason trade-packages article over October highlights and calls it "the file," the audience is handed speculation with the timestamp removed. We will use current facts, and where we estimate, we will say so.

What is actually being reported today.

Here is the live, named, dated reporting, all of it.

Tony Grossi, ESPN Cleveland, on October 6: asked whether there is a market, he said "Absolutely. Okay, first of all, there's gotta be a demand." He described the quarterback market as being in a lull with teams still evaluating. And then, in the same conversation: "I don't expect him to be traded." He said no quarterback trade appears close anywhere in the league.

Jeremy Fowler, ESPN: Cleveland would consider moving one of its two young quarterbacks for the right offer, trading Sanders would require genuine comfort in Dillon Gabriel as the primary backup, and he senses no movement.

Mary Kay Cabot, cleveland.com: the Browns have no plans to trade either quarterback before the November 10 deadline.

Read those together and the picture is consistent rather than contradictory. Demand exists. Momentum does not. Those are two different things, and almost every viral version of this story collapses them into one.

The Jets. The loudest case, the weakest fit.

Because this is the team Cleveland plays on Sunday, let's start here, and let's be honest, because the loudest version of this argument is also the shakiest.

The Jets are one and three. They lost in Chicago in Week 4, a game in which Geno Smith finished with eight completions. Head coach Aaron Glenn's postgame assessment was blunt: "Very, very disappointing. Very disappointing on a number of different levels. basically just not playing complementary football at its best." He also pointed at the penalties that wiped out takeaways his defense had earned.

So the frustration is real and on the record. But look at the position the front office is in. This regime chose Geno Smith, hired Glenn, and brought in Frank Reich to run the offense. General manager Darren Mougey, Mougey, not the "Muji" the transcripts keep producing, is already absorbing public criticism for the one and three start.

A general manager under fire in October does not trade for the most scrutinized young quarterback in football. That move does not quietly fix an offense; it becomes the only story in New York for three months. Teams make desperate trades in October. They rarely make loud ones. The Jets have the most noise around this idea and the least institutional reason to do it.

Miami. The most desperate, and therefore the cheapest.

Miami is the team whose situation genuinely fits, and that is exactly why Cleveland should be unexcited.

The Dolphins are oh and four after a fifteen to ten loss to Minnesota. Tua Tagovailoa is no longer there, he is in Atlanta. The offense belongs to Malik Willis, who against the Vikings went 9 of 17 for 85 yards with a sixty-seven point oh passer rating, lost a fumble, and had a sixty-eight yard touchdown reversed. And they are now without De'Von Achane, Achane, not the two different mangled spellings in the transcript, for the season, which removes the one player who made that offense frightening.

Note also that the video cited Willis at both "50 percent" and "fifty-six point three percent" within a few minutes, and his rating as 74, numbers stitched together from different weeks. The Minnesota line above is the verified one.

So Miami has the need, the cap room, and a reason to gamble cheaply. Here is the problem for Cleveland: a oh and four team is a cheap buyer, not a motivated one. It is already thinking about April, shopping the clearance rack while it works out whether it will have a new head coach and a top-five pick. Desperation looks like leverage from outside. From the selling side it is the opposite.

Tampa Bay. A three week rental.

Tampa is the only interested team with a roster built to win now, which makes it the most interesting call and the least valuable trade.

Baker Mayfield dislocated his thumb. Head coach Todd Bowles, Bowles, which the transcript rendered as "Todd BS", said he will miss at least three weeks, and ESPN reported his return hinges on when he can grip a football again. In the meantime the Buccaneers are starting Jalon Daniels, an undrafted rookie. Not "Jaylen," and not to be confused with Washington's Jayden Daniels, which is a collision of names this story cannot afford.

A contending team does not want to throw away a month of its season, and that is a real motive. But think about what Tampa is buying: three or four weeks of bridge quarterback, after which Mayfield returns and the acquired player goes back to the clipboard. Nobody pays a meaningful price for that, and you do not trade a 24-year-old on a cheap deal through 2028 to solve somebody else's three week problem.

Arizona. New coach, old question.

The transcript got something right here that we want to credit rather than correct, because it is counterintuitive.

Mike LaFleur really is the Arizona Cardinals' head coach. The Cardinals fired Jonathan Gannon in May and hired LaFleur away from the Rams, where he was the offensive coordinator. So when the quote about the passing game "not throwing the ball for crap" and the scheme not being good gets attributed to Arizona's head coach, that attribution is correct, LaFleur is the one evaluating that offense, and he is evaluating an offense he was hired to fix.

Arizona moved on from Kyler Murray and is starting Jacoby Brissett, Brissett, not "Brassette", with rookie Carson Beck waiting. They lost to the Giants in Week 4, and there are already public calls to turn to Beck.

Which is precisely why Arizona is not a buyer at any real price. A first-year head coach with a rookie quarterback already on the roster does not spend assets on a second one. He plays the one his front office drafted, because that is the evaluation his job depends on finishing.

Tennessee. The one team that structurally cannot.

Tennessee gets mentioned constantly in these lists, and it is the clearest "no" of the group.

The Titans selected Cam Ward first overall in 2025. He has had, by the reporting's own framing, a historically rough start, and the honest version of that framing is more interesting than "bust." The comparisons being drawn in print are JaMarcus Russell, who went seven and eighteen across his first 25 games, and Trevor Lawrence, who went five and twenty in his first 25 starts and now leads a winning Jacksonville team. That is the actual range of outcomes for a struggling early-career number one pick: catastrophe, or a guy who figures it out and takes a franchise with him.

And that is exactly why Tennessee will not trade for a quarterback this fall. When you spend the first pick in a draft on a quarterback, the organizational obligation is to finish the evaluation. Acquiring a competitor in year two does not clarify anything; it muddies the only data point the franchise actually needs. The local coverage has it right: the rest of 2026 in Tennessee is about getting a full read on Ward.

Washington. Insurance, not a solution.

Washington is the case we have covered more than any other in this series, so briefly: with Jayden Daniels having now had three separate elbow episodes across two seasons, the argument for a young backup with starting experience is the most rational of the six. It is also the smallest. Insurance is worth a late pick, not a premium, and a team with playoff ambitions buying insurance is not going to bid against anybody.

And the jersey argument is still wrong.

One claim in this transcript needs stepping on again, because it will not die: "you'd sell a whole bunch of jerseys and merch."

No NFL team acquires a quarterback for merchandise money, because the money does not work that way. League-wide merchandise revenue is shared among all 32 clubs, and group licensing revenue from jersey sales flows to the players through the NFLPA, not to the club that employs them. A team trading for a popular player does not get a bigger merchandise check. That is the fourth time we have explained it in this series, and we will keep explaining it, because it is the most common piece of fake front-office logic in this whole conversation. Teams trade for quarterbacks because they think he will win them games. That is it.

The part nobody says. His real competition is the 2027 draft.

Here is the thing that explains the fifth-round price better than anything else, and we have not heard a single show say it.

When a general manager considers acquiring Shedeur Sanders in October 2026, he is not comparing him to Geno Smith or Malik Willis. He is comparing him to what a draft pick buys in April.

A top-of-the-draft quarterback comes with a full college season of film, a combine, a medical, interviews, and a five-year contract. A trade for Sanders comes with one pass for one yard in 2026 and seven starts from last season. The buyer is being asked to pay for projection in October, when he could pay for evidence in April, and most of the teams on this list will be picking high enough to have that choice.

That is the whole market in one paragraph. His price is not low because the league dislikes him. His price is low because nobody can see him. The only entity that can change that is the Cleveland Browns, and they have given him one snap.

Which is why we keep landing in the same place: three to five designed snaps in neutral situations. Not a benching, a package. It is the only mechanism that converts projection into evidence before November 10, and evidence is the only thing that turns a fifth into a third.

The precedent shelf. What young quarterbacks actually sell for.

If you think a fifth sounds insulting, look at what the last decade of these trades has actually returned. Every one of these quarterbacks was moved while still on his rookie contract, and every one was a first-round pick, a pedigree Shedeur Sanders, taken in the fifth round, does not have.

J.J. McCarthy (number ten, 2024), Minnesota to the Giants: a 2027 fifth.
Baker Mayfield (number one, 2018), Cleveland to Carolina: a conditional fifth.
Justin Fields (number eleven, 2021), Chicago to Pittsburgh: a conditional sixth.
Mac Jones (number fifteen, 2021), New England to Jacksonville: a sixth.
Blaine Gabbert (number ten, 2011), Jacksonville to San Francisco: a sixth.
Zach Wilson (number two, 2021), the Jets to Denver: effectively a pick swap, New York took a seventh back for a sixth.
Trey Lance (number three, 2021), San Francisco to Dallas: a fourth.
Kenny Pickett (number twenty, 2022), Pittsburgh to Philadelphia: a third plus late picks.

Two sold higher. Josh Rosen (number ten, 2018) brought a second and a fifth from Miami, and Sam Darnold (number three, 2018) brought a sixth plus a second and a fourth from Carolina. Notice what those two have in common: both were offseason trades of recent first-round picks acquired to start immediately, before anything had been disproven. The cheap ones were all backups, or players moved after being benched.

That is the entire pricing rule, and it has nothing to do with talent. The market pays for a starter in March and a backup in October. Rosen's buyers paid a second and got one season; Darnold went for late picks and won a Super Bowl in Seattle. Draft compensation is a statement about role and timing, not about who the player turns out to be.

Sanders is being discussed in October, as a backup, from the fifth round. On this shelf that is the bottom half, and the only thing that moves him up it is being allowed to look like a starter first.

Why six suitors is not an auction.

One more piece of market logic, because "multiple teams are interested" is being sold as leverage and it is not.

An auction requires bidders all chasing the same thing at the same time. Look at what these six want. Miami wants a lottery ticket at a discount. Tampa wants a three week bridge. Arizona and Tennessee need to finish evaluating quarterbacks they already own. Washington wants insurance. The Jets want a story they can survive.

Those are five different transactions, and not one is "we must have this player, name your price." Worse, several of those franchises may be hiring new decision-makers in January, and nobody spends real draft capital in October on a player the next regime might not want.

A long list of suitors with different motives and no urgency produces a floor, not a ceiling. Cleveland should read that list as proof that there is no rush at all, which happens to be exactly what Fowler, Cabot and Grossi are all describing.

Where we stop.

"They are not giving him a chance in Cleveland." He is the listed number two behind a quarterback who just led three consecutive game-winning drives, the first Brown to do that since Brian Sipe in 1979. "He is not getting snaps" is true and we have said it for a month. "He has not been given a chance" skips the reason, and the reason is that the starter is playing well.

"The Jets/Dolphins/Bucs are pursuing him." No reporter has written that any of them has called Cleveland. These are analysts and fans building cases, including a July article read as October news.

"A fifth or sixth is the price." An estimate, ours included. No offer has been reported by anyone.

"Cam Ward is a bust." Twenty starts is not a career, and the reporting's own comparison set includes Trevor Lawrence, who was five and twenty and is now good.

Anything about Baker Mayfield's or De'Von Achane's recovery beyond what was reported. A dislocated thumb, a three week timeline and a season-ending injury are facts. The rest is guessing.

Any trade prediction. Grossi: "I don't expect him to be traded." Fowler senses no movement. Cabot reports no plans. We will not be braver than the three best-sourced people on this beat.

And on Sunday, he plays at one of them.

The schedule has a sense of humor. Cleveland is at the New York Jets on Sunday, October 11, one o'clock Eastern on CBS, the team whose fan base spent this week arguing loudest for a trade, watching from the other sideline as Shedeur Sanders holds a clipboard behind a three and one quarterback.

Watch three things: whether Jerry Jeudy's snap share climbs back above 40 percent after Todd Monken publicly called him a rotational player, whether Dillon Gabriel, whose twenty-one day practice window opened October 6, does anything beyond scout-team work, and whether Sanders takes a single snap in a game still being decided. In a week when six teams were discussed as buyers, one series would have been worth more to Cleveland's asking price than every podcast segment combined.

Then Baltimore comes to Cleveland October 18, with Lamar Jackson's ankle an open question. New Orleans at home November 8, the deadline November 10, the bye in Week 11.

The bottom line.

The demand is real. Tony Grossi is right that there has to be a market for a 24-year-old on a cheap contract through 2028 when this many teams are quarterback-sick. Six franchises can justify the phone call.

And the price is still a fifth, because every one of those six is either too broken to pay, too temporary to care, or too committed to a quarterback it already owns, and because the only thing a buyer can price is tape, and Cleveland has produced one yard of it this season.

That lesson keeps arriving from new directions this autumn. Odell Beckham Junior learned it on an active roster with one target. Jerry Jeudy is learning it at thirty-seven and a half percent of the snaps. And Shedeur Sanders is learning it the most expensive way available: a league full of teams that need a quarterback, none willing to pay for one they have not been allowed to watch.

An unused asset is not a preserved asset. Six interested teams and a fifth-round price is what that sentence looks like on a general manager's spreadsheet.
