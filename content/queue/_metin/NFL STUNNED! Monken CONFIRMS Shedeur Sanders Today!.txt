A superpower." "He got knocked down and came back in." Those quotes were real. They were about Deshaun Watson.
 
A press conference breakdown went around this week that had Browns fans genuinely fired up, and we understand why. The quotes in it are great.
 
Todd Monken stopping a reporter mid-question: "Hold on, I'm not done yet. Let me enjoy the moment."
 
Calling a quarterback's legs "a superpower."
 
The story about a quarterback getting knocked down, popping up, jabbing at his coach on the sideline, "I'm good, I'm good", then going back in, scoring, and finishing it with a two-point conversion.
 
"We're going to be a damn good football team once Browns stop hurting Browns."
 
All of that is real. Every word of it was said.
 
It was said about Deshaun Watson.
 
Not Shedeur Sanders. And this matters enough to spend an article on, because thousands of people now believe a thing happened on Sunday that didn't, and because the actual story underneath is better than the mixed-up version.
 
How we know.
 
Let's lay the evidence out plainly, because this isn't a matter of opinion.
 
One: Shedeur Sanders' entire afternoon was one pass for one yard, a dump-off to the running back. That's his whole statistical contribution to the home opener. He did not score. He did not convert a two-point conversion. He did not lead a drive.
 
Two: the quarterback who got knocked down and came back is the one who finished the game. Cleveland was down eighteen to thirteen and won twenty-one to eighteen. Thirteen plus a touchdown plus a two-point conversion equals twenty-one. That's the go-ahead drive, the eight-play, 55-yard possession that ended with Watson rolling left, throwing back across his body to the right, and hitting Harold Fannin Junior in the back of the end zone with one forty-eight left. The two-point conversion Monken was talking about is on that same drive.
 
Three: the "still learning the system, getting his eyes right, getting his drops right" language is Watson's, and it has been for three weeks. Monken used nearly identical words after Tampa: "There's a lot of football left in him. He just hasn't played in a while… learning our system and trying to get him consistent with his drops and his reads." That's a man who missed 22 months with two Achilles surgeries, not a second-year backup who has been in the building since last spring.
 
Four: the legs. Watson had eleven carries for 45 yards on Sunday, broke a would-be sack for an eight-yard first down, and led the team in rushing for the second straight week. Monken told us two weeks ago that Watson asked for it, "Don't be afraid to use my legs." The superpower comment lands exactly where the carries are.
 
The praise was genuine. The player was just the other one.
 
Also, Monken is the head coach.
 
Smaller fix, but it's the third time this week.
 
Todd Monken is Cleveland's head coach. He calls the offense, which is why he's the one explaining second-and-one, but the title is head coach. He won his first game as one in Tampa.
 
Earlier this week, two different videos called the Browns' head coach Kevin Stefanski. Now one has Monken as the offensive coordinator. These aren't gotchas; they're a symptom. When content gets produced at this speed, the roles and the names blur, and then the quotes get attached to whoever the audience most wants to hear about.
 
What "superpower" actually means.
 
Now the football, because the substance deserves a real look.
 
Monken calling Watson's mobility a superpower is a significant thing for a head coach to say out loud, and it tells you how this offense is being built right now: the quarterback's legs are a designed part of the plan, not an accident.
 
Look at the evidence. Eleven carries, 45 yards. A broken sack converted into a first down on a key series. Team-leading rusher two weeks running. And a passing game that produced 61 yards in the first half and 144 total yards the week before.
 
That's a quarterback keeping an offense alive with his feet while his passing game catches up. Monken said as much, that the Browns have not yet played their best football on offense.
 
And here's the part that needs saying every time we praise it, because we've said it since Tampa: it's also an exposure. A quarterback with two Achilles surgeries behind him, carrying eleven times a game, across seventeen games, is a bet on a body that has already broken twice. The superpower and the risk are the same play.
 
The knocked-down story, told correctly.
 
And now the moment, given to the right man, because it's genuinely excellent.
 
Watson took a hit and had to come off. Before Monken had even finished calling the next play, Watson was on the sideline jabbing at him, "I'm good, I'm good." Monken's response, basically: okay, fine, I already called the play, what do you want me to say?
 
Then he went back in and finished the drive. Touchdown. Two-point conversion. Lead.
 
Sit with that for a second, with the right name attached. This is a man who spent 22 months rehabbing two Achilles tears on the same leg, who was booed by his own crowd an hour earlier, taking a hit in the fourth quarter and arguing with his head coach to get back on the field.
 
That story is more impressive about Watson than it would have been about anybody else in that building, because of what it took for him to be out there at all. The version going around accidentally gave the best thing he did all season to somebody else.
 
The real best quote. "Once Browns stop hurting Browns."
 
And the line nobody should have buried under a mix-up.
 
"We're going to be a damn good football team once Browns stop hurting Browns. And we're going to fight like hell."
 
That's the whole season in one sentence, and the number behind it is ten penalties.
 
Think about what that phrase means coming from a first-year head coach in this city. "Browns hurting Browns" is a precise, honest description of a franchise that has spent years losing games to itself, the pre-snap operation, the flags, the lost fumble, the two three-and-outs to open a second half. Not the opponent. Us.
 
And it pairs with the part of the press conference that got almost no attention: Monken said the offense wasn't good enough, the coaching wasn't good enough, and that it starts with him. Same as Tampa, when he said the run game "is not a negative, it's a fact" and that his challenge flag was wrong.
 
A coach who points at himself after a win is the most underrated thing happening in Berea right now.
 
And what's still true about Shedeur.
 
Here's why we didn't let this one slide, and it's not about scoring points on another channel.
 
Shedeur Sanders doesn't need borrowed praise. He threw one pass for one yard. Attaching a veteran's toughness story to him doesn't help him, it sets up a standard he never agreed to, and it hands his critics an easy correction to make.
 
What's actually true about his situation is sharper than any of it:
 
Mary Kay Cabot reported the Browns have no plans to trade him or Dillon Gabriel before November 10. That question is closed. And the J.J. McCarthy trade, a former first-round pick with ten starts for a 2027 fifth, showed the market for young quarterbacks is far cheaper than this rumor cycle claimed.
 
So the only question left is the one we've asked for two weeks: if Cleveland is keeping him, what is Cleveland going to learn about him? A package. A series. Situational snaps. One yard isn't information, and neither is a quote that belonged to somebody else.
 
Why this mix-up happens.
 
It's worth understanding the mechanism, because it isn't dishonesty and it isn't stupidity.
 
Press conference quotes almost never contain names. Listen to how coaches actually talk: "he's still learning the system," "that's one of his superpowers," "to get knocked down and come back in and make a play." He. Him. His. The reporter asked a question with a name in it; the answer comes back with a pronoun. If you clip the answer and lose the question, the subject is gone, and whoever is listening fills it in with the player they're already thinking about.
 
That's the whole mechanism. A pronoun plus an expectation equals a false story, and it travels faster than the correction ever will.
 
The defense is simple and it costs nothing: before you attach a quote to a player, check what that player actually did in the game. One pass for one yard cannot produce a touchdown and a two-point conversion. Thirty seconds of arithmetic protects you from a week of arguing.
 
And this cuts at us too. We've written eight of these in ten days from transcripts, and the discipline that keeps it honest is boring: count the snaps, check the score, read the box. Not glamorous. It's the job.
 
Where we stop.
 
"That's not a coordinator secretly shopping for a replacement." Correct, and it's about Watson, which makes it a statement of support for the starter, not a defense of the backup.
 
"The haters were quiet all afternoon." They were, because he played one snap. You can't be criticized for a game you didn't play, and you can't be credited for one either. Same rule we applied to Watson after Tampa, and to Sanders after Jacksonville.
 
And the bigger one: don't build an argument on an unchecked attribution. It's how a fan base ends up arguing for a week about something that never happened. We've done eight of these breakdowns in ten days and the pattern is always the same, the emotion travels, the facts don't.
 
Thursday.
 
The part everybody got right: Cleveland is two and one and tied for first in the AFC North, with Pittsburgh coming in Thursday night with first place on the line.
 
Monken's reaction to that, isn't that cool or what, is the correct reaction. After a thirty-four to ten opening loss in Jacksonville, nobody had this team in first place in October.
 
What has to change on four days' rest: the ten penalties, first and foremost. You can't simplify your way out of self-inflicted damage, and a short week means there's no time to install anything new, only to clean up what's already there.
 
Browns hurting Browns is the thing Thursday will be decided by.
