A Future Fifth-Rounder? Breaking Down the Shedeur-to-Baltimore Proposal — And Why the Price Is the Entire Argument

Let's get the most important sentence out of the way first, because Ktl Report isn't going to let anybody panic over something that hasn't happened.

This is a media trade proposal. It is not a report.

There is no indication that Cleveland and Baltimore have discussed anything. No insider has attached a source to it. Nobody's phone rang. A FanSided piece floated a hypothetical — Shedeur Sanders to the Baltimore Ravens for a future fifth-round pick — and the internet did what the internet does.

So why are we spending an entire article on a hypothetical?

Because the proposal is a useful test. It forces a question this fan base has been arguing around for weeks without ever pinning down: what is Shedeur Sanders actually worth? And the moment you put a real price on the table, everybody's position gets a lot more specific.

We're also going to break down why Baltimore genuinely makes sense, why the price in this particular proposal is indefensible, and the one buried detail about Dillon Gabriel that might be the most revealing thing in the entire story.

THE SETUP: FOUR QUARTERBACKS, ONE ROOM 🧮

Start with the situation, because it's what generates every one of these conversations.

Cleveland has four quarterbacks. Deshaun Watson is QB1. Shedeur Sanders is behind him. Dillon Gabriel is on injured reserve with a back issue. Rookie Taylen Green made the roster.

Four is unsustainable past the first month of a season — we've walked through that math here before. Something eventually gives.

And the front office hasn't exactly slammed the door. Assistant general manager Catherine Hickman, asked about trade offers involving the quarterback room, didn't confirm anything and instead deferred the question to general manager Andrew Berry.

That's a second consecutive non-answer from that podium on this exact subject, and it's worth noting what it isn't: a denial. When nothing is happening, executives say nothing is happening — it costs them nothing. Deferring is not the same as denying, and everyone in that room knows it.

Berry, for his part, has a documented willingness to explore, evaluate, and move when he sees value. That isn't an accusation; it's a description of how he operates.

WHY BALTIMORE ACTUALLY MAKES SENSE 🟣

Now the football logic, because this proposal isn't random — it identifies a real need.

The Ravens have Lamar Jackson, and that's settled. Two-time MVP. Nobody is trading for a quarterback to compete with him. That's not the opening.

The opening is what happens behind him. Baltimore's depth currently runs through Tyler Huntley and undrafted rookie Joe Fagnano. Huntley has struggled in recent years, and Fagnano hasn't done enough in preseason to lock down a backup role with any confidence.

And here's the detail that turns depth from a luxury into an emergency: Lamar Jackson missed four important games last season.

That's the number that makes this whole idea coherent. A team with genuine championship expectations, whose entire offense is built around one irreplaceable player, and whose contingency plan is shaky. Every front office in that position spends the fall quietly asking who's available.

Then there's the coaching angle, and it's more interesting than it looks. Baltimore has a new offensive coordinator in Declan Doyle, who comes out of Chicago and has been described as the first coordinator off the Ben Johnson tree — the staff credited with helping Caleb Williams take a major step forward in his second season.

Nobody's claiming Shedeur Sanders is Caleb Williams. But the underlying argument is legitimate: quarterback development is heavily dependent on the coaching and infrastructure around a player. The same prospect in two different buildings frequently produces two different careers.

THE DEVELOPMENTAL ARGUMENT 🌱

And this is where the proposal makes its strongest point, which Cleveland fans should sit with even if they hate it.

In Baltimore, Shedeur Sanders would be nobody's referendum.

Think about what that actually means. Lamar Jackson is the franchise. There's no quarterback controversy to feed, no contract to litigate, no talk radio segment every Monday about whether the backup should play. A young quarterback there gets to do the thing young quarterbacks almost never get anymore: learn quietly.

Now compare that to his current environment. Every incompletion by the starter turns into a question about him. Every ticket price story has his name in it. Every press conference is a trap. He is, through no fault of his own, the most scrutinized backup in professional football.

That's not an ideal developmental environment. It's a fishbowl with a spotlight in it.

So yes — the developmental case for a change of scenery is real, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

But the developmental case is an argument for a trade. It is not an argument for this trade.

THE PRICE IS THE WHOLE PROBLEM 💸

A future fifth-round pick.

Let's be direct: that return is not close to acceptable, and Cleveland should hang up the phone.

Here's why, laid out properly.

One: it's a wash on capital. Cleveland used a fifth-rounder to acquire him. Getting a future fifth-rounder back means the franchise spent a full year of coaching, development, reps, and organizational attention and came out exactly even. That's not a trade. That's a refund — and refunds are what you accept when a player has shown you nothing.

Two: he's shown considerably more than nothing. By multiple accounts, including reporting cited in this very proposal, he outperformed a veteran in preseason. We've covered the film breakdowns here at Ktl Report: forced offside penalties, a third-and-fifteen conversion with a full-field progression, a fourth-down conversion, quick release, avoiding sacks. You do not sell a stock that has appreciated for the price you paid for it.

Three — and this is the one front offices understand best — quarterbacks carry option value. Every other position is worth roughly what it produces. A quarterback is worth what he might become, and that possibility has a price of its own. Teams routinely pay second- and third-round picks for quarterbacks who've shown a fraction of what this one has shown, precisely because the upside case is worth so much. The entire league operates on that asymmetry.

Four: the market isn't empty. The whole premise of this proposal is that Baltimore has a need. Needs create competition, and competition sets prices. If a contender genuinely believes its season could hinge on the backup spot, a fifth-round pick is a lowball opening offer, not a fair valuation.

So here's the honest framing: the idea of a trade isn't crazy. The number attached to it is. Anyone arguing this deal should be asked a simple follow-up — would you do it for a second-round pick? Because that's a different conversation entirely, and it's where the real debate lives.

THE GABRIEL DETAIL THAT EXPLAINS EVERYTHING 🔍

Now the buried item, and it might be the most revealing thing in the entire story.

According to the same report, Cleveland's front office actually preferred Dillon Gabriel as a prospect. They took him in the third round of the 2025 draft — roughly fifty picks before selecting Sanders in the fifth.

We'll label that properly: that's a reported characterization of internal preference, not a confirmed statement from the building. But it's consistent with the observable behavior, which is what makes it worth taking seriously.

And it creates a genuinely awkward inversion:

More draft capital was invested in Gabriel. More depth chart position was earned by Sanders.

Sit with that, because it explains an enormous amount about how this quarterback room has been managed. When an organization's stated evaluation and its own depth chart point in different directions, somebody has to lose an argument — and organizations are historically terrible at admitting their board was wrong.

It also reframes the trade conversation completely. If Cleveland's internal view is still that Gabriel is the better long-term fit, then trading Sanders isn't a betrayal of a plan. It's the plan. Uncomfortable for fans, but coherent from the inside.

And if you want the most cynical version — one we'll clearly label as speculation, not reporting — trading the more popular quarterback would also quietly remove the weekly public pressure that a backup with this much attention generates. That's the thing this front office would gain that doesn't show up as draft capital at all: silence.

We're not asserting that's the motive. We're noting it's a benefit, and organizations respond to benefits.

THE DIVISION PROBLEM 😤

Then there's the part that makes Cleveland fans physically uncomfortable.

Baltimore is in the AFC North. You see them twice a year, every year, forever.

Trading a young quarterback within your own division is one of the few moves that can haunt a franchise on a fixed schedule. Every mistake is re-broadcast on a calendar. And given how this fan base has attached itself to this particular player, watching him develop in purple would be an annual event nobody in Cleveland wants.

The counterargument is fair: he'd be a backup behind an MVP, not a starter, so the nightmare scenario requires a lot of things to break in a specific order.

But here's the thing about nightmare scenarios in Cleveland sports history — they have a documented habit of breaking in exactly that order.

THE TRAP RUNS BOTH WAYS 🪤

And this is what makes the situation genuinely hard rather than obvious.

If Cleveland trades him and Watson struggles, the questions start in Week 2 and never stop. If he then develops somewhere else into something worth more than a fifth-round pick, this becomes a decision people are still arguing about in a decade — and this city has a long memory for exactly that kind of decision.

If Cleveland keeps him and he never plays, the organization burns a developmental year. A quarterback who takes no meaningful snaps doesn't improve on a schedule; he mostly just gets older while the questions get louder.

There's no clean option. That's not a failure of imagination — it's the actual structure of the problem, and it exists because of a decision made years ago at a negotiating table, not anything happening on a practice field now.

WHAT WE'D ACTUALLY DO 🎯

Here's the Ktl Report position, stated plainly.

Keep him — unless someone genuinely overpays.

Not out of sentiment, and not because of what the ticket market thinks. Because of asset management. A team carrying an $86.2 million cap charge into 2027 has exactly one affordable path to a functional roster: young, cheap, high-upside players on rookie contracts. A quarterback on a fifth-round deal who might be a starter is the single most valuable version of that asset in the entire sport.

You don't sell that for what you paid. You either find out what you have, or you make somebody pay a real price for the chance to find out for you.

And if the answer this season is that Watson holds the job and Sanders never plays? Then Cleveland is having this exact conversation again in March — with more information, a cleaner cap picture, and a market that has watched another quarterback-desperate offseason arrive.

Patience costs almost nothing here. A fifth-round pick costs the option.

THE BOTTOM LINE 🏈

So where does this land?

It's a proposal, not a report. Nothing is happening. But the questions underneath it are completely real, and the front office's refusal to close the door — twice now, from two different podiums — is why fans keep pulling on this thread.

Baltimore's need is legitimate. The developmental argument is legitimate. The division discomfort is legitimate. And the price in this specific proposal is not.

What we're really watching isn't who starts on Sunday. It's what Cleveland does with this quarterback room once the season is actually moving — when Watson has tape, when Gabriel comes off IR, and when other teams start calling for real.

Andrew Berry and Todd Monken have decisions coming that will define this franchise for years. The one thing this fan base cannot survive is another quarterback decision people are still debating a decade from now, wondering what would have happened if the team had chosen differently.

Jacksonville is here. The information starts arriving Sunday.
