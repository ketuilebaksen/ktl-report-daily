The motion. Two analysts moved that Shedeur Sanders be named starter, so here is who actually has standing, what evidence is admissible, and what the standard of proof really is.

First, the title. Monken made no announcement at all.

The title is "Todd Monken Make Huge Announcement After Crazy Saints Game!"

Todd Monken announced nothing. He was asked the question directly and refused it in plain language, with a reason:

Q: "Did Sanders make a strong case for himself to start?"
MONKEN: "I'm not even, I mean, how long has it been? Thirty minutes since the game ended. I'm not getting into quarterback. If we want to talk about Shedeur, we can talk about Shedeur."

That is the opposite of an announcement. It is a man declining to open a quarterback competition half an hour after an overtime loss, before watching a single frame of tape.

So where does the "huge announcement" in the title come from? It comes from the first ninety seconds of this video, which are not Monken at all. They are two television analysts, and one of them says this:

"Moving forward, Shedeur Sanders should be the starter for this football team. He's going to be the future, and I think what he showed today, his resiliency, continued to come back, he made great decisions, he extended plays. I love the way he played and I would move forward with him."

That is an opinion delivered on a broadcast. The title turns it into a team announcement. Those are not the same object and the gap between them is the entire subject of this article.

So here is how we are going to handle it.

Treat the analyst's sentence as what it actually is: a motion. Somebody has moved that the quarterback be changed. Fine. Motions are legitimate. But a motion only means something if you can answer three questions about it, and nobody in this video answers any of them.

One. Who has standing, that is, whose motion can actually produce the outcome?
Two. What evidence is admissible, as opposed to merely exciting?
Three. What is the standard of proof, what would actually have to be shown?

Here at Ktl Report we are going to take the motion completely seriously, which means putting it through all three. And we will tell you now where we land, so you can argue with us the whole way down: the motion is reasonable, the evidence is real but thinner than it feels, and the people filing it cannot grant it.

Question one. Who has standing.

The analysts do not. This is not a slight on them. Their job is to have a view on television and they did it well, the second one immediately said "I agree with you one hundred percent as far as how he played. He played very well and he took exactly what the defense was giving him, especially in overtime. That's how they got in position to even go for two." That is a sharp, specific observation and it is better analysis than most of what we audit.

But a broadcast opinion has no mechanism attached to it. Nobody at that desk can change a depth chart.

The head coach does have standing, and he used it to say "not tonight." Which is the correct use of it, and he explained why in the same session: asked whether Sanders had given him confidence he did not previously have, he said "that's a great question," credited the improvement he had seen over six months, and then stopped himself:

"But ultimately, you've got to go play, right? You got to go on the field and you got to execute, and guys got to play well around him. It's not a one-man deal. We got to coach. We got to scheme. So, till I really see the tape."

"Till I really see the tape" is not stonewalling. It is the only honest answer available thirty minutes after a football game, and it is also, quietly, the answer to the motion: he is not ruling on it because the evidence has not been examined yet.

And then there is the party with the most standing of all, which nobody in this video mentions: the medical process. More on that below, because the video gets it badly wrong.

Standing, ruled: the motion is filed by people who cannot grant it, before the only person who can has reviewed the record. That does not make it wrong. At Ktl Report we would call it premature, which is a different thing entirely.

Question two. What evidence is admissible.

Now the part that matters, because there is real evidence here and we are not going to wave it away the way the sceptics will.

Exhibit A, the first half, which the motion's supporters have to carry too.

By halftime Shedeur Sanders had completed five of eight passes for thirty-eight yards. The video is honest about this and deserves the credit: "Those were hardly the numbers of a quarterback taking over the NFL."

Thirty-eight yards in a half. Whatever happened later, this is part of the record and any argument that skips it is not an argument.

Exhibit B, the sixty-yard connection with Denzel Boston.

The first genuine explosion of the afternoon, and the thing the offense had been missing: a willingness to attack downfield rather than let the defense dictate every possession. Admissible, and significant, because the deep passing game is precisely the area Monken had called "under construction" two days earlier.

Exhibit C, the sixty-four-yard touchdown to KC Concepcion.

The fourth quarter, Cleveland trailing, and the longest play of the day. This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the file, and it is stronger for a reason the video does not state: Concepcion is the first-round rookie who spent all of July and August catching passes from Sanders specifically, while Sanders was the backup. The reps he kept pointing at all season produced the play that changed the game. That is not a coincidence, it is a thesis being confirmed.

Exhibit D, the drive that forced overtime.

New Orleans answered, Cleveland was in trouble, and Sanders moved the team into position for a fifty-one-yard field goal that tied the game at sixteen and forced the extra period.

Admissible, with a caveat the video states honestly: "Shedeur wasn't perfect. He made mistakes, took sacks, and struggled through long stretches." Correct, and it belongs in the file.

Exhibit E, the overtime drive, which is the one we would put first.

New Orleans scored first in overtime. Cleveland needed a touchdown or the afternoon was over. Sanders led a sixty-seven-yard drive and threw a thirty-nine-yard touchdown to Harold Fannin Junior

Consider what that required. A tight end who went into the week limited with a groin injury. A quarterback who had not taken a first-team snap in ten weeks. Season over if the drive stalls. They went sixty-seven yards and scored.

Exhibit F, what the coach actually credited, which is not what the highlights show.

Monken's own list of what Sanders did was almost entirely about the unglamorous parts:

"He gave us a chance at the end. He really did. Made some plays. Made some plays with his legs to extend plays and get us first downs. When we were in four-minute or close to four-minute, he got us a third down conversion. Unfortunately, we got the holding call."

Extending plays with his legs. A third-down conversion in four-minute offense. Four-minute is the offense you run to close a game out, and converting a third down in it is a quarterback doing the hardest, least visible job on the list. The coach led with that and not with the sixty-four-yard touchdown, which tells you what he was watching.

Exhibit G, the maturity answer, including the self-correction.

"He's really matured. And I don't mean that in a negative way, that he was immature, that's unfair, that's not what I mean. What I mean is in terms of his work ethic, in terms of his diligence, in terms of what he will say to the coaching staff and ask the questions, his feedback. Those are all the right steps moving forward as any young player, especially at quarterback."

A head coach interrupting himself to protect a player from his own phrasing is admissible as evidence of something, and what it is evidence of is a coaching staff that is invested rather than merely tolerant.

And the evidence we are ruling inadmissible.

Inadmissible: "this loss is not on Shedeur Sanders. This is on the defense. They dropped the ball when it mattered. They couldn't make a play."

We understand the impulse and we are not carrying it. Cleveland lost by one point after a two-point conversion failed, following a first quarter in which the team committed six penalties across special teams, offense and defense. A one-point overtime loss does not have a single owner, and assigning it to the defense in order to protect the quarterback is the same error as assigning it to the quarterback in order to protect everyone else.

Inadmissible: the comparison to Deshaun Watson's season as though both men faced the same conditions. Watson has started eight games behind a line that has allowed thirty-three sacks. Sanders started one, at the end of a week in which he was the full focus of the game plan for the first time in two and a half months. Those are different experiments and you cannot read the results against each other yet. The video half-admits this and then does it anyway.

Inadmissible: anything about Watson's head, his contract, or "the beginning of the end of his reign." He took a helmet-to-helmet hit, was cleared on the field, reported symptoms the next day honestly, and entered a protocol. We are not building a succession narrative on a head injury and we have refused to do it three weeks running.

The factual error at the centre of the video.

This one matters enough to stop everything for, because the entire emotional architecture of the piece rests on it.

The video says:

"Deshaun Watson was already cleared from concussion protocol before Cleveland faced the Saints. He was available to return to the conversation. Yet Todd Monken had already committed to giving Shedeur his opportunity."

That is not what happened, and it reverses the meaning of the week.

Going into the game, Watson was listed as questionable, not cleared. He missed Wednesday and Thursday practice entirely and returned in a limited capacity on Friday. Monken described the limitation precisely: "He's still not able to do any team reps. He can do individual, he can do some routes, been in meetings, but you're not able to do any of the team reps, which makes it difficult."

And when a reporter asked straight out whether the reason for starting Sanders was the practice time, Monken answered in a single word: "Exactly."

So Monken did not pick Shedeur Sanders over an available Deshaun Watson. He started the only quarterback who had practised with the team. The video's version turns a medical and logistical necessity into a bold act of faith, which is a better story and a false one.

If you record this segment, fix it. It is the difference between "the coach chose the kid" and "the protocol chose the kid," and every conclusion downstream depends on which one you say.

Question three. The standard of proof.

Here is where we part company with everybody on both sides, because almost nobody states a standard before arguing about whether it has been met.

The wrong standard is "did he look like a franchise quarterback for one quarter?" By that measure the motion carries easily and it tells you nothing. Backup quarterbacks produce brilliant fourth quarters every weekend of the season. A defense protecting a lead plays differently, softer and deeper, and yards come more cheaply. The second analyst said the honest version himself without realising he was undercutting his own case: "he took exactly what the defense was giving him, especially in overtime." Taking what is given is good quarterbacking. It is also a description of favourable conditions.

The wrong standard in the other direction is "five of eight for thirty-eight yards at half, so this was nothing." That ignores a sixty-yard throw, a sixty-four-yard touchdown, a drive that forced overtime and a sixty-seven-yard drive to stay alive.

So here is the standard we would actually apply, and we are printing it before the next game so nobody can move it:

One. Does it repeat with the defense playing normally? Everything in Exhibits B through E happened with Cleveland chasing. The test of a starter is the first quarter, not the fourth, and Cleveland has the worst first quarter in football.

Two. Does it survive a second week of film? Opponents now have a full game of him. The second start is the one that tells you something, because this time somebody has prepared for him specifically.

Three. Does the sack and turnover column hold up over three games, not one? The video concedes he "took sacks." Behind this line everybody does. The question is the trend.

Four. Does the operation hold at home? Monken's stated worry all week was "getting to the line of scrimmage" and "handling the noise." Cleveland now plays four home games and a bye before it travels again, having played six of its first nine on the road. That is the friendliest possible environment in which to find out.

Ruling on the motion: not granted, not denied, held over for evidence. And we would add the thing nobody wants to hear, the schedule is about to make this decision much easier either way, because for the first time all season Cleveland is going to be at home long enough to see a quarterback in normal conditions.

And the quote from Sanders that closes a three week story.

Buried in the middle of this video is a pre-game exchange that resolves something we have written about three times, and almost nobody is going to notice it.

Q: "Are you just going to let your game speak for itself today?"
SANDERS: "Everything I do up to this point is taken out of context, but I can't be one to care what people say. I would never disrespect or ever talk down on another opponent or another team. So that's just another trick in the media, you know, they use. But however they feel is as they feel, I don't care how they feel. Best of luck today."

That is him answering the "smile" story directly, and he answered it the way we read it. For a week the internet insisted he had mocked the New Orleans defense. The full version of that original quote ended with six words almost everyone cut, "whenever it's just like some questions", which made clear he was declining to answer a type of question, not describing an opponent.

Here he says it outright: he would never talk down on an opponent, and the interpretation was a media construction. Monken confirmed the same thing from the other side after the game:

"I think the opposition took it, when they mentioned playing this week, like it was disrespectful. I think he was just excited to play. I think the smile on his face just said, 'Man, I'm nothing to do with your defense. I'm just fired up to play.'"

Player and coach, independently, gave the same account. The story was wrong. We said so when it was running and we are saying so now that it is over, which is the only time saying so is worth anything.

The numbers the motion has to live with.

Set out plainly, because the video gives you the thrilling ones and skips the structural ones.

Thirty-eight passing yards at halftime, on five of eight.
Six penalties in the first quarter, special teams, offense and defense, including jumping offside twice on defense. Monken: "it's not sustainable for us to continue down that road."
A holding call that wiped out the third-down conversion he had just earned in four-minute offense.
A fifty-one-yard field goal to tie it at sixteen, not a touchdown.
A two-point conversion that failed, after a holding penalty complicated the attempt.
Final: twenty-three to twenty-two, New Orleans.
And the sentence Monken offered as the identity of his own team: "right now we're five and two in one-score games."

That is a side that cannot start and will not quit, losing by one point in the margin where it lives. A quarterback change does not fix a first quarter with six penalties in it. It is worth saying that out loud in a week when everybody wants one decision to be the answer to everything.

What we could not verify.

Shedeur Sanders' full final stat line, Cleveland's turnover count and the total sacks allowed. The video gives the halftime line and the big plays and not the totals. Read the box score before you assert anything about efficiency.
Whether the sixty-yard play to Denzel Boston was a touchdown or a gain. The video says gain; we are reporting it as a gain.
The exact sequence of the overtime possessions beyond what the video states.
Which analysts are speaking in the opening segment and the later clips. We are describing them by their arguments rather than guessing at names, and you should caption them correctly or not at all.
The identity of the rookie receiver who left with an ankle problem and returned. In the Friday and post-game material the names do not line up cleanly. Confirm before you say it.
Whether the holding call on the two-point attempt was correct. We have not seen the tape and will not referee from a transcript.

What we are not carrying.

We are not carrying "Monken made a huge announcement." He refused the question and said why: thirty minutes, no tape.

We are not carrying "Watson was cleared and Monken chose Shedeur anyway." He was questionable, missed two practices, took no team reps, and Monken said the reason was "Exactly" that.

We are not carrying "the beginning of the end of his reign in Cleveland." That is a sentence about a man in a concussion protocol and we have declined it three weeks running.

We are not carrying "this loss is on the defense." One point, overtime, six first-quarter penalties, a failed two-point play. It has no single owner.

We are not carrying the claim that Sanders disrespected the Saints. He denied it on the record before the game, Monken denied it after, and the full original quote never said it.

The bottom line.

Nobody from the Cleveland Browns announced anything on Sunday. Two television analysts moved that the quarterback be changed, which they are entitled to do and which cannot by itself change anything, and the only man with the authority to rule on it said "thirty minutes since the game ended" and "till I really see the tape." The title of this video promotes an opinion into a decision, and the body of it rests on a factual error that reverses the week: Deshaun Watson was not cleared. He was questionable, he missed two practices, he took no team repetitions, and when a reporter asked whether that was the reason Shedeur Sanders started, Monken said one word, "Exactly."

The evidence underneath all that is real and it is mixed, and both halves belong in the file. Five of eight for thirty-eight yards at the break. Then a sixty-yard connection with Denzel Boston, a sixty-four-yard touchdown to the first-round rookie he had been throwing to since July, a drive to set up a fifty-one-yard kick that forced overtime, and then, with the season a stalled drive away from ending, sixty-seven yards and a thirty-nine-yard touchdown to a tight end who had practised limited all week with a groin injury. All of it from a quarterback who had not taken a first-team snap in ten weeks and had spent two and a half months holding up scout-team cards.

And what his coach credited first was none of those throws. It was extending plays with his legs and a third-down conversion in four-minute offense that a holding call then took away.

So here at Ktl Report the ruling is: motion not granted, not denied, held over for evidence, and we printed the four tests we will apply, which are whether it repeats with the defense playing normally, whether it survives a second week of film, whether the sacks and turnovers hold over three games, and whether the operation holds at home. Cleveland is five and two in one-score games, has played six of its first nine on the road, and now has four home games and a bye. The answer is coming, in better conditions than it has had all year.

And one last thing, because it closes a story we have carried for three weeks. The "smile" was never a taunt. He said so himself before kickoff, "I would never disrespect or ever talk down on another opponent", and his coach said the same thing afterwards, unprompted: "I think he was just excited to play."

Six penalties in a first quarter is the problem. No quarterback on earth fixes that one by himself.
