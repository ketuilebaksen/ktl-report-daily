But first, the error that should stop the video at second one.

The opening line, repeated verbatim at the ten-minute mark, is: "The Miami Dolphins are one of the favorites to trade for Browns Pro Bowl quarterback Shedeur Sanders."

Shedeur Sanders has never been to a Pro Bowl. He is a second-year quarterback, the one hundred forty-fourth pick of the twenty twenty-five draft, twenty-four years old, on an inexpensive rookie contract through twenty twenty-eight, who has not taken a meaningful snap in twenty twenty-six.

That is not a slip of the tongue, because it is said twice, and it is not harmless. It inflates the asset at the exact moment the video is trying to argue about his trade value. A Pro Bowl quarterback is not available for a fifth-round pick. The fact that the same script argues both things in the same breath is the tell.

Where the forty-five percent actually came from.

The video presents the number like this: "Polymarket has suggested there is a forty-five percent chance that Shedeur ends up getting traded. That is not a rumor floating around on social media. That is real money from real people saying this is almost a coin flip."

Here is the actual chain of custody, and it is short.

Link one: an account on X called MLFootball posted that Miami is "one of the FAVORITES" to trade for Sanders, citing Polymarket's forty-five percent.

Link two: content sites wrote that up. The write-up we read states plainly that it is attributing the figure to MLFootball's citation and does not verify it itself.

Link three: this video converts that into "several reports now say Miami is ahead in the negotiations," and then into "they are reportedly pushing. They are negotiating."

Read the two ends of that chain together. It begins with a social-media aggregator quoting a betting market. It ends with the assertion that two front offices are in negotiations. No reporter, anywhere, has reported a negotiation between Cleveland and Miami. Not Schefter, not Rapoport, not Cabot, not Grossi, not Fowler.

And note the irony in the video's own framing: "that is not a rumor floating around on social media." It is, almost exactly, a rumor floating around on social media, with a percentage attached.

What a prediction market actually measures.

This part matters beyond the Browns, so let us be precise rather than dismissive. Prediction markets are genuinely useful. They are not reporting, and they are not evidence of a negotiation.

What they measure: the aggregate opinion of the people choosing to trade that specific contract, weighted by how much they are willing to risk.

What they do not measure: whether anybody with information is among those people.

And here is the part that should end the "real money" argument: the market is reflexive. Where do the traders get their information? From reports, from podcasts, from X accounts, from videos like this one. When a rumor spreads, the price moves. Then the moved price is quoted as independent confirmation of the rumor. That is a loop, not a check. On a market about a specific player's specific transaction, the price can rise purely because the rumor got louder, and nothing in the football world changed.

Compare that with the thing this video does not weigh at all: Jeremy Fowler, who actually works sources, put Jerry Jeudy's trade chances at twenty percent and said the quiet part out loud about price, "I'm not sure, say, a fourth-round pick would move anyone in Cleveland." That is a probability from a person with phone numbers. It is worth more than a market price, and it is lower.

So the honest sentence is: forty-five percent is what a group of anonymous traders thought on a given day, and it is one input. It is not a report, it is not money changing hands in Berea, and it is certainly not a negotiation.

So let us build an honest number.

If we are going to criticise a probability, we owe you one of our own. Here is ours, with the reasoning exposed so you can disagree with the steps rather than the conclusion.

Start with the named-reporter record. Mary Kay Cabot, October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth: the Browns are not shopping any of their quarterbacks and have no plans to trade Sanders or Gabriel before November tenth, "unless someone makes them an offer they can't refuse." Jonathan Jones of The Athletic, October seventeenth: "the Browns dealing Sanders by the deadline would be a stunner." Jeremy Fowler: Cleveland would welcome a deal at the right price. Those are not in conflict. They describe a team that will answer the phone and will not dial out.

Add the base rate. Seven trades have closed in this league since late September with public terms. Not one of them involved a team in first place in its division selling a contributing player. Sellers sell. Cleveland is five and two.

Add the mechanical fact from Friday. Grossi reported Gabriel inactive and Sanders as Q B two. A club that trades its active backup quarterback nine days before the deadline has to dress a quarterback who has not held that job all season, in a playoff race, with a starter who has a significant injury history. That is a real cost, and it is a cost that rises every week Cleveland keeps winning.

Add the price problem. Even the Dolphins-side analysis we read floats a twenty twenty-seven sixth-round pick and labels it explicitly as not reported and not a valuation. The video says a fifth. Cabot's standard is "an offer they can't refuse." A sixth is not that. A fifth is not that. Nobody has named a number that is.

Our estimate: well under forty-five percent. We would put it in the ten-to-fifteen percent range, deliberately below Fowler's twenty percent for Jeudy, because Jeudy is a veteran on an expensive contract whose targets have gone to two rookies, while Sanders is a cheap, controllable quarterback whose own general manager is reported to be fond of him and whose head coach named him the backup on Friday.

What would move our number up, fast: a named reporter describing an actual conversation; Cleveland losing two of its next three; or a quarterback injury somewhere that turns a sixth-round market into a second-round market. Any one of those and we revise publicly.

And the price that would actually move him.

The probability question and the price question are the same question, and this market has already answered it in public.

Seven trades have closed in this league since late September with published terms. One of them is a near-perfect comparable, and nobody in this conversation has mentioned it.

J.J. McCarthy, quarterback, Vikings to Giants, September twenty-eighth. Minnesota received one twenty twenty-seven fifth-round pick. McCarthy was the tenth overall selection of the twenty twenty-four draft, on a first-round rookie contract, sitting third on a depth chart. A former top-ten quarterback pick, with control, moved for a single Day Three pick.

That is the market rate for a young quarterback who is not playing. One fifth.

Now stack the rest of the board against it. Marvin Harrison Junior brought a twenty twenty-seven second and a sixth. Joey Porter Junior brought a sixth and a twenty twenty-eight second. DeForest Buckner, a three-time Pro Bowler, brought a single twenty twenty-seven fourth. And at the top, Jeffery Simmons brought Tennessee two first-round picks and a fourth, because a buyer got four more contract years, not because of reputation.

So here is the arithmetic that sets our probability. Shedeur Sanders is a fifth-round pick in year two who has not played. The comparable sale says a fifth. The Dolphins-side analysis we read floats a sixth. Cabot's threshold is "an offer they can't refuse."

A fifth is not an offer anyone cannot refuse. Neither is a sixth. For Cleveland to say yes, somebody has to pay roughly Buckner money or better, a fourth at minimum, realistically a third, for a quarterback who has thrown no meaningful passes this season, and do it while Cleveland is five and two and dressing him as its only experienced backup.

That gap between the market price and the asking price is the whole reason our number is ten to fifteen percent rather than forty-five. It is not optimism about Cleveland's intentions. It is arithmetic, the kind we would rather show you at Ktl Report than hide behind a percentage somebody else generated.

Miami's case, audited.

The underlying argument for Miami is the strongest part of this video, and it is almost entirely true. It just needs its numbers fixed.

That is a franchise that has blown up its quarterback position and committed real money to an unproven answer. A cheap young passer to evaluate alongside him is a sane idea. Wrong: the radio clip says Miami "paid him forty-four million dollars." The deal is three years, sixty-seven and a half. Forty-four may be the guarantee; we could not confirm that, so do not say it as the contract value.

The vlog, and why we are not using it.

The video builds a section on a house-hunting trip: Shilo Sanders, the correct spelling, not "Shiloh", filmed a vlog in Fort Lauderdale, a short drive from the Dolphins' facility, in which he says to his brother, "This could be your home. This could be your life, bro."

We are not carrying this, for three reasons, and they are not squeamishness.

One: it is not evidence. A brother saying a nice thing about a nice city in a family vlog is compatible with a trade, with no trade, with buying an off-season house, with filming content, and with nothing at all. It cannot distinguish between those outcomes, which is the definition of not being evidence.

Two: it is where private people live. We will report a man's contract, his snaps and his depth-chart position all day. Where his family looks at houses is not a transaction signal, and treating it as one invites an audience to go looking.

Three: the video says the quiet part itself, "you can take that as a joke between brothers. Maybe it was just a casual line." Yes. Then it spends two minutes arguing it is "very hard to call that a coincidence." Both halves cannot be the segment.

The bottom line.

Forty-five percent sounds like information. Traced back, it is an X account citing a betting market, written up by a site that said it had not verified it, and then converted into "two front offices are negotiating" by a video that admits nine minutes later that nothing is guaranteed.
