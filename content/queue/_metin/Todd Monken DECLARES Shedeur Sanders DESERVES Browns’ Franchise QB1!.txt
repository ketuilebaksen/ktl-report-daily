The August tape. What Todd Monken actually promised Shedeur Sanders, and the ledger of what it was worth.

First. This footage is from August.

We have to start here, because it changes everything about how you should watch the clip going around.

That Todd Monken press conference is from the preseason. It is roughly two months old. Here is how you can date it yourself in ten seconds, using only what is said in the audio:

He references "the Bears game", Cleveland's preseason opener. He talks about Buffalo as the next opponent, the preseason Week 2 game. He says "I don't know how many of our guys we're going to play." The analyst reacting to it says "throughout these last two preseason games" and asks "who's going to really start in game one."

There is no Week 1 in October. The Browns are three and two, they have played five regular-season games, and they host Baltimore this Sunday.

So no, Monken did not just declare Shedeur Sanders the franchise quarterback. At Ktl Report we are going to tell you what he actually said in August, what happened two days later, and what the eight weeks since have been worth, because the real story is more interesting than the recycled one, and it ends with a question that is genuinely live today.

What Monken actually said, and it is to his credit.

Let's quote him properly, because this part deserves respect rather than inflation.

"Going into the last game it was relatively close in terms of both having an opportunity at the starting position. So it would be unfair to not go into the next game and allow Shedeur that opportunity, albeit a different team, certainly at home. it's the closest you have, and we owe it to both players who have really handled it well and have competed at a high level."

Read that for what it is. It is not a coronation. It is a head coach explaining why fairness required giving the rookie the same preseason start the veteran had just been given. Same first-team group, same real opponent, one at home and one on the road.

That is a genuinely admirable thing to say out loud, and it was unusual. Plenty of coaches would have handed the veteran both starts and called it a competition. Monken split first-team reps through camp and gave each quarterback a start. The word he used was "owe."

What he did not say, anywhere, in any form: that Shedeur Sanders is the franchise quarterback, the starter, or the future. Those words are supplied by the thumbnail.

August twenty-second. The audition.

Shedeur Sanders started the preseason game against the Buffalo Bills on August 22, 2026.

His line: nine of eleven for 67 yards, one touchdown, an eight yard pass to Luke Floriea, the Kent State receiver who became a training-camp favorite, and one interception, picked off by Damar Hamlin on a short throw over the middle. He also ran once for two yards.

Nine of eleven is an eighty-one point eight percent completion rate. It was, by any fair reading, a good audition. The coverage at the time said he shined, and he did.

It was also 67 yards in a preseason game, against a defense that was neither game-planning nor showing anything exotic. Both of those sentences are true, and anyone selling you only one of them is selling you something.

August twenty-fourth. The verdict, two days later.

And here is the fact that the recycled clip quietly leaves out.

Two days after that start, on August 24, 2026, the Cleveland Browns named Deshaun Watson their starting quarterback.

Monken's explanation: the staff weighed the full body of work and "felt like to start the season on the road, Deshaun gave us the best chance to move the ball and score points." On the rookie, he was generous and specific, the staff believed Watson had been ahead through the offseason and camp, but Sanders "has closed that gap, for sure," and Monken credited that to Sanders himself.

And the line that matters most for today: Monken said the decision was not a week by week call.

So the actual arc of August is the reverse of the headline. Shedeur Sanders got his fair audition, played well in it, and the job went to the veteran forty-eight hours later, permanently, by the head coach's own framing.

So what was the promise worth?

This is the honest accounting, and it cuts in an unexpected direction.

Monken kept his word exactly. He said both quarterbacks deserved an equal shot. He split first-team reps. He gave each one a start with the ones. He named a starter on the basis of the full body of work and explained his reasoning in public. Then he stopped re-litigating it.

That is what a fair process looks like. Anyone arguing that Shedeur Sanders was denied a fair competition in 2026 has to explain away a split-reps camp, a start against Buffalo, and a head coach who used the word "owe."

So the complaint cannot be about the process. The process was clean. The complaint, and we think it is a legitimate one, is about everything that has happened since. Because Monken's own standard in August was that you cannot evaluate a quarterback you do not let play. That standard was applied in August. It has not been applied once since.

And here is the ledger since.

What the two men have actually done in the regular season, with no adjectives.

Deshaun Watson started all five games. He led a game-winning drive in three consecutive weeks, against Tampa Bay, Carolina and Pittsburgh, becoming the first Cleveland quarterback to do that since Brian Sipe in 1979, a 47-year gap. He is completing sixty-five point oh percent for the season. And on Sunday he went fifteen of thirty-three for 155 yards, took eight sacks, and the offense gained 198 net yards in a seventeen to twelve loss to a one and three Jets team.

Shedeur Sanders has thrown one pass. It gained one yard. Red zone, Week 3, against Carolina.

That is the whole ledger. The August promise bought him an audition and a fair hearing. It bought him nothing afterward, because nothing in the August arrangement obliged anybody to keep looking.

The part of the August argument that still holds.

One observation from that old clip has aged well, and it is worth rescuing.

The analyst said Cleveland had "probably the only really competitive quarterback situation" in the league, the only team where nobody could confidently name the Week 1 starter. That was true then, and the residue of it is still true now: no other team in football is carrying four quarterbacks and fielding trade questions about two of them in October.

And Monken's own phrase has quietly become the central fact of the season: the decision was "not a week by week call." In August that sounded like stability, and it was the right call, constant quarterback re-evaluation destroys offenses. Eight weeks later the same sentence means something heavier: there is no mechanism by which the evaluation reopens. Not a bad game, not 198 yards, not eight sacks. The job was settled in August and the structure contains no door.

That is why we keep arguing for the smallest possible version of a door: three to five designed snaps in neutral situations. Not a competition, not a benching, a package. It is the only thing that generates new information without undoing the stability Monken correctly chose to protect.

Not a week by week call is the most important sentence of Cleveland's season.

It deserves its own section, because that one clause has quietly governed everything from Week 1 to today.

What it bought Cleveland, and it is real: stability. An offense whose quarterback is re-evaluated every seven days cannot install anything. Linemen cannot build timing with a rotating cadence. Receivers cannot build a feel for the ball. Coaches cannot commit to a system. Teams that run public weekly quarterback referendums usually finish four and thirteen with three quarterbacks who all look bad. Monken closed that door on purpose and he was right to.

What it cost, and it is also real: there is now no trigger. Think about how a starting job normally reopens in the NFL. An injury does it. A benching mid-game does it. Mathematical elimination does it, once a season is dead, teams look at young players. A public competition structure does it by design.

Cleveland has deliberately removed three of those four and cannot manufacture the fifth. Watson is healthy. Monken has not benched him and gave no indication he would after Sunday, saying four times that he had not watched the tape. And at three and two, leading or near the top of the division, Cleveland is nowhere close to elimination, which, ironically, is the thing we flagged in Garrett Bush's analysis: winning closes the door that losing would open.

So the honest structural picture is this: barring an injury nobody should wish for, there is no event on Cleveland's calendar that reopens the quarterback evaluation. Not a bad game. Not eight sacks. Not 198 yards. August decided it, and August built no door.

A three play package is not a crack in that door. It is a window, small enough to preserve everything the "not week by week" decision was designed to protect, large enough to produce information that does not currently exist.

And what a fair audition is worth on the trade market. Nothing.

Here is the commercial consequence of August that nobody discussed at the time.

Shedeur Sanders' most recent meaningful football, the only extended sample any rival front office can study from 2026, is nine of eleven for 67 yards against Buffalo in a preseason game.

Ask any evaluator what preseason tape is worth and you will get the same answer: very little. Defenses play vanilla coverages. Nobody game-plans. Blitz packages stay in the folder. The players across from him in the fourth quarter are mostly not on an NFL roster by October. A good preseason performance tells you a quarterback can execute an install against a defense that is not trying to disguise anything, which is a real but very small thing.

Now connect it to the number we have been writing about for a month. The floated price for Sanders is a fifth-round pick, the same round he was drafted in. Why? Because a buyer looking at his file sees seven starts from last season, one pass in 2026, and a preseason game against Buffalo.

August gave him a fair hearing. It did not give him an asset. That distinction is the whole reason the "three to five snaps" argument is a business argument and not a sentimental one: preseason completions do not move a price, and regular-season ones do.

The three things August got right.

To be fair to the old clip, and to the analyst in it, who was more careful than most, three observations from two months ago have held up well.

One: Cleveland really did have the league's only genuinely open quarterback competition. That was unusual then and it remains the root of everything happening now, including a four-man room in October and two twenty-one day clocks.

Two: the even-handedness was correct. The analyst's line, "I thought they both made mistakes. they all got some room for improvement", is a far better read than anything either fan camp has produced since. Both men made errors in the preseason. Both have made errors since. That has never stopped being true.

Three: the money was always going to be a factor. Not the deciding one in August, as we said above, but the 230 million dollars and the 86.2 million dollars 2027 charge were always going to shape the 2027 decision. Mary Kay Cabot has since laid out the three live paths, let Watson walk, a short-term bridge deal, or another multi-year contract, and the money sits underneath all three.

A clip can be two months stale and still contain good analysis. The problem is never the analyst. It is the headline bolted onto him in October.

A footnote. The man who caught that touchdown.

One small thing that connects August to this week, and we think it is worth thirty seconds.

The eight yard touchdown Shedeur Sanders threw against Buffalo went to Luke Floriea, the Kent State receiver who became a training-camp favorite in Cleveland.

Floriea is still unsigned. When the Browns needed a returner this month after Tylan Wallace's knee injury, they signed James Proche II off the street instead, a reasonable choice, because Proche has 68 career punt returns and Floriea does not. We wrote that at the time and we stand by it.

But it is a reminder of how thin the margins are at the bottom of a roster. Two months ago a rookie quarterback and an undrafted receiver connected for a touchdown in front of a stadium full of people who wanted to believe in both of them. Today one of them has thrown a single pass and the other is waiting for a phone call.

That is the NFL in October, and it is worth remembering before anybody gets too confident about what any August evening proved.

And the part that has not aged well.

The weakest claim in that August conversation was that Watson was leaning as the starter only because of money, "a fat contract sitting on the books instead of actual dominance on the field. that's a paycheck talking."

Whatever you thought of that in August, the September record answered it. A quarterback who leads three consecutive game-winning drives, something no Brown had done since Jimmy Carter was president, did not hold the job with a cap sheet. He held it on the field.

We will also say the obvious: the contract is enormous and it does distort everything. 230 million dollar fully guaranteed, final year, an 86.2 million dollar charge against the 2027 cap. The money is a real factor in Cleveland's planning. It just was not the reason Watson beat out a fifth-round rookie in August, and pretending otherwise makes the argument easier to dismiss than it should be.

How to date a clip in ten seconds.

Since preseason footage is being repackaged with October headlines, here is the toolkit. It takes almost no effort and it will save you from the next one.

Listen for opponents. Preseason opponents are different from regular-season ones. "Bears," "Bills" and "Patriots" were Cleveland's preseason slate; Jacksonville, Tampa Bay, Carolina, Pittsburgh and the Jets are the real ones so far.

Listen for the words "game one" or "week one." Nobody says those in October.

Listen for players who are no longer in that situation. This clip discusses a quarterback competition that formally ended on August 24.

Check the records. If the discussion has no record attached, no "three and two," no mention of a loss, it is almost certainly from before the season.

And the simplest test of all: has the thing been reported anywhere today? A head coach naming a franchise quarterback would be the biggest story in the NFL within four minutes. If the only place it exists is a thumbnail, it did not happen.

What is actually true today.

For the record, here is the genuinely current news, all of it verified this week.

Adam Schefter reported on October 14 that Shedeur Sanders signed with agent Adie von Gontard of Young Money APAA Sports, his first representation, after going through the draft without an agent.

Cleveland is three and two and hosts the Baltimore Ravens on Sunday, October 18, with Lamar Jackson's sprained ankle still a question after he missed Week 5.

Eight players did not practice Wednesday, including Mason Graham, Maliek Collins, Quinshon Judkins and Carson Schwesinger. KC Concepcion practiced three days after a concussion. Raheim Sanders went on injured reserve, Audric Estime was signed off Minnesota's practice squad, and Dylan Sampson's twenty-one day window opened, meaning two twenty-one day clocks now run alongside Dillon Gabriel's.

None of that is a quarterback declaration. All of it is more consequential than one.

The question August actually left behind.

Here is where we land, and it is not where either side of this argument usually lands.

Todd Monken's August conduct was better than his critics allow and more limited than his boosters claim. He ran a genuinely fair competition, he said out loud that he owed the rookie an equal look, he gave him one, and then he made a decision and defended it by name. That is how it is supposed to work.

But a fair process in August does not create information in October. "Has closed that gap, for sure" was a real evaluation of a real sample. That sample is now two months old, and nothing has been added to it except one pass for one yard.

So the honest question is not whether Shedeur Sanders should be the starter, he should not be, not while the man ahead of him has three game-winning drives in a season where Cleveland has won three games. The question is whether a head coach who believed in August that you owe a quarterback a look still believes it in October, when a look costs three plays instead of a start.

That is the one thing the August tape genuinely has to say to the present. It is just not the thing the thumbnail says.

Where we stop.

"Monken declared Shedeur the franchise QB1." He did not, in August or since. Two days after the quotes in that clip, he named Deshaun Watson the starter.

"This is breaking news." The footage is from the preseason, before the August 24 announcement.

"Shedeur was denied a fair shot." Split first-team reps through camp and a start against Buffalo. The process was fair; the eight weeks after it are the argument.

"Watson only has the job because of money." He leads the league's longest active streak of game-winning drives by a Brown since 1979. He won it on the field.

"Shedeur's preseason proved he's ready." Nine of 11 for 67 yards with an interception is a good preseason performance. It is not proof of anything in October, in either direction.

Any prediction about Sunday's quarterback. Watson is the starter. Nobody in or around the building has suggested otherwise.

The bottom line.

In August, Todd Monken said it would be unfair not to give Shedeur Sanders the same starting opportunity Deshaun Watson had just received, because the team owed it to both men. He meant it. Sanders started against Buffalo on August 22 and went nine of eleven for 67 yards with a touchdown to Luke Floriea and an interception.

On August 24, Monken named Watson the starter, said Sanders "has closed that gap, for sure," and made clear the decision was not a week by week call.

Everything since has been the consequence of that last clause. Watson has produced the most remarkable fourth-quarter stretch by a Browns quarterback in 47 years and one of the worst offensive afternoons of the decade, in the space of five games. Sanders has thrown one pass.

The clip going around is two months old and the headline on it is the opposite of what happened. But buried inside it is the sharpest argument anybody has made for the thing we keep asking for, and it comes from the head coach himself. He said the team owed a quarterback a look.

In August that cost him a preseason start. In October it would cost him three plays.
