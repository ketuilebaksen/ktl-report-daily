One hundredth out of one hundred thirteen.

We at Ktl Report sorted that ledger twice to be sure of it, and the picture is complete: Cleveland's pass rush is a tackle, a tackle, a linebacker and a scheme. It is not an edge rush. It has not been an edge rush all season.

And then the sentence that turns a statistic into a football problem, which is the best writing in the video:

That is the whole thing. Interior pressure wins you a down. An edge rusher who can beat one man on his own wins you a game, because in the last two minutes, with the game on the line, the blitz is a risk you cannot keep taking.

Why the column is empty. The three things that were supposed to fill it.

The video's reconstruction here is clean and it is the clearest account of Cleveland's edge situation we have seen anywhere.

One. The best in the building was traded.

"just last year the Browns had the best edge rusher in the history of the NFL. Last season, Myles Garrett had twenty-three sacks all by himself. The whole team had fifty-three, which was third most in the entire NFL. Then on June the first, Cleveland traded him to the Rams for a draft pick and Jared Verse."

Twenty-three sacks from one man out of a team total of fifty-three. Forty-three percent of an entire defence's sacks came from one player.

And this is the sentence we have been missing all season. Two days ago we reported that Jared Verse came from the Rams in the Myles Garrett trade. This video now gives us the date and the full shape of the deal: June the first, Garrett to Los Angeles, Cleveland receiving a draft pick and Jared Verse. That is the transaction that created the empty column.

Two. The man he was traded for has not filled it.

"Verse was supposed to be the next guy off that edge. So far this season in Cleveland, he has zero sacks. Through eight games, PFF had him as thirteen pressures on two hundred pass rush snaps and still no sacks. Verse has even said that the drought was a mental block and that he worked on it with a sports psychiatrist."

Thirteen pressures on two hundred pass-rush snaps, and zero sacks.

And note how careful that framing is, and how we are going to keep it that way: those are Jared Verse's own words about Jared Verse. He described it as a mental block. He said he worked on it. We have refused to interpret this man's state of mind for three straight articles and we are refusing again. He said it; it is quoted; that is the end of it.

Three. The backup plan tore an Achilles.

"The backup plan was Alex Wright who had five and a half sacks last year. That's pretty solid. But Wright went down with an Achilles injury in the preseason and he was done for the year. So that pushed Derek Barnett into a bigger role and through eight games he had fifteen pressures on ninety-four pass rush snaps. The sack column next to those pressures though still reads zero."

Five and a half sacks removed before the season started.

And Derek Barnett's line is the strangest number in the entire video: fifteen pressures on ninety-four pass-rush snaps, a better pressure rate than Verse's, and zero sacks.

Add it up. Twenty-three sacks traded away. Five and a half lost to an Achilles in August. And a two-time Pro Bowler at zero. That is not bad luck in one column. That is the column being dismantled three different ways in six months.

And the forward projection: "That had the Browns on pace for forty-nine sacks, down from last year's total." Forty-nine against fifty-three. A four-sack decline after trading away a man who had twenty-three of them is, honestly, a coaching achievement, and the video says so in its own way: "keep in mind Garrett was completely carrying the load all year. And now the Browns are getting zero help off the edge."

The deadline decision, and why we agree with this video.

Here is where the video does something we almost never see: it argues against its own frustration and lands somewhere fair.

"Before the trade deadline, the Browns were linked to Cardinals edge rusher Josh Sweat. Sweat is currently in the second year of a four-year, seventy-six point four million dollar deal. That's what a real pass rusher costs.... However, Sweat is on a very large contract carrying a twenty-three point six million dollar cap hit in twenty twenty-seven. So getting a player like that would cost real money and real draft capital. So I'm not entirely mad that Cleveland didn't go after him."

And then the comparison that makes the point properly:

"Other contenders were paying real prices. Chicago went out and traded for Jeffery Simmons at the end of October and they gave up two first round picks."

Two first-round picks.

That is the market price for a proven interior rusher at a deadline, and it is the number every fan who wanted Cleveland to "just go get somebody" needs to sit with. A six-and-four team with a rookie-heavy roster giving up two first-round picks is not aggression, it is mortgaging the thing that is actually working.

And remember what we established in an earlier article about sunk costs and deadline logic: the picks and money you have already spent tell you nothing about what you should do next. But the picks you have not yet spent are the only currency a young team has.

So the honest ledger on the deadline is this: Cleveland declined to pay seventy-six million dollars of someone else's contract, with a twenty-three point six million dollar cap charge arriving in twenty twenty-seven, plus draft capital, for a rental in a season where they were four and four at the time. And then they solved the same problem three weeks later for the price of a roster spot.

Four seasons at Rutgers, then a transfer to James Madison in twenty twenty-two.

Undrafted. Signed by Chicago as a free agent in twenty twenty-four.
Since then: the Bears, the Patriots, the Bears again, the Dolphins, and now the Browns.

And look what just happened to the Simmons trade: it appears twice in the same story. Chicago gave up two first-round picks for an interior lineman, and the roster spot that paid for it belonged to this man. He is, quite literally, the player Chicago's aggression cost, and Cleveland just picked him up for free.

The video's verdict is measured and we endorse it:

"the Browns didn't necessarily find a locked-in solid starter on the waiver wire, but we did find a guy who had a real college resume and a guy that's also not really gotten the chance to shine."

Eleven sacks and twenty and a half tackles for loss in a season is a real college resume. Three tackles in four professional games is not a professional one. Both things are true and the gap between them is the entire bet.

The depth chart, which is the real argument for the move.

This is the part of the video that convinced us, and it is pure roster mechanics.

"On one side, it was supposed to be Jared Verse with Derek Barnett and Sam Williams behind him, and on the other side, it was supposed to be Alex Wright with Isaiah McGuire and Adin Huntington behind him. Take Wright out of the equation and we're already down one of our best players. And the other guy who was supposed to be the best player still hasn't even recorded a sack yet. So that's the real problem with this group. It's thin. So the same few guys have to carry the rushing downs."

Two sides, three men each, six total. Remove Wright entirely and remove Verse's production, and you are asking four men to do the work of six.

And then the thesis, which is modest and therefore believable:

"Kromah doesn't have to be Myles Garrett to help with that. He just needs to be a fresh body who can take the load off of Verse and McGuire so they still have legs when the game is on the line late."

That is the correct standard for a waiver claim and we want to underline it, because it is the opposite of how these moves are usually sold.

Nobody is claiming he will produce sacks. The argument is that he will produce rest, ten or fifteen snaps a game that Verse and McGuire do not have to take, so that in the fourth quarter of a one-score game the men you need are not running on empty. On a defence ranked twenty-sixth against the run, a fresh two-hundred-seventy-five-pound body also helps on early downs.

And the timing is the best part:

"Miami waived him on Monday and Cleveland's claim went through on Tuesday. So it tells you that this front office knows exactly where this roster is weak, even after sitting out at the deadline.... And Kromah also gets extra time to learn this defense because the Browns are on bye this week."

Claimed on the first day he was available, into a bye week. Two weeks of installation before he has to play a snap. That is as good as the timing gets for a player joining his fifth organisation.

What we are not carrying.

We are not carrying "new signing." This was a waiver claim, which is a different mechanism: no negotiation, no money committed beyond the existing contract, no agent involved. You raise your hand and the player is yours. Saying "signing" makes a free look sound like an investment.

We are not carrying "Carsen Ryan was released." He was waived, he cleared, and he was back on the practice squad in two days, for the third time this season.

We are not carrying "Kromah fixes the pass rush." Three tackles in four professional games. The video does not claim it either: "he's not the long-term answer."

We are not carrying "the Browns should have signed Josh Sweat." Seventy-six point four million dollars, a twenty-three point six million dollar cap hit in twenty twenty-seven, plus draft capital, and Chicago's comparable move cost two first-round picks.

We are not carrying any interpretation of Jared Verse's mental health. He described a mental block and said he worked on it. Those are his words. Nothing is added to them.

We are not carrying "third in the AFC North" until somebody checks the table.

And we are not carrying the sack total until two sources agree on it. Twenty-six or twenty-seven, nine games or ten, one of those videos is wrong and it is not our job to average them.

The video asks a good question and we will pass it on exactly as it was asked: who gets the most sacks off the edge for Cleveland the rest of the way, the new man, Jared Verse, or Isaiah McGuire?

But here at Ktl Report we think there is a sharper one underneath it.

Cleveland is tied for fourth in the NFL in sacks with a pass rush that does not include an edge rusher. The production is coming from two defensive tackles, an off-ball linebacker and a scheme. The edge column has three sacks in it, and the one man with four is graded one hundredth out of one hundred thirteen as a pass rusher.

So: is this defence good, or is this defence a very clever answer to a problem it should not have?

Because both readings are defensible. You can say a coaching staff that produces a top-five sack rate without an edge rush is doing extraordinary work. Or you can say that in January, against a good offensive line, scheme pressure gets picked up and the man who beats one block on his own is the only thing that still works.

And the real question for the offseason, which the video raises and we agree with: if you were running this front office, do you spend the first big cheque on an edge rusher, or do you believe the twenty-three sacks you traded away are already being replaced by committee? Tell us in the comments.

The bottom line.

The title is honest and the video underneath it is better than the title. Cleveland claimed a defensive end off waivers from Miami on Tuesday, a claim, not a signing, and the roster spot came, for the third time this season, from rookie tight end Carsen Ryan, who cleared waivers and was back on the practice squad two days later. That makes ten transactions involving one twenty-two-year-old, and it confirms the nine-move log we published on Tuesday down to the last entry. "The roster spot the front office borrows" is the right description, and the risk is real: three for three is a streak, not a guarantee.

But the reason for all of it is one fraction. Twenty-six sacks, tied fourth in the NFL, and only three of them from an edge rusher.

Sort the ledger and the picture is stark. Mason Graham, a defensive tackle, four. Maliek Collins, a defensive tackle, three and a half. Carson Schwesinger, an off-ball linebacker, three. And on the edge: Isaiah McGuire with four, graded one hundredth out of one hundred thirteen as a pass rusher; Jared Verse at zero; Derek Barnett at zero. Cleveland's pass rush is two tackles, a linebacker and a scheme, and as the video puts it, "late in a close game when you need one guy to beat his man off of the edge and end a drive, there's nobody right now who can do that every single week."

The column emptied three ways in six months. Myles Garrett, who had twenty-three of the team's fifty-three sacks last year, was traded to the Rams on June the first for a draft pick and Jared Verse. Verse has thirteen pressures on two hundred pass-rush snaps and no sacks, and described the drought in his own words as a mental block he worked on. And Alex Wright, five and a half sacks a year ago, tore an Achilles in the preseason. Derek Barnett's line is the oddest of all: fifteen pressures on ninety-four snaps, zero sacks.

Cleveland declined to pay for the expensive fix, Josh Sweat, four years and seventy-six point four million dollars, with a twenty-three point six million dollar cap hit waiting in twenty twenty-seven, in a market where Chicago gave up two first-round picks for Jeffery Simmons. And then it took the free one. The man they claimed had eleven sacks and twenty and a half tackles for loss at James Madison and has three tackles in four professional games, and he is here precisely because Chicago needed a roster spot for the Simmons trade.

The standard the video sets for him is the right one, and we will hold him to it and nothing more: "He doesn't have to be Myles Garrett. He just needs to be a fresh body who can take the load off of Verse and McGuire so they still have legs when the game is on the line late."

Six and four. A bye week to learn the defence. And Las Vegas on November twenty-ninth, which is now confirmed as the Sunday Night Football game, the question we left open in our last two articles. A division this tight is decided late in close games, and close games are decided by the one column Cleveland cannot fill.
