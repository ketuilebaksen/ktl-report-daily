The excuse he wouldn't take. Five alibis available to Todd Monken, and what he did with each one.

No decision was announced, but the best exchange of the season happened.

Another headline promising a shocking Week 7 decision. Again, none was announced. Monken gave injury updates and answered questions.

But something genuinely remarkable happened in that press conference, and nobody has clipped it.

A reporter handed Todd Monken a complete, reasonable, pre-packaged excuse for everything wrong with his offense. Not a hostile question, a generous one. The kind of question a coach prays for. And Monken took about a third of it and pushed the rest back across the table.

That exchange is worth more than any depth-chart rumour, so Ktl Report is going to build today's article around a question nobody asks: what alibis are actually available to this coach, and what has he done with each of them?

There are five. He has used none of them cleanly. And then, because refusing excuses is not the same as fixing anything, we will put the one new number against him that is worse than the sacks, and preview a game that kicks off this afternoon in Nashville.

The alibi he was handed, in full.

Here is the question, almost verbatim. A reporter suggested that the early offensive struggles could be down to Cleveland being brand new in every way on offense, so new that the staff cannot know how opposing coordinators will attack until the game starts. Players keep saying they saw more of this coverage or that shell. Is it an adventure every week?

That is a free pass with a bow on it. Accept it and every slow start for the rest of the season has a built-in explanation.

Monken's answer:

"Some of that, I think, is true. I think some of that would be an excuse. I think there's a part of that that we're trying to feel our way with our personnel, who's playing, what we're calling, what we're designing. But there's also a part of that that you can't take that too far and exonerate yourself from the things where we can put our players in the best position to be successful. And you don't get to where you're at without owning it as a coach and saying we certainly have to be better."

"Exonerate yourself." That is not a word a man reaches for by accident. He identified the offer as an acquittal and declined it on the record, in public, two days before a game he is favoured to win.

So here is the inventory.

Every struggling coach in football has a drawer of these. Monken has five available to him, all of them legitimate, all of them things fans would accept. Here is what he has done with each.

Alibi one. We're brand new. Took a third, refused the rest.

Why it would work: it is true. New head coach, new offensive coordinator in Travis Switzer, new quarterbacks coach in Mike Bajakian, new line coach in George Warhop, a new scheme, and five new offensive line starters, two of them rookies. Nobody would blink.

What he did: conceded the part that is real, "we're trying to feel our way with our personnel", and refused the part that would absolve him. Then he named his own fixes again: "we got to scheme it better, got to coach it better, got to execute better, certainly early in the game, because it's below the line."

He also repeated the phrase that has become the season's label: "we're still under construction." Note that he uses it as a description, never as a defence.

Alibi two. Wins are hard to come by. Never touched.

Why it would work: Cleveland is four and two and tied for first in the AFC North for the first time this late since 2014. Every coach in the league has this sentence in his pocket, and most of them use it after a division win.

What he did: after beating Baltimore he went the other way entirely, describing lying awake at half past ten not wanting the night to end, "because tomorrow I've got to look at the offensive tape." He called 46 first-half yards "below the line" and said of that half, "I really don't know what to say."

A coach grading a win on the curve of winning does not say that.

Alibi three. The line is young and hurt. True, and unused.

Why it would work: it is the single most defensible excuse available. Joel Bitonio retired in June. Teven Jenkins has not played a game all season. The interior today is Zion Johnson at left guard, Elgton Jenkins at centre and the rookie Austin Barber at right guard. Cleveland has allowed 24 sacks, the most in the National Football League.

What he did: broke five sacks into three causes and put his own call sheet in the list, "protection, calls, players, quarterback, everybody." He has never once said the line is too young to protect anybody, which is the easiest true thing he could say.

Alibi four. I'm the head coach and the play caller. Offered, and redirected.

This one came up in the same press conference, and the answer was the most human part of it.

A reporter asked how he manages calling an offense while also being responsible for a whole team. Monken's answer was to credit everybody else. He said he feels lucky, naming Byron Storer on special teams and his defensive staff, and that their success is precisely what lets him concentrate on fixing the offense. "The more those areas are successful, the easier it is to really try to focus on how can we fix what we're doing offensively."

Then he told on himself about the one game where it genuinely broke down: the Jets, in the rain. "That was the biggest one, the communication there, and then the call sheet itself." A soaked play sheet, and he said so out loud.

And the detail that shows how granular it gets: during a game he will flip over to special teams to check which hash mark Andre Szmyt wants the ball on before a field goal. "Where does Dre want the ball?"

Alibi five. The injuries. The easiest one, never used.

Why it would work: Cleveland beat Baltimore with its fourth and fifth running backs, having lost Quinshon Judkins, Dylan Sampson and Raheim Sanders. It did it without Mason Graham and Mike Hall Junior, and after losing Grant Delpit to a concussion in the first quarter. It has played all season without Teven Jenkins.

What he did: turned it into a compliment to the players. Asked what it says that Graham, Judkins and Carson Schwesinger have all pushed to get back, he said: "I think we got a lot of guys that are wired the right way... not wanting to let their brother down." And he spread the credit, "being connected helps that. I think winning helps that. I think having a strong coaching staff that supports the players."

Five alibis. Not one of them cashed.

And the one thing he does lean on, which is not an excuse at all.

There is a sixth item, and the distinction matters, because a coach who claims nothing is just as unserious as one who claims everything.

In the same breath where he refused the alibi, Monken pointed at the one thing his offense has genuinely earned:

"We've been good when we've needed it and we haven't turned the ball over, which is a recipe to giving yourself a chance... as long as we've ended the possession with a kick, we've certainly not given them anything. And Bojo's been a weapon punting."

That is Corey Bojorquez, and it is the most Cleveland sentence of the season: a head coach naming his punter as an offensive asset. It sounds like a joke until you look at the arithmetic. Cleveland has gone four of its last five games without an offensive turnover. Three of its four wins were by one score. A team that never hands the opponent a short field, and whose punter flips the field when the drive dies, can survive an offense ranked twenty-seventh in scoring.

Notice the shape of the claim, though. He is not saying "we are fine." He is saying "we have not lost games by giving them away." That is a floor, not a ceiling, and he said so himself in the next sentence: "we certainly have a ways to go, and that's starting with coaches, and then players and scheme."

An excuse explains a failure. This explains a survival. They are different things, and he kept them separate.

And why this actually matters, beyond the nice words.

It would be easy to file all of this under character and move on. It is worth being more concrete than that, because the refusal has a mechanism.

An accepted excuse stops the search. The moment a staff agrees that slow starts are caused by not knowing what coverage an opponent will show, the question "why are we slow?" has an answer, and nobody looks for the real one. That is how a problem survives a season. Monken's "you can't take that too far and exonerate yourself" is, in practical terms, a refusal to close the investigation.

And players hear everything. A room where the head coach says the opening script is his to fix is a room where a left guard cannot say the scheme let him down. Monken has now put the order on the record twice, "it starts with me, and then our offensive staff, and then the guys that we put on the field." Every player in that building has heard it, which is why "wired the right way" is something they can say back to him without it sounding like a slogan.

The cost is that he now owns the next slow start publicly, with no cover left. He has pre-agreed to it. That is the trade, and he made it on purpose.

And now the counterweight, because it is not a free pass either.

Here is where Ktl Report has to be as honest about the coach as he has been about himself.

Refusing an excuse is not the same as fixing a problem. Accountability is a prerequisite, not an achievement. And the scoreboard has not moved.

Cleveland averages one point per game in the first quarter, thirtieth in the National Football League. Eighteen-point-six points a game, twenty-seventh. Twenty-four sacks allowed, the league high.

And there is a new number this week that is arguably worse than all of those.

The number that is worse than the sacks.

Cleveland converts thirty point eight percent of its third downs. That is last in the NFL.

Sit with that for a second, because it explains more than the sack total does. Third down is where drives live or die; it is the conversion rate that decides how many plays an offense runs, how long a defense rests, and how many times a tight end like Harold Fannin ever gets a route thrown to him.

Last. Not twenty-seventh, not bottom five. Thirty-second.

Put it beside Monken's own diagnosis, that first-down efficiency is the root of everything, and the chain is complete. Inefficient first down, long second down, undefendable third down, punt. That is the whole offense, and it is why a man who refuses excuses still has one point a quarter on his record.

The matchup that decides this afternoon.

And today it meets the worst possible opponent for it.

Jeffery Simmons was a first team All-Pro in 2025. He has four sacks and six tackles for loss this season. Deshaun Watson's own description of him this week was blunt: "a very, very game wrecker."

Simmons lines up inside. Cleveland's inside is Zion Johnson, Elgton Jenkins, who missed Wednesday with a knee, and a rookie right guard in Austin Barber. Against a line that has already surrendered a league-high 24 sacks, that is the game.

If Cleveland protects the interior today against the worst scoring offense in football, the first-quarter problem is fixable. If Simmons lives in the backfield, nothing else on the call sheet matters.

Meanwhile the defense keeps answering.

The other half of this team continues to make its own case, and two numbers tell it.

Cleveland's defense has 39 tackles for loss, the most in the National Football League. And it is second in the league with 19 sacks, with eleven different players having recorded at least one.

Eleven. That is the whole story of this unit in one figure. It is not one superstar bending a game; it is a deep rotation getting home from everywhere, which is exactly the bet Cleveland made in June when it traded Myles Garrett to the Rams for Jared Verse and three picks.

We are still not calling that trade won, and neither is the head coach. But a defense leading football in tackles for loss, with eleven men contributing sacks, is no longer a unit anybody needs to apologise for.

And Mason Graham set his own standard.

The best quote of the week came from a player, not a coach.

Mason Graham returns today after ankle and knee sprains suffered early in the Week 4 win over Pittsburgh. He was limited Wednesday and Thursday and practised fully on Friday.

And here is the number that makes the return matter: Graham still leads the Browns with four sacks, all of them recorded in the first three games, plus six tackles for loss, despite missing nearly three weeks.

He called the injury "just a little bump on the road." On missing time: "I wanted to be out there, but it is what it is." And then the line that belongs in this article: he said he wanted to play against Baltimore, but did not think he would have met his own standard, so he waited another week.

A second-year player choosing not to play because he would not have been good enough by his own measure is the same instinct as a head coach refusing an alibi. Monken's reaction to getting him back was simply relief that the scans showed nothing worse: "he was off to such a great start... you can feel him on the field."

And the Maliek Collins story, which is about the same thing.

Monken told one story at length on Friday, and it was about a 30-something defensive tackle.

He said he voted Maliek Collins All-Pro last season, before he had ever worked with him, because he was "super disruptive playing the run and the pass." Then Collins told him he was going to spend the offseason in Houston, working on his own, near his family. Monken's private reaction was exactly what you would expect: "Yeah, right. This is going to stink. We need this guy to come back and be ready to go."

Collins came back ahead of schedule. The staff kept reporting he was ahead of schedule. Monken was told a return would take four weeks and watched him jumping onto boxes on day one.

His verdict: "That says a lot about him as a man, to not be here in the building and get the work done. That's rare. That's not normal."

Why it belongs in an article about excuses: Collins had the best available one, he was not in the building, and he did the work anyway. Monken told that story unprompted, to a room full of reporters, two days before a game. Coaches tell the stories they want copied.

And the one thing that still has to change. Fannin.

The sharpest number in this week's coverage is not about the quarterback.

Harold Fannin Junior has 19 catches for 166 yards and three touchdowns, and exactly one catch in each of the last two games.

One. Twice. From a tight end who had 30 catches in seven games last season.

Monken's position is that this is a team problem rather than a Fannin problem, and the third-down number backs him: an offense converting thirty point eight percent of its third downs does not run enough plays to feed anybody. But "the offense will get better and he will too" has now been the answer for three weeks. At some point getting him the ball is the fix, not the reward for a fix.

And the statuses for today.

Denzel Ward is questionable with a finger injury. Monken did not know how it happened, "I just know that when we met afterwards, something happened to his finger." His read: "Knowing Denzel, I would expect him to play, but we'll see." It matters, because Tennessee's Carnell Tate, the fourth overall pick, has 29 catches for 344 yards. If Ward cannot go, Tyson Campbell likely draws him.

Grant Delpit has one final hurdle in concussion protocol, which Monken said could clear as early as Saturday.

And the headline for the offense: Quinshon Judkins and Dylan Sampson are set to be available together for the first time since the opener. Monken pointed out that Sampson was lost essentially on the opening kickoff of Week 1. His assessment was characteristically unromantic: "there's a lot of things we need to do better on offense, but to have those guys back together should be a boost."

And then Tuesday.

Two days after this game, Dillon Gabriel's twenty-one day practice window closes on October 27. Activate him to the 53-man roster, or he reverts to injured reserve for the rest of the season. Monken said the decision is due by Tuesday and will involve several members of the staff.

It will arrive as a transaction line, with no press conference, and it will say more about how this staff ranks its young quarterbacks than anything said at a podium this season.

What to watch in Nashville.

The first quarter. One point a game. Everything begins here.

Third down. thirty point eight percent, last in the league, against the worst scoring offense in football. If it does not improve today, it will not improve.

Simmons against the right guard. A first team All-Pro inside against a rookie.

And the schedule behind it: this is the first of three straight road games, Nashville, then Pittsburgh, then New Orleans. A four and two team is about to find out what it is away from home.

Scott Petrak's prediction for the afternoon: Browns twenty-three, Titans twenty.

Where we stop.

We are not going to tell you that refusing excuses wins football games. It does not. One point a quarter and the league's worst third-down rate are the record, and they belong to the man who keeps refusing to explain them away.

We are not going to tell you this offense is close. Last in third down is not close.

What we will tell you is that a reporter offered Todd Monken a complete, reasonable alibi on Friday, and he used the word "exonerate" to turn it down. Five excuses were available to him this season. He has cashed none of them.

The bottom line.

No decision was announced. What happened instead: a head coach was handed a free pass and said "some of that would be an excuse."

Five alibis available. None used. Brand new, wins are hard, the line is young, I'm doing two jobs, and the injuries, every one of them true, every one of them declined.

And the number that still indicts him: thirty point eight percent on third down, last in the National Football League. Plus one point a game in the first quarter, thirtieth, and 24 sacks allowed, the most in football.

Against that: 39 tackles for loss, the most in the league, 19 sacks from eleven different players, and Mason Graham back, still the team leader in sacks after missing three weeks.

Nashville at one o'clock, the first of three straight on the road. Then Tuesday, and Dillon Gabriel's window closes.
