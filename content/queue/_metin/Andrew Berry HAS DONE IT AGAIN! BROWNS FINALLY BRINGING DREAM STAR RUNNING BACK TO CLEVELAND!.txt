The record breaking running back Cleveland just signed, and the one already on the roster nobody is talking about.

First, who the Browns actually signed.

Let's be straight, because that is the deal at Ktl Report and because the real story is better than the rumor version.

The Browns signed a running back on October 7: Jaret Patterson, one T, not "Jerrett" or "Jarrett." He went to the practice squad, not the 53-man roster. He is 26, and in 27 NFL games he has 503 rushing yards. Nobody in Berea signed him expecting him to carry this offense.

So is he a "dream star running back"? In the NFL, no, and we will not pretend otherwise two days before a road game.

But here is the part that is completely true, and it is extraordinary: Jaret Patterson holds an FBS record that may never be broken, and he once had the kind of two-week stretch that college football will still be talking about in fifty years. The "record-breaking" half of that headline is real. It just happened in Amherst, New York, not Cleveland.

And while everyone argues about a practice-squad signing, the actual running back story in Cleveland is being ignored completely, because the best running back on this roster just had the best receiving game of his career, and almost nobody noticed. We are going to get to both.

The star part is real, in college, and it's astonishing.

If you only know Patterson as a depth signing, you are missing one of the great individual performances in the history of college football.

November 2020. Buffalo against Kent State. Patterson carried the ball 36 times for 409 yards and eight touchdowns. Eight rushing touchdowns in a single game, the FBS record. Four hundred and nine yards in sixty minutes of football.

Then, the following week, against Bowling Green: 301 yards and four more touchdowns.

Add those two games together: 710 rushing yards and 12 rushing touchdowns in eight days, which set records in both categories for a two-game stretch. He finished his Buffalo career with 3,884 rushing yards and 52 touchdowns.

And he did all of it at five foot seven.

That is not filler. It is a genuinely great football story, and the reason his name still makes people sit up, ESPN once ran a feature on him built around the phrase "don't bet against Jaret Patterson." The honest framing: Cleveland did not sign a star NFL running back. It signed a man who was once the most unstoppable back in the country and has spent five years refusing to leave the league. Different things, and the second is still worth your attention.

And now the N F L resume, accurately.

Here is the part the video circulating gets wrong in its first sentence, and it matters.

Patterson was not drafted. He entered the NFL in 2021 as an undrafted free agent with Washington out of Buffalo, the transcript says the Commanders drafted him, and they did not. He has since spent time with the Los Angeles Chargers and the Minnesota Vikings.

The career numbers: 27 games, 126 carries, 503 yards, three touchdowns, plus 13 receptions for 81 yards. Last season with the Chargers: six games, 41 carries, 159 yards, one touchdown. He was with Minnesota in September before being released, then worked out for Houston as the Texans looked at several backs, and Cleveland got him.

So: a real NFL body with real NFL experience and a modest NFL record. Which is exactly what a practice squad is for, and exactly what Cleveland needed this week, because of what happened to the room in front of him.

And the injury news is a day worse than the video.

One housekeeping note, because the version going around was recorded off Wednesday's practice and the week has since resolved. The video says there is "still hope that at least one of them and potentially both can play." That hope is gone.

The final report has Cleveland without seven players at the Jets: Mason Graham (sprained MCL and sprained ankle, ruled out, week-to-week), Mike Hall Junior (ankle, ruled out), Kalia Davis (quad, ruled out, he had just been designated to return from injured reserve), Carsen Ryan (calf), Tylan Wallace (knee, second straight game), Teven Jenkins (back, fifth straight game) and Dillon Gabriel (back, ruled out despite practicing fully all week).

Three defensive tackles. Todd Monken's answer: "It's next man up. that's why you have practice squad players and the ability to elevate them, and we'll make a decision who that'll be later today."

The one piece of good news the video had right: Elgton Jenkins, Elgton, not "Elgaten", cleared concussion protocol and was a full participant all week, after Luke Wypler filled in capably at center against Pittsburgh.

The real story. Cleveland's running back room is being run out of the practice squad.

Now the thing nobody has connected, and it is the most interesting fact in this entire news cycle.

Dylan Sampson, Sampson, not "Samson", is on injured reserve after knee surgery. That is what opened the door. And look who walked through it: Jaleel McLaughlin signed to Cleveland's practice squad on September 1, and was elevated into a real role specifically because Sampson went on IR.

Then, against Pittsburgh, McLaughlin got exactly one carry. He took it 28 yards for a touchdown, behind blocks from rookie first-round receiver KC Concepcion.

One snap. One touchdown. From a man who was on the practice squad five weeks ago.

And now Berry has added Patterson to that same squad. The picture: Cleveland's lead back is a second-year player, his backups are Raheim Sanders, Raheim, not "Raim", and a practice-squad call-up who scored on his only touch, and the newest addition is an undrafted veteran with an FBS record. That is not a weakness. That is a front office treating the practice squad as a working part of the roster rather than a storage closet.

And the actual star is already here, as a receiver.

Here is the development that deserved the headline this week, and it got almost no attention.

Against Pittsburgh, Quinshon Judkins ran 17 times for 53 yards with a two-yard touchdown, his first rushing score of the season. Fine, unremarkable. Now the other column: six catches on seven targets for 43 yards. He led the Browns in receptions and tied for second in targets, both career highs.

Monken was asked about it on Friday and gave the most useful football answer of his press conference:

"He's a bull out in space. Him and Harold are similar, their ability once you get them out in space, when you're fighting for yards, those guys are hard to bring down. They've got great contact balance. And when you talk about where teams are going to more and more of these zone looks, their ability to puncture and take a check-down and instead of getting three or four, getting seven, eight, nine, ten."

Translate that. Defenses are playing more zone, which takes away deep throws and concedes short ones. A check-down to a back is supposed to be a four-yard play that leaves you behind schedule. If Judkins and Harold Fannin Junior turn those concessions into eight and nine, the defense's whole bargain collapses, you cannot play soft zone against an offense that converts your giveaways into first downs.

Monken also said Judkins "has really improved his hands" and is "still a young player that's developing." That is a head coach telling you, in public, that his running back has become a passing-game weapon. That is the dream star running back story in Cleveland, and he has been on the roster the whole time.

The uncomfortable number underneath it.

Now the part that keeps us honest, because the run game itself is not good.

Cleveland is averaging ninety-three point eight rushing yards per game, 25th in the league. Judkins is averaging forty-four point three yards a game at three point oh yards per carry. Three yards a carry is not a lead back having a good season; it is a lead back grinding behind a line that has been without Teven Jenkins since Week 1 and lost its center to a concussion for a week.

Hold both facts at once. The ground game is bottom-eight in football, and the back running it just posted a career-high six catches while his head coach calls him a check-down weapon who turns four yards into nine.

That tells you where this offense creates value: not between the tackles, but in the short passing game out of the backfield, which changes what "the running back Cleveland needs" even means.

So what does Cleveland actually need at running back?

Not a star rusher. A pass-catching back. And this is where we have to be fair rather than enthusiastic about Patterson.

Look at the profiles. Raheim Sanders is described by people who watch him daily as solid as a pass-catcher. Jaleel McLaughlin is elusive, fast, and a natural change-of-pace, pass-catching fit. Judkins just caught six balls in a game. The identity of this backfield in Monken's offense is catch it and make somebody miss.

Patterson, in 27 NFL games, has 13 career receptions. That is roughly one catch every two games. He is a downhill, between-the-tackles runner, which is genuinely what you want if the weather turns and Cleveland needs to hand it off twenty-five times, and genuinely not what you want if the plan is six targets to the back.

So the accurate way to describe this signing: Patterson is carry insurance, not passing-game help. If Sampson's recovery stretches on or somebody else goes down, Cleveland has an experienced body who can take fifteen hard carries in a rainstorm. That is the job. It is a useful job. It is not a dream.

The real run game story is the offensive line.

Before anybody blames a running back for three yards a carry, look at who has been blocking.

Teven Jenkins, the starting guard, has not played since Week 1, Sunday will be his fifth straight absence with a back injury. Elgton Jenkins, the center, suffered a concussion against Carolina in Week 3 and missed the Pittsburgh game entirely, with Luke Wypler stepping in and playing well. And two of the five men up front are rookies: Spencer Fano, one of Cleveland's two 2026 first-round picks, and third-rounder Austin Barber, both of whom have drawn real praise but are also four games into their professional lives.

Now re-read the numbers. ninety-three point eight rushing yards a game, 25th in the league; three yards a carry for the lead back. That is not a tailback problem. That is a line missing a starting guard for a month, breaking in two rookies, and playing a game without its center.

Which is why the most encouraging item on this week's report has nothing to do with running backs: Elgton Jenkins cleared protocol and practiced fully all week. A veteran center back for a road game in the rain is worth more to this ground game than any practice-squad signing. It also sets up the cleanest test of the week: if the yards per carry climbs with him back, the problem was the line. If it stays at three point oh, the backfield conversation becomes a real one.

What a practice squad back has to do to get elevated.

Since the question everyone is asking is whether Patterson will actually play, here is what the decision really turns on, and it is almost never about rushing ability.

One: pass protection. The fastest way for a backup running back to never see the field is to miss a blitz pickup. A back who cannot identify and block a linebacker coming free is a liability the offense cannot carry, no matter how well he runs.

Two: knowing the install cold. Practice-squad players get the week's game plan on the same schedule as everyone else but with a fraction of the reps. Coaches elevate the man who runs the right route on the first try, not the one who needs it explained twice. Monken made this exact point on Friday: "that's why you're consistently trying to develop those players, so when they get their number called, the expectation of the position, you're trying not to let that drop."

Three: special teams. An elevated backup usually earns his helmet on coverage units before he earns a carry, the quiet reason Cleveland's recent additions fit together, since Proche is a returner and depth backs are coverage bodies.

Four: the elevation budget. Three standard elevations per player per season, spent only on the week you genuinely need the man, which is why Cleveland's will almost certainly go to a defensive tackle this Sunday, not a fourth running back.

So the honest answer: Patterson plays if somebody in front of him gets hurt, or if the weather turns this into twenty-five handoffs and Cleveland wants fresh legs late. Otherwise he spends October learning the install, which is the job he was signed to do.

Why it's still a smart signing. The pattern.

Here is the thread, and it is the honest version of "Berry has done it again."

Look at the last four days of Cleveland transactions. October 6: James Proche the Second signed to the practice squad, a returner with 68 career punt returns, the day before Tylan Wallace's knee showed up as a DNP, and after Wallace had already missed a game. October 6: Kalia Davis's return window opened, the same day Mason Graham's injury surfaced. October 7: Jaret Patterson signed, while Sampson is still on IR and the two backs behind Judkins are absorbing extra work.

Three moves. All boring. All made before the emergency rather than during it. That is the same instinct as the big trade, measured in days instead of years: acquire the replacement while it is cheap, so you never overpay for it desperate.

And the bigger version of he did it again.

If you want the case for Berry, it is not a practice-squad running back. It is this.

On June 1, he traded Myles Garrett to the Rams for Jared Verse, a 2027 first-round pick, a 2028 second-round pick and a 2029 third-round pick, which, barring other moves, puts Cleveland in line for two first-round picks in 2027, the year its quarterback contract expires. In the 2026 draft he spent picks 24 and 39 on KC Concepcion and Denzel Boston, who played 92 and 95 percent of the snaps against Pittsburgh. And his 2025 first-rounder, Mason Graham, went from half a sack as a rookie to four sacks in four games, before this injury.

The counterweight has to be named too, or none of the praise is worth anything: the same general manager guaranteed 230 million dollars to Deshaun Watson, a deal in its final year carrying an 86.2 million dollars charge against the 2027 cap. Berry is currently out-drafting his own largest decision. Both halves are true.

Why all of this matters on Sunday.

The weather turns this from a depth story into a game-plan story.

Monken was asked about a hurricane potentially reaching New York and gave the correct coaching answer: "I don't know, rain, little bit of wind. What are you going to do? Spot the ball, let's go play." Then the real part: "We've worked on wet ball. you try to get out there when it's raining to get used to it."

Wet games compress playbooks, raise fumble risk, and push offenses toward runs and short throws, which is to say, toward the running backs. And Cleveland's defense is about to take the field without three interior linemen, which means the single most valuable thing the offense can do is keep the ball and finish drives.

So the backfield matters more this week than it has all season. If the weather is bad, the game plan is Judkins between the tackles, Judkins and Fannin on check-downs, McLaughlin for a change of pace, and if anybody gets hurt, an undrafted 26-year-old who once scored eight touchdowns in one afternoon.

Where we stop.

"Dream star running back." He is a 26-year-old practice-squad signing with 503 career rushing yards. The star part is his college record, and it is real.

"Washington drafted him." Undrafted free agent, 2021.

"Berry has done it again." The signing is sensible housekeeping. The case for Berry rests on the Garrett haul and two draft classes, not on this.

"Graham and Hall might play." Both ruled out, along with Kalia Davis. Graham is week-to-week; nobody has given a return date, including the team.

Any projection of Patterson's role. He has to earn an elevation first. He may never take a snap for Cleveland.

Any trade prediction before November 10. Cabot reports no plans and the Browns eyeing all four quarterbacks. Fowler senses no movement. Grossi: "I don't expect him to be traded."

Sunday at the Jets.

Cleveland is at the New York Jets, Sunday, October 11, one o'clock Eastern on CBS, the Browns' first game as a first-place team, with a win putting them at four and one before Baltimore visits on October 18. The Jets are one and three, and Breece Hall (quad) sat out practice for them too.

Three things to watch in the backfield specifically. Judkins' target count, if he gets six again, the check-down offense is now the identity, not a one-week wrinkle. Whether McLaughlin gets more than one touch, a man who scored on his only carry has earned a second look. And the yards per carry, because three point oh with Elgton Jenkins back in the middle of the line should start climbing, and if it does not, the problem was never the running back.

The bottom line.

Cleveland signed Jaret Patterson to its practice squad, and the headline-friendly truth about him is genuinely great: 409 yards and eight touchdowns against Kent State, the FBS record, with 710 yards and 12 scores across two games. The sober truth is that he has 503 NFL rushing yards, 13 career catches, and a job description that reads "insurance."

The dream running back story in Cleveland this week is somebody else. Quinshon Judkins caught six passes on seven targets against Pittsburgh, a career high, team-leading, and his head coach called him a bull out in space who turns a four-yard check-down into nine. In an offense scoring 20 a game with a run game ranked 25th, that is where the value is being created.

And the front-office story is the same one it has been all autumn: Berry buying the replacement before he needs it. A returner on October 6. A defensive tackle's return window on October 6. A running back on October 7. None of it trends. All of it is why this team, missing seven players including three defensive tackles, is still going to New York expecting to win.
