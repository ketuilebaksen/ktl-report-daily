Below the line. Todd Monken's Monday press conference, tested claim by claim.

The headline first, and what Monken actually did.

Two promises in that headline. Let us be straight about both.

"Todd Monken shocking QB decision." There wasn't one. Monken did the opposite, he spent the first ninety seconds taking the blame himself and describing how he intends to help his starter: "We certainly need to call it better and execute better... it starts with me and then our offensive staff," and then the sentence that settles it, "put Deshaun in a better position to be successful." A coach planning a change does not spend Monday explaining how he will fix things for the man he is about to bench.

"Andrew Berry has done it again." Berry's name never came up, but there is a real version of that claim in this transcript, and we will build a section out of it, because Sunday's story is a running back who arrived on a Wednesday and a safety who had not played a defensive snap all year.

Here is why Ktl Report is giving this press conference its own article. Monken did something coaches almost never do: he laid out a complete, specific, self-incriminating diagnosis of his own offense. So rather than react to it, we are going to test it, claim by claim, against the numbers.

Below the line. The phrase of the day.

He gave us the frame himself, and it is a brutal one.

"It's more than just one thing. Obviously when you end up with whatever it was, 44, 45 yards in the first half, I mean, it's below the line."

The official number was 46 total yards. Three first downs. Thirty-five rushing yards on ten carries. Deshaun Watson completed three passes for 24 yards in thirty minutes of football. Cleveland trailed ten to nothing and should have trailed by more.

"Below the line" is coaching language for a performance beneath the minimum standard, not a bad day, a disqualifying one. A head coach saying that about his own offense, on the record, a day after a win, is not spin. It is an admission.

And it matters more than the final score, because Cleveland won thirteen to ten anyway. Everything Monken said Monday is about a problem the scoreboard hid.

Claim one. It's more than just one thing. True, and he proved it himself.

Asked later about the five sacks, Monken gave the most useful answer of the hour. He broke them into categories instead of hiding behind one cause.

"Some of it was we were behind the sticks, so it allowed them to get into some of their exotic blitzes, which is always going to be hard... Two is sometimes we were covered. That can happen. There's a couple times where we need to get rid of the ball. It's all the above."

Three causes, named by the head coach, none of them comfortable. Behind schedule. Receivers covered. Quarterback late.

Then the part that is actual progress: "The good thing is there wasn't as many communication issues. We did not have, in my opinion, Browns hurting Browns."

Browns hurting Browns is the best phrase anybody has coined about this line. Five new starters, two rookies, a new scheme and a new line coach in George Warhop produced a month of pre-snap chaos. On Sunday, Monken says, the mistakes were execution rather than confusion, "maybe depth of route on one, but those are easy to clean up."

Verdict: true and well-evidenced. Five sacks with three distinct causes is a very different problem from five blown assignments, and it is the first encouraging thing anyone has said about this unit.

Claim two. It starts with me. And what that commits him to.

Asked whether he expected six weeks this bumpy, Monken refused both available escape hatches. He did not blame the roster, and he did not pretend to be happy.

"You're expecting it to go a lot smoother than it's gone. I would say that. But I'm not frustrated... there's certain parts of what we're doing that I feel better about. In some ways I feel like we still got some work to do. So that's what we're going to do. Go back to work and try to figure it out. And it starts with me, and then our offensive staff, and then the guys that we put on the field."

Note the order. Head coach, then staff, then players. That is the correct order, and the rarest one in professional sports.

But accountability is worth only what it commits you to. Monken is the play-caller, and he has now said publicly that the opening scripts are the problem, "we certainly need to look at how we're starting the game, what we're calling." The next slow start belongs to him specifically, and he has pre-agreed to it.

The actual decision on that podium. Tempo.

Here is the headline's honest payoff, and it was buried in the fourth question.

A reporter noticed something real: Watson gets hot when there is urgency, specifically on fourth-quarter drives, even when Cleveland is not formally in hurry-up. So why not use tempo earlier to untrack him?

Monken's answer:

"We've considered that a little bit... it's certainly something we've looked at in terms of trying to speed that up, and we certainly have that at our disposal."

And then he conceded the pattern that makes the question unavoidable: "It just has worked out to where we've played a lot of our best football in the second half, minus the Steelers game."

That is a head coach confirming on the record that a specific lever exists, that his staff has studied it, and that he has it available. Not an announcement, but in coaching language, "we certainly have that at our disposal" is as close to a loaded gun as Monday gets.

Four fourth-quarter game-winning drives, and the thread through all four is tempo. The real decision in front of Todd Monken this week is not which quarterback plays. It is what minute the offense starts playing like it is behind.

And the honest case against doing it.

Ktl Report is not going to sell you tempo as a free fix, because it is not one.

Playing fast means fewer substitutions, less protection help, simpler calls, and a line that cannot huddle to sort out a look it has not seen. With five new starters, two of them rookies, that is the group you least want operating without a reset between snaps. Monken's own "Browns hurting Browns" phrase is the argument against his own lever. Fast tempo also shortens his defense's rest, and right now that defense is the whole football team.

The compromise most staffs reach is partial: script the opening drive at tempo to steal a rhythm, then settle. Given that Cleveland has conceded points on the opening possession in three straight games, starting fast on offense may also be the cheapest way to stop starting slow.

Verdict: a real lever with a real cost, already studied. Watch the first drive on Sunday.

Claim three. First down efficiency is the root cause.

This was Monken's best piece of football reasoning all day, and it ties the whole season together.

"If you can't comfortably turn around and hand the ball off, or you're not efficient on first downs, it makes it hard on a starting-level quarterback in the NFL. We're dealing with that now at times. The more efficient you are on first down, the easier it is to call the game."

Test it. Quinshon Judkins has 73 carries for 221 yards, three yards a carry. Cleveland went five for sixteen on third down Sunday. Monken's own example was the Harold Fannin Junior screen: "We tried to get him a screen and they snuffed it out, so that sucked. That was early in the game and that put us at second and 10."

That is the loop, stated by the man calling the plays. Inefficient first down creates second and long, which removes the run and the play-action, which puts you behind the sticks, which, in his words, "allowed them to get into some of their exotic blitzes." Blitzes produce sacks. Sacks produce punts. Forty-six yards.

Verdict: this is the correct diagnosis, and it is not a quarterback problem. It is a first-down problem that becomes a quarterback problem by the third play of every series.

Claim four. The biggest number nobody reported.

And then, almost in passing, Monken dropped the single most important statistic of Cleveland's season, and we have not seen one outlet lead with it.

"He's very conscientious of not turning the ball over. We've gone four of the last five games really without an offensive turnover, besides the last play of the game with the Jets. And that's a winning formula. I mean, that's going to win if we end every possession with a kick."

Four of the last five games without an offensive turnover.

Hold that next to the sack total. Watson leads the NFL in times sacked and is also, by his head coach's account, barely giving the ball away. Those facts are not in tension, they are the same decision. A quarterback who refuses to force a throw into coverage, and who comes down with two hands on the ball when he is beaten, takes more sacks and commits fewer turnovers.

Cleveland is four and two, with three of those wins by one score. You do not win one-score games while losing the turnover battle. The sacks are the price of the thing keeping this team in first place.

If we end every possession with a kick.

That clause deserves its own moment, because it is a complete football philosophy in nine words.

Monken is describing a team built not to lose. End every drive with a punt or a field-goal attempt, never a giveaway. Make the opponent earn every yard of field position. Then let the defense do the rest.

It is not beautiful, and he has not pretended otherwise. But look at the record: four and two, three one-score wins, head-to-head victories over Baltimore and Pittsburgh, and a share of first place in the AFC North for the first time this late since 2014.

The question for the rest of the season is simple. This formula wins close games; it does not win shootouts. At some point Cleveland meets an offense that scores 27, and that day "end every possession with a kick" will not be enough.

The refusal. I'm not going to say that. You said that.

Now the moment of the entire press conference, and it was a refusal.

A reporter handed him the easiest layup a coach has been offered all year: you coached against this defense for three years, why is it better without Myles Garrett?

Monken would not take it.

"Well, I'm not going to say that. You said that. I'm not going to say that it's better without Myles. I don't know that, because he's not here."

The invitation was to claim credit for a trade that cost Cleveland the reigning Defensive Player of the Year, a man who had just set a single-season record with 23 sacks, for Jared Verse and three picks. The head coach declined, on the simplest grounds available: you cannot compare a defense to a version of itself that does not exist.

We wrote on Monday that one thirteen to ten win does not settle that trade. The head coach of the team that won said the same thing a day later, with every incentive not to.

What he offered instead was behavior rather than personnel: "our guys play with physicality. They play with elite effort. They're connected and they enjoy playing together... they swarm to the football." That is the only thing a staff can actually install.

Claim five. Same scheme, different coordinator.

This was the most technically interesting answer, and it explains a defense that looked lost in Week 1 and looks like this now.

"It's the same scheme, but it's a different coordinator. We're calling it different. There's different things that we're doing on the back end, which I think has really added something to where we're at now."

So the structure Cleveland fans know was not torn up. Mike Rutenberg, the defensive coordinator, and for the record not "Mike Greenberg", inherited the architecture and changed how it is deployed, most visibly in the secondary.

Sunday backs that up: six pass breakups and no interceptions, Denzel Ward taking away a third-down throw to Mark Andrews, and rookie safety Emmanuel McNeil-Warren playing a season-high ninety-four point seven percent of the defensive snaps after Grant Delpit's concussion, with six tackles, a tackle for loss and a pass defensed.

Verdict: credible, and visible. "Different things on the back end" is a coordinator telling you where to look.

And the pattern he volunteered that nobody asked about.

The most useful thing Monken said was something no reporter raised. He brought it up himself, unprompted, in the middle of praising his defense.

"Defensively, we've given up scoring drives on the first drive of each of the last three games, and then they've settled in and played outstanding and have gone stretches where they've gotten stops, just like nine straight the other day."

Three consecutive games conceding a scoring drive on the opponent's opening possession.

That is not bad luck three times. That is a preparation problem, an opening script the defense has not seen, or a plan that needs live reps first. On Sunday it cost ten points in fifteen minutes.

So both units have the same disease. The offense cannot start and the defense cannot start. Then both play outstanding football for three quarters. A coach who volunteers that about his best unit, after a win, is watching the tape rather than the standings.

Claim six. The run defense, with receipts.

Monken was generous with detail here, including crediting a reporter's notebook for the number.

"Scott had that last night of like a 17 and 11-yard run. And then after that... 58 yards on 17 carries. I thought our guys did a great job of being physical at the point of attack. Swarming. Not really allowing, when Derrick gets downhill, it's hard. It's hard to stop his momentum. Thought we did a good job of making him run sideways and stuttering his feet."

The arithmetic is devastating. Henry's two longest runs accounted for 28 of his 58 yards. Take those away and the Hall of Famer had 15 carries for roughly 30 yards. Across six games he averages 89 rushing yards; Sunday he had 58 and a lost fumble. One reporter put Baltimore's season average at 164 rushing yards a game, third in the league, and said Cleveland halved it; Monken did not dispute the framing.

"Making him run sideways" is not a cliché, it is the job description of an interior line against a downhill back. And Cleveland did it without Mason Graham, the fifth overall pick of 2025, and without Mike Hall Junior

And here is where Berry actually did it again.

Now the honest version of the first half of that headline.

Audric Estimé was signed off the Minnesota Vikings' practice squad on October 13. Five days later he took meaningful snaps in a division game and scored the game-tying touchdown. Monken: "The guy shows up on Wednesday and heck, he's taking meaningful snaps and scores a touchdown."

Daniel Thomas played his first defensive snaps of the season and recorded a tackle for loss, after Grant Delpit left with a concussion on Baltimore's opening drive.

Emmanuel McNeil-Warren, a rookie safety, played ninety-four point seven percent of the defensive snaps and stopped a third and goal run at the one-yard line for a loss. In a game decided by three points, that single play may have been worth four points by itself.

Jaleel McLaughlin led the team in rushing with 41 yards on nine carries, with Judkins, Dylan Sampson and Raheim Sanders all unavailable.

Monken's framing was a standard rather than a compliment: "You should have expectations of any player on your roster playing at a high level." He pushed the credit onto his staff, naming running backs coach Duce Staley for staying late to get Estimé ready: "Nothing in our contract says we get the guys ready that were here all the way through training camp."

That is the real "done it again." Not a blockbuster. A roster deep enough that the eleventh and twelfth men win a division game.

And the Jeudy line worth keeping.

One more, because it matters for the next six weeks.

"We're better with Jerry than we are without Jerry. And I think we're doing a better job of getting him involved... It hadn't been easy, as I said before. But he's handled it well the last few weeks, and you can see it with the confidence, the juice he has."

Jeudy led Cleveland with four catches for 69 yards, including the 33-yard reception on third and five that set up the winning kick. Three weeks ago he had eight catches for 81 yards on the season and was being floated as a trade candidate. "We're better with Jerry than we are without Jerry" is a head coach saying, three weeks from the deadline, that his receiver is not for sale.

Names and fixes.

For the record, since these keep getting mangled: Deshaun Watson. Todd Monken. Mike Rutenberg, defensive coordinator. Travis Switzer, offensive coordinator. Audric Estimé. Grant Delpit. Harold Fannin Junior Jerry Jeudy. Duce Staley. Myles Garrett. Emmanuel McNeil-Warren. And the first-half total was 46 yards, not 44 or 45, Monken was estimating from memory and undershot himself.

What is actually next.

Asked whether the injured players return this week, Monken gave the only honest answer available: "It's too early to really say, but I'm hopeful that we'll get a number of guys back, and we'll see with the guys that got dinged up yesterday."

Translation: Judkins, Sampson, Graham and Hall are all possible, Delpit is in concussion protocol, and nobody knows yet.

Two deadlines remain fixed. Dillon Gabriel's twenty-one day practice window, opened October 6, expires late this month, activate him or shut him down. And November 10, the trade deadline, now arrives with Cleveland tied for first rather than selling.

Where we stop.

Accountability is not the same as a fix. Forty-six yards is forty-six yards, and nothing has been repaired yet.

But in one hour Todd Monken put his own offense below the line, ordered the blame head coach first, named three separate causes for five sacks, identified first-down efficiency as the root problem, volunteered a defensive weakness nobody had asked about, confirmed a tempo option exists, and refused a free compliment about trading away the best defensive player in football.

That is not a man managing a narrative. That is a man doing a film review in public. Whatever else is wrong in Cleveland, that part is working.

The bottom line.

No quarterback decision was made. The opposite happened: the head coach spent Monday explaining how he intends to put Deshaun Watson in a better position.

The real numbers from that hour: 46 first-half yards, "below the line." Five sacks with three different causes. Five-for-sixteen on third down. Four of the last five games without an offensive turnover. Scoring drives allowed on the opening possession of three straight games. Nine straight stops. Henry held to 58 on 17, 28 of it on two runs. A running back signed on a Wednesday scoring the tying touchdown.

And the sentence to keep: "That's going to win if we end every possession with a kick." That is the whole plan, stated plainly by the man who made it. It has produced four wins, three of them by one score, and first place in the AFC North.

Ktl Report will be watching exactly one thing on Sunday: what minute this offense decides to start playing.
