# THE IDENTITY TEST: Pittsburgh Has One, and the Panel Said So — So Let Us Find Out What Cleveland's Actually Is

Here is the thing, though: **what Shedeur Sanders actually did on Sunday is a real story, and it is better than an invented quote.** So we will give you that first, properly, and then we will do the genuinely valuable analysis that this panel handed over for free and the video walked past.

## ✅ WHAT HE ACTUALLY DID: FOUR SNAPS, PLAY BY PLAY

Deshaun Watson took a helmet-to-helmet hit from Steelers safety Jaquan Brisker a little over five minutes into the first quarter — **a hit that drew a roughing penalty** — and went to the medical tent for a concussion evaluation. Shedeur Sanders came in and finished the series. **Four snaps.** His first regular-season action of 2026.

Here is every one of them:

**Snap one.** First and ten from the Browns' forty-four. He threw incomplete to the right sideline under heavy pressure. **Tytus Howard was flagged for an ineligible man downfield** on the play.

**Snap two.** First and fifteen. A short completion to Denzel Boston for no gain.

**Snap three.** Third and fourteen. A two-yard out to Harold Fannin Jr. — **who was stripped of the ball**, with Pittsburgh recovering at the Cleveland forty-two.

**And then the break Cleveland got:** Chris Boswell missed a fifty-seven-yard field goal.

Final line: **two of two, two yards.** A series that produced a penalty, no gain, and a fumble that was not his.

That is the honest version. It is four snaps on no notice in a hostile stadium with his starter in a tent, and the series ended in a turnover he did not commit. Nobody should build a career verdict on it in either direction.

## 📋 AND MONKEN'S REVIEW, WHICH IS THE REAL VALUE

Monken reviewed those four snaps publicly, with specifics, and it is the most information anybody has given us about Shedeur Sanders in two months.

On the first throw: **"Instead of getting downfield, he needs to throw it in the ground."** Translation — with pressure arriving and no one open, eat the play rather than risk it.
On the checks: *"There were a couple of plays where he could have helped us out with a check."*
And the structural point Monken made *for* him, unprompted: *"**Again, a backup doesn't get a lot of reps during the week.**"*
On the third-and-extra-long: *"he had to escape a little bit, but he was fine."*
And the compliment that is hardest to coach: *"**I think he's always composed. He did fine.**"*

Two technical corrections, one excuse made on the player's behalf, one real compliment about temperament. That is a coach giving you an evaluation rather than a hug — and it quietly concedes the development argument fans have been making for weeks, from the man who sets the practice schedule.

## 🗣️ AND THE ONE THING THAT WAS ACTUALLY SAID, WHICH WE WILL HANDLE CAREFULLY

There is a quote from that sequence. It is not from Shedeur Sanders.

Jaquan Brisker — the safety who hit Watson in the helmet and was penalised for it — said afterwards: *"He had his binky today, so."*

We will report that it was said and leave the mockery where it was made. A man going to a medical tent for a concussion evaluation is not a punchline, and Watson's own description of the sequence was the opposite of flippant: *"They were doing NFL protocol. Checking for concussion. The brain is one of the most important things in the body outside the heart and the lungs. But they had to do their job and had to do the evaluation and we did that and I was able to go back in."*

One of those two quotes is worth putting in a video. It is not the one about a binky.

## 🧪 NOW THE IDENTITY TEST, BECAUSE THE PANEL HANDED IT OVER

Here is the best thing anybody said in this entire transcript, and it came from the other side's studio:

> *"In a point of the season where a lot of teams don't have an identity yet, the Pittsburgh Steelers have an identity... And so much about winning in the NFL is having an identity and playing to it."*

That is a sharp, testable claim. So let us test it — on Cleveland.

First we need a definition, because "identity" is usually used as a synonym for vibes. Here is a usable one: **an identity is something you can do on purpose, when the opponent knows it is coming.** Not something you are good at. Something you can *call.*

By that standard, let us score Pittsburgh and then Cleveland.

## 🏴 PITTSBURGH'S IDENTITY, EXACTLY

The panel named it precisely: *"It's run duo, which is double teams up front on the offensive lineman and let Jaylen Warren get north and south, put his foot in the ground and go make plays."*

Duo is a two-back-free, double-team concept: two linemen drive one defender, the back reads the push and picks a side. There is nothing clever about it. Everybody knows it is coming.

Sunday's result: **two hundred net rushing yards, three rushing touchdowns, fourteen rushing first downs**, and **one hundred seventeen net rushing yards in the first quarter alone.** Jaylen Warren finished with **one hundred forty-three on seventeen carries**, tying his career high. Rico Dowdle added fifty-six and a touchdown.

That passes the test completely. Cleveland knew. Cleveland prepared all week. Monken said so: *"we were determined to fix at least the run defense."* It did not matter.

And the panel's extra point is fair: Pittsburgh won three previous games with the pass as the lead and this one on the ground. *"To be able to win in different ways"* is the luxury an identity buys you, not a replacement for it.

## 📊 TESTING CLEVELAND'S FIVE CANDIDATES

Now Cleveland, eight games in. Five things this team is good at. Which of them can it *call?*

**Candidate one: the fourth quarter.** Five fourth-quarter game-winning drives in eight games. Watson is the third quarterback since the 1970 merger with a game-winning drive in each of his team's first five wins, after **Troy Aikman in 1990** and **Matthew Stafford in 2016**. Every Cleveland win has been by four points or fewer.

**Verdict: a skill, not an identity.** And Sunday proved it with brutal economy. Pittsburgh led 20-6 at halftime and 27-6 after three quarters. **Cleveland's best trait was never available**, because you cannot choose to be in a two-minute drill. An asset you can only use when the opponent permits it is not something you can call.

**Candidate two: the run game.** One hundred eighty-three yards against Tennessee. **Thirty-four** against Pittsburgh.

**Verdict: not an identity. A variable.** Monken said it himself this week: *"It is so hard if you can't run the football, and we've been making it hard."*

**Candidate three: protection.** One sack allowed against Tennessee. **Eight against Pittsburgh.** Thirty-three in eight games, the most in the National Football League.

**Verdict: not an identity, and the worst unit on the team.** This is the one that turns candidate one off.

**Candidate four: the pass defense.** Two hundred one passing yards allowed per game, **seventh in the league.** Twenty sacks, top six, with **eleven different players** recording at least one. On Sunday, in a twenty-one-point loss, it held **Aaron Rodgers to thirteen of twenty-five for one hundred seventy-two yards** and got him pulled for Mason Rudolph, with Denzel Ward taking his third interception of the season.

**Verdict: this passes.** It is repeatable, it is scheme-based, it does not depend on the score, and it held up on the worst afternoon of the season. This is Cleveland's identity on defense, and almost nobody says so because it happened in a blowout loss.

**Candidate five: the kicker.** Andre Szmyt is **sixteen of eighteen with three game-winners**, and has made **twenty-five consecutive field goals dating to 2025 — the third-longest streak in Browns history.** He made both his kicks on Sunday, from thirty-two and forty-nine, and those six points were the entire Cleveland total.

**Verdict: elite and genuinely repeatable — and a kicker cannot be a team identity.** He is the reason three of the five wins exist, and he cannot be called on first down.

## 🧮 THE VERDICT, AND WHY IT EXPLAINS THE WHOLE SEASON

Run the test and Cleveland comes out with **one identity and one specialist:** a pass defense that is genuinely seventh in football, and a kicker who does not miss. Everything else is a variable.

Which explains eight games exactly. **When the game stays close, those two assets plus a Watson fourth quarter win it — five times.** When the game does not stay close, there is nothing to deploy, because the two things that keep a game close on offense — running it and protecting the quarterback — are the two things Cleveland cannot do on command.

That is not "same old Browns." It is far more specific, and far more fixable, than a slogan. The panel's own prescription is correct: *"so much about winning in the NFL is having an identity and playing to it."* Cleveland's problem is not a shortage of identity on defense. It is that its offense has no callable identity at all, and the unit standing between it and one is the worst unit on the roster.

## 🔄 AND THE IDENTITY CLEVELAND USED TO HAVE

Here is the part of the identity question nobody in that studio raised, and it is the most Cleveland-specific thing in this whole piece.

**Until this summer, the Browns had the most callable identity in football: Myles Garrett.** One man, one edge, and a plan every offensive coordinator in the league had to build a week around. He produced a franchise-record twenty-three sacks. You could call that. You could call it on first down, on third and thirteen, in a two-minute drill. It did not depend on the score.

On **June 2, 2026**, Cleveland traded him to the Rams for Jared Verse and three picks — including the second of the two 2027 first-rounders sitting in the drawer right now — and split roughly forty-one million dollars in dead money to do it.

Look at what replaced it. Cleveland's pass rush is **not worse**: twenty sacks through eight games, top six in the league, with **eleven different players** recording at least one. That is a healthier structure than one man carrying a unit, and it is why the pass defense ranks seventh.

But it is a **distributed** identity rather than a **callable** one. Eleven contributors is a system working. One Myles Garrett was a weapon you aimed. And the clearest evidence of the difference is the man Cleveland got back: **Jared Verse has no sacks as a Brown** through eight games, finished Sunday with one tackle, and spent the week telling anybody who would listen that Pittsburgh did not worry him.

We are not relitigating that trade. The structural point is what matters for the identity test: **Cleveland swapped a weapon it could aim for a system that works, and then lost the thing that made the system's results feel like a plan.** On an afternoon when the pass defense did its job and the scoreboard read 27-6, nobody noticed the system working, because a system does not announce itself the way a twenty-three-sack season does.

That is the honest cost of the rebuild, and it is a cost, not a mistake. At Ktl Report we will take the distributed version over the one-man version for the next three seasons. It just does not help you on a day when you need somebody to go win a down by himself.

## 🪞 "SAME OLD BROWNS" — THE REAL EXCHANGE, WITH DATES

The panel reached for a line and got the attribution almost right, so here it is properly, because it is a better story than the paraphrase.

On **September 14, 2026**, in his debut on NBC's *Football Night in America* — he is a broadcaster now, not a coach — **Mike Tomlin** said of Cleveland's thirty-four to ten Week 1 loss to Jacksonville: *"In the words of my friend JuJu Smith-Schuster, the Browns is the Browns."*

Monken's response was not a tantrum. It was three sentences: *"Everybody's afforded their opinions. That's what you do when you go from coaching to television."* Then: *"I don't see it that way, 'Browns being Browns.'"* And then the part that has aged into the whole season: *"**The only way to change people's opinions is to play better. That's a fact.**"*

Cleveland went five and two after that. Then it lost by twenty-one in Pittsburgh. Monken's own standard is the one to judge him by, and he set it himself in September.
