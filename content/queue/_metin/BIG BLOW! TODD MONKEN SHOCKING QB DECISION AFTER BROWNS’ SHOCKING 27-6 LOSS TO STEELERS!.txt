Three quarterback decisions in one afternoon. Who made each one, by what authority, and the only one worth arguing about.

First, the title. This one is actually true.

The title is "Big Blow! Todd Monken Shocking QB Decision After Browns' Shocking twenty-seven to six Loss To Steelers."

We spend most weeks telling you a headline overpromised. Not this week. There was a quarterback decision in Pittsburgh. In fact there were three of them inside one afternoon, and the video, like almost all the coverage, walks past the two most interesting ones to get to a trap play.

Here is what the transcript and the week's reporting actually establish, and we will build the whole piece on it, because this is the kind of thing Ktl Report exists to sort out.

A little over five minutes into the first quarter, Deshaun Watson took a blow to the head from Steelers safety Jaquan Brisker. He was sent to the medical tent to be checked for a concussion. Shedeur Sanders came in and finished the series, his first regular-season action of twenty twenty-six. Watson cleared the evaluation and returned the next series. He then took eight sacks for forty-one yards in a twenty-one-point loss, and when a reporter asked whether Monken ever thought about taking him out, the answer was: "Did not. I want to see him play."

Three decisions. Different people made them, under different rules, to different standards. Only one of them is genuinely arguable, and it is not the one with "shocking" attached.

Decision one. The one Monken did not make.

The first quarterback decision of the afternoon was not a coaching decision at all, and that matters.

When a player takes a blow to the head, the league's concussion protocol takes over. The coach does not get a vote on whether the evaluation happens. Watson went to the tent; Sanders went into the game.

Monken was asked about exactly this at the end of his press conference, at what point do a coaching staff and a medical staff have to step in and save a player from himself? His answer:

"Well, there are, the NFL's put in a number of protocols for that, you know, from upstairs, which I think has been great, you know, for protection of the players. And our medical staff is always trying to protect our players. I've never once seen that to be the case where our medical staff is not trying to protect play[ers]."

Read that as an answer about authority, because that is what it is. On head injuries, Monken is telling you he is not the decision-maker and does not want to be. The protocols come "from upstairs," he calls them "great for protection of the players," and he vouches for his own medical staff without being asked to.

That is the correct answer, and it is the answer you want from a head coach. It is also the thing that makes decision three a separate question, rather than the same question.

And what Watson said about it, which deserves repeating.

Watson described the sequence himself afterwards, and it is worth quoting in full because it is unusually sober:

"They were doing NFL protocol. Checking for concussion. The brain is one of the most important things in the body outside the heart and the lungs. But they had to do their job and had to do the evaluation and we did that and I was able to go back in."

He said he felt fine throughout and that the check was routine.

We are going to leave it exactly there. He took a blow to the head, independent medical staff evaluated him, they cleared him, he returned. We do not have his medical file, we are not doctors, and a quarterback's brain is not raw material for a thumbnail. What we will say is that both the player and the coach described the process with respect rather than irritation, and that is not always how this goes.

Decision two. Shedeur Sanders actually played.

This is the headline nobody wrote, and it is strange that nobody wrote it.

For two months, Cleveland content has run on the question of when Shedeur Sanders would get on the field. Trade rumours, prediction-market percentages, a brother's house-hunting vlog, agent reports. We have audited most of it in these pages and found almost all of it to be sourced to aggregator accounts.

And then, on Sunday, with no warning and no announcement: Shedeur Sanders took the field on Cleveland's opening possession and finished the series. He went two for two for two yards.

Two yards. It is not a highlight. It is also the first time this season that the argument stopped being hypothetical, and it happened in Pittsburgh, in front of a hostile crowd, on three minutes' notice, with his starter in a tent.

If you want a true "shocking QB" story this week, that is it, and it required no invention at all.

And Monken's coaching notes on him, unvarnished.

Here is the part that should interest you more than the two yards: Monken reviewed those snaps publicly, in specific coaching detail, without softening and without padding.

On the opening screen: "Instead of getting downfield, he needs to throw it in the ground."
On the checks: "There were a couple of plays where he could have helped us out with a check."
On the context, offered on Sanders' behalf: "Again, a backup doesn't get a lot of reps during the week."
On the third-and-extra-long: "We had an opportunity on the third and extra long, he had to escape a little bit, but he was fine."
And on the thing that is hardest to teach: "I think he's always composed. He did fine."

That is a coach giving you a real evaluation rather than a press-conference hug. Two technical corrections, one structural excuse made for the player, and one genuine compliment about temperament. It tells you more about where Shedeur Sanders actually stands than every trade rumour of the last three weeks combined.

It also quietly answers the development complaint we have been fielding for weeks. The grievance has been that he never gets real reps. Monken's own explanation for the mistakes is that "a backup doesn't get a lot of reps during the week." Both things are true, and now they are on the record from the man who sets the practice schedule.

Decision three. Did not. I want to see him play.

And now the one that is actually arguable.

Late in the second half, with Pittsburgh, in the reporter's phrase, "teeing up" on Watson in a game that was already gone, Monken was asked whether he considered getting him out of there.

"Did not. I want to see him play. I want to see him continue to play and battle. And he did. There's some things that we need to clean up in coaching... I could have done it better, right? As a coach. I mean, that's, who are you if you don't say that? Who you are as a man if you just say no. Starts with me. Everything starts with me."

Unlike decision one, this one was entirely his. No protocol, no doctor, no rule. A head coach with a twenty-one-point deficit chose to leave his starting quarterback in a game where seven different Steelers were getting home.

Both cases deserve to be made properly, so here they are.

The case against leaving him in.

The hit count. Eight sacks for forty-one yards on Sunday, and thirty-three in eight games, still the most in the National Football League. Each one is a man arriving at speed.

The medical history. Watson ruptured his Achilles tendon in October twenty twenty-four and re-tore it in January twenty twenty-five, requiring a second surgery. That is a public, material fact about durability, not speculation.

The leverage. His contract runs out after this season, and Cleveland's season does not hinge on the fourth quarter of a game it is losing by twenty-one.

The alternative was dressed and had already played. Shedeur Sanders was active as Q B two, had taken a series that afternoon and had looked, by his coach's own account, composed. There was a quarterback available who did not need protecting from a lost game.

And the scoreboard was settled. Pittsburgh led twenty to six at halftime and twenty-seven to six after three quarters. There were no points worth a hit.

The case for leaving him in.

He had been cleared. Independent medical staff evaluated him and returned him to play. Removing a cleared quarterback is not a medical act; it is a football judgment, and coaches may make it either way.

"I want to see him play." There is a real argument that the fourth quarter of a blowout is the cheapest place in football to learn things. Monken said it in the same press conference: "you speak with your play." You cannot find out how a team behaves when it is buried if you remove the people who are buried.

The locker-room signal. Pulling a starter to protect him while his teammates keep playing says something about who the team is. Monken's framing all week was fight: "other than our guys that continue to fight all the way to the end."

And he was not hiding from the cost. In the same answer he took the blame for the sacks first: "certainly got to call it better. Certainly got to scheme it better. Got to protect better."

Our read, labelled as a read: we would have taken him out, and we think the medical history plus a settled scoreboard plus a dressed backup who had already played makes that the lower-risk call. But we are not going to pretend this is obvious, because it is not. It is a judgment call where the coach's reasoning is stated, consistent with everything else he has said this season, and defensible. What it is not is "shocking."

And the quarterback took it too.

One more thing from Sunday night that belongs in any honest version of this story, because it completes a chain of three men all refusing to pass the blame along.

Watson, asked about being sacked eight times, declined to point at the line that let it happen:

"Put it on me, I'm never gonna tend to try to put it on the guys up front."

He went further, and the detail is the useful part: he said quicker checkdowns and more throwaways could have prevented some of the negative plays, and he admitted his own tendency to extend plays sometimes hurt the offense, because holding the ball pushed Cleveland further behind the chains.

Now stack that against what Monken said about the same eight sacks, "certainly got to call it better, certainly got to scheme it better, got to protect better, got to work to get our guys open", and against what left guard Zion Johnson said: "At the end of the day, it's on us."

The coach blamed his call sheet. The quarterback blamed his own clock. The guard blamed the line. Nobody blamed anybody else. That is an unusual afternoon in professional football, and it is worth saying out loud before the week turns into blame-hunting.

It also narrows the problem usefully. If the play-caller, the passer and the blocker are each describing their own piece honestly, then the fix is not one personnel change, it is sequencing, which is exactly what Monken described after the Tennessee win when Cleveland allowed one sack and ran for one hundred eighty-three yards.

One paragraph on the trap, because we did it yesterday.

We devoted a full piece to Monken's best answer, the one explaining that an attack-style front will concede trap plays, wham plays and edge plays by design, and that the counters are moving the front, pressuring off the edges and getting over blocks. The exhibit is Jaylen Warren's fifty-one-yard run on third and thirteen, on a trap, which Monken flagged himself and called "a good call by them." Pittsburgh finished with two hundred net rushing yards, including one hundred seventeen in the first quarter alone. That is a scheme trade-off against twenty sacks and the seventh-ranked pass defense. The sacks allowed are not a trade-off, and no scheme explains them.

The roster signal nobody noticed.

And here is a transaction from this week that matters more than any rumour.

Cleveland released running back Audric Estime. He had signed off Minnesota's practice squad on October thirteenth, played once, nine carries for nineteen yards and a touchdown against Baltimore on October eighteenth, and was inactive for two games after Dylan Sampson returned from injured reserve on October twenty-fourth.

The important part: no corresponding move was announced. Cleveland is carrying an open active-roster spot, eight days before the trade deadline closes at four o'clock on Tuesday, November tenth.

A team that is done shopping does not leave a spot open. That is not proof of anything, and we will not dress it up as a report. But it is the first concrete, dated thing all week that points toward Cleveland doing something rather than nothing, and it arrived on the same day its head coach said: "It is so hard if you can't run the football, and we've been making it hard."

And the fourth decision, which is not Monken's at all.

Three decisions were made on a football field on Sunday. The fourth one sits on Andrew Berry's desk and closes in eight days.

The named record, dated, has not moved: Mary Kay Cabot reported Cleveland is not shopping any of its quarterbacks and has no plans to trade Sanders or Gabriel before November tenth "unless someone makes them an offer they can't refuse." Jeremy Fowler put Jerry Jeudy at twenty percent and said a fourth-round pick "would not move anyone in Cleveland." Jonathan Jones of The Athletic called a Sanders trade by the deadline "a stunner."

What has moved is the context. Those statements were made by a five-and-two team in first place. Cleveland is now five and three and second.

And the market price for what this roster actually needs remains the cheapest thing on the board. In the last five weeks this league has priced a three-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle, DeForest Buckner, at one twenty twenty-seven fourth-round pick. Meanwhile Jeffery Simmons, twenty-nine and under contract through twenty thirty, cost Chicago two first-round picks and a fourth. The premium is for control, not reputation, and a nine-week rental of a veteran lineman sits firmly in the Buckner aisle.

Cleveland holds two twenty twenty-seven first-round picks, the Rams' twenty twenty-eight second and twenty twenty-nine third, and now an open roster spot. That is as equipped as a buyer gets. Whether it is still a buyer is the fourth decision, and it is the only one of the four that money can touch, which is why, at Ktl Report, it is the one we will be watching hardest between now and Tuesday.

Eight days, and New Orleans first.

Cleveland is five and three. Pittsburgh is six and two and holds the division. Monken said it twice: "They've earned being six and two. We've earned being five and three."

Next: at New Orleans, Sunday November eighth, one o'clock, against a two-and-five team, two days before the deadline.

And the sentence that should frame the whole week: "You're going to see the similarities each week until you get it fixed, right? It's not just defense, it's offense, it's everywhere."

Two weeks, two games where the run defense leaked and the protection collapsed. The scheme explains half of one of those. Thirty-three sacks in eight games explains itself.

What we are not carrying.

We are not carrying any speculation about Deshaun Watson's brain. He took a blow to the head, independent medical staff evaluated and cleared him, and he said the process was routine and that he felt fine. We have no basis to say anything beyond that, and we would not use it for views if we did.

We are not carrying "Monken risked his quarterback's health." The medical decision was not his, and he said plainly that it should not be. The decision he did make was about hits in a lost game, and we argued that one on the merits above.

We are not carrying Shedeur Sanders' two yards as either a breakthrough or a verdict. It was one series on no notice.

We are not carrying youth as an explanation for the loss. Monken dismantled it himself: "otherwise what were the other five wins, right?"

And we are not carrying panic. Five and three, eight games left, a kicker on a twenty-five-field-goal streak, a tight end with ten catches for one hundred fifty-four yards on Sunday, and a pass defense ranked seventh.

Your turn.

One real question, and it is the only arguable decision of the three.

Twenty-one points down, a quarterback who has now been sacked thirty-three times in eight games, two Achilles surgeries behind him, a contract that expires in January, and a backup already dressed and warm. Do you take him out?

We would have. Monken did not, and his reason, "I want to see him play", is a real coaching argument rather than an excuse. Tell us which way you would go, and then tell us whether your answer changes if Cleveland is five and three or if it is nine and three.

And the second one, for the next eight days: there is an open roster spot sitting there. What do you put in it?

The bottom line.

Three quarterback decisions, one afternoon.

The protocol made the first one, took Watson to the tent after a blow to the head from Jaquan Brisker, and cleared him. Both the coach and the quarterback described that process with respect, Monken calling the protocols "great for protection of the players," Watson saying "the brain is one of the most important things in the body outside the heart and the lungs."

The second one put Shedeur Sanders on an NFL field for the first time this season, on three minutes' notice, in Pittsburgh. Two for two, two yards, and a coach's candid review that was worth more than the stat line: two corrections, one excuse made on his behalf about reps, and "I think he's always composed."

The third was Monken's alone, and it is the one worth arguing: a cleared quarterback left in a twenty-one-point loss while seven different Steelers took turns. "Did not. I want to see him play." We would have pulled him. He would not, he said why, and he took the blame for the protection before anybody asked.

None of that is shocking. All of it is real, which is better. Eight days to the deadline, an open roster spot, New Orleans on Sunday, and here at Ktl Report, we will take three true decisions over one invented announcement every single week.
