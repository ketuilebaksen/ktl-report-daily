First, the title, and benching is the wrong word.

The title is "Shedeur Sanders In Terrible After Monken's Ruthless Benching Decision!"

Leaving aside that "in terrible" is not a sentence, the word doing the work here is benching, and it is wrong in a way that changes the whole story.

A benching is when a coach removes a starter because of how he played. That is not what happened.

Here is the actual sequence, and all of it is a matter of record. Deshaun Watson started Cleveland's first eight games. He took a helmet-to-helmet hit in Pittsburgh, was cleared on the field, honestly reported symptoms the next day, and entered the concussion protocol. He was cleared on the Saturday before the New Orleans game and then held out, because he had taken no team repetitions all week. When a reporter asked Monken straight out whether that was the reason, his whole answer was one word: "Exactly."

So Shedeur Sanders started one game, because the starter could not practise. Then the starter practised. Returning a healthy starter to his job is not a benching, it is the undoing of a temporary arrangement, and the video, buried under eleven minutes of outrage, actually tells you so itself: "He did not lose his starting job because he was bad."

Correct. He did not lose a job he had not been given.

And "ruthless" is the other word that does not survive the tape, because in the very same press conference Monken said Shedeur Sanders "did a really good job." We will get to that.

So here is what we actually did with this press conference.

The video's thesis is that Monken built "a specific evidentiary case" against Shedeur Sanders, "describing the crimes without naming the defendant."

That is a serious claim about a coach's intent, so we tested it the only way you can: we counted.

We went through every criticism Monken made at that podium and assigned each one a target. Not what it could be read as implying. What it names.

Here at Ktl Report this is the dullest possible method and it is the only one that settles an argument about whether somebody was being attacked. Twelve criticisms. Here is the tally.

The tally. Every criticism, with its target.

Target. The whole team. Seven of twelve.

One. Penalties, overall. "Not sustainable to have lost yardage plays, to have sacks, to have penalties." Target: the offense collectively. A quarterback does not commit a lost-yardage run.

Two. The penalty ranking. Monken volunteered that Cleveland is nineteenth in penalty yards and thirty-second, dead last in the National Football League, in total penalties. Target: the whole roster. Thirty-second out of thirty-two.

Three. Pre-snap penalties specifically. He acknowledged the penalties have been overwhelmingly pre-snap, false starts, offsides, illegal procedure. Target: offensive linemen, receivers, special-teamers. A false start is committed by the man who moves. Note what this does to the video's argument: the one category of penalty Monken singled out is the category a quarterback almost never commits.

Four. The refusal of the crowd-noise excuse. A reporter offered him the obvious out, that playing at home might clean up the pre-snap problems, and he declined half of it: "some of that is true and some of that is not true." He said Cleveland had home games where they had not taken care of business from a penalty standpoint. Target: his own players and staff, with the excuse removed.

Five. The inability to start fast. A season-long theme, and one he has named four separate causes for: turnovers, penalties, lost-yardage plays and failing to stop the run early. Target: offense, defense and special teams.

Six. Red zone execution. "In the red zone over the last couple of weeks, the team has not executed at a high level." Target: named as the team.

That is a head coach standing at a podium and describing his own football team as the embodiment of what he coaches against. It is a remarkable thing to say out loud, and we are not going to pretend it is anything other than damning, of him and of everybody in that building.

Target. Unnamed, could be either quarterback. Two of twelve.

Eight. A sack taken in the red zone when the ball could have been thrown away. Monken gave this as a specific example of failed execution. He did not say which quarterback, which game, or which drive.

And here is the fact that matters: Deshaun Watson has been sacked thirty-three times this season across eight starts. Shedeur Sanders has started once. On the raw arithmetic, an unattributed red-zone sack is far more likely to belong to the man with thirty-three of them than to the man with one start. The video assigns it to Shedeur by implication. Nine. The failure from the one-yard line against Pittsburgh. Monken said they "certainly had opportunities at the end of that game and did not convert."

Target: identifiable, and it is not Shedeur Sanders. Shedeur Sanders did not start the Pittsburgh game. Deshaun Watson did, twenty-seven of thirty-nine for three hundred twenty-four yards, and eight sacks taken. Shedeur attempted two passes in that game, for two yards.

So the second of Monken's two red-zone examples is explicitly from a game Shedeur Sanders barely played in, and the video presents both as part of an evidentiary case against him. That is not a stretch. It is a misattribution you can check in thirty seconds.

Target. Fourth down and red zone as coaching problems. Two of twelve.

Ten and eleven. "Got to be better on fourth down. Got to be better in red zone."

The video calls these "the five words that are the entire verdict on this situation." They are not a verdict on a quarterback. "We" is the subject of both sentences, and fourth down and red zone are the two areas of football most completely determined by the play call. Target: the coaching staff, Monken included.

And note what he explicitly refused. Asked whether Cleveland has a short-yardage problem, he rejected the framing, he said he does not see it as a short-yardage concern, he sees it as an execution concern. The video treats that as damning because "execution" means players rather than scheme. It can equally mean the staff, and the sentence immediately after it is "got to be better," which is a coach including himself.

Target. Shedeur Sanders by name. Zero of twelve, and one piece of praise.

Twelve. The only time Monken named him was to defend him.

Asked whether Shedeur was given real consideration for the job going forward, Monken said he thought Shedeur "did a really good job for ten weeks of really not taking any reps."

The video calls this a defence "wrapped in an asterisk." Read it again. The clause the video treats as an asterisk, "for ten weeks of really not taking any reps", is the strongest part of the compliment. It is the coach supplying the context that makes the performance impressive. Removing it would make the praise weaker, not stronger.

And the two sentences the video quotes alongside it are not attacks either:

"There is a lot of room for improvement with both quarterbacks.", both. That is a criticism of Watson as much as of Shedeur, and it is the only line in the press conference that touches Shedeur critically at all. It touches the starter equally.

"There is a high belief in Deshaun Watson.", that is praise of another player, not criticism of this one. Those are different things, and a channel that cannot tell them apart will find villains everywhere.

And the clause after the comma.

Now the sharpest thing we found, and it is in the video's own audio.

The video's central "receipt" is this:

"The intention was for Deshaun to start. Said it out loud. No ambiguity, no hedging. The intention was for Deshaun to start. That is the admission."

And it builds the whole back half on it: that Shedeur "was never the plan," that he was "the placeholder."

But listen to the clip the video itself plays. Twice. It is in the transcript both times, and it does not stop where the narration stops:

"But the intention was for Deshaun to start, it's just when you come in on that Monday..."

The sentence continues past the comma, and the continuation is about the week's sequencing. It is a coach explaining that the plan entering the week was for his healthy starter to start, and then describing what happens when you come in on the Monday, the beginning of a sentence about the practice schedule that the video cuts off mid-clause.

"The intention was for Deshaun to start" is not an admission about Shedeur Sanders' future. It is an answer about which week, from a coach whose starter had just been cleared. And the video plays the longer version, with the comma and the next clause audible, twice, while telling you there is "no hedging" in it.

That is the whole trick, and at Ktl Report we did not have to go anywhere to find it, it is on the video's own tape.

There are two more amputations worth flagging, because a channel using this footage needs to know:

The "everything I'm against" quote is a fragment both times it plays, "and yet it's everything..." and then nothing. We do not know how that sentence ends. The video completes it for him, "everything that we are", and then analyses the completed version. That is a paraphrase dressed as a quote. It may well be what he said. We have no way to know, and neither does the video, because it never plays the end.

And the opening clip is cut off mid-word: "on the field. So, and it's it's crazy...", starting a press conference analysis with a sentence that begins in the middle of somebody else's clause is how you lose an audience that checks.

What the press conference actually tells you, which is worse for Cleveland.

Strip the quarterback frame off this tape and what is underneath is genuinely alarming, and nobody is covering it.

Cleveland is thirty-second in the NFL in total penalties. Dead last. And it is nineteenth in penalty yards, which is the tell, that gap means the penalties are frequent but short. Short, frequent penalties are pre-snap penalties, exactly as Monken said: false starts, offsides, illegal procedure.

Here is why that is the most damning number in Cleveland's season. A holding call is a player losing a battle. A false start is a player who was not ready. One is a talent problem and the other is a preparation and discipline problem, and a team that leads the league in the second kind is telling you something about its own operation.

Then put it next to the first-quarter record. Six penalties in the opening quarter in New Orleans. The worst first-quarter scoring differential in football across the first eight games. The two facts are the same fact.

And then Monken removed his own best excuse. Offered crowd noise as an explanation, he said "some of that is true and some of that is not true," and pointed at home games where it had happened anyway. He had a way out and he declined it, which is the single most creditable thing he did at that podium.

Which makes the schedule the whole story now. Four home games and a bye before Cleveland travels again. Seven of eleven offensive starters in their first or second NFL season. A team last in the league in pre-snap penalties, about to spend a month in its own building.

If the penalties do not come down over those four games, the quarterback question was never the question.

And the one thing the video gets completely right.

Credit where it is due, and this passage is genuinely good:

"He was asked what kind of collaboration there is with the front office on the quarterback decision. And Monken's answer was precise and deliberate. He said everything is a collaborative effort, but ultimately he has to make the decision of who the starting quarterback is."

That is a real and important answer, and it is the end of a story we declined to carry yesterday.

On Wednesday we refused to run a theory that Cleveland's quarterback decision was coming from somewhere other than the head coach, one commentator raised it, labelled it a conspiracy theory himself before saying it, and cited a different situation from years ago involving different people. We said we would not carry it without a source.

Here is the source, and it is the only one that counts. Asked directly, on the record, the head coach said the decision is his. "Ultimately he has to make the decision of who the starting quarterback is."

So that question is answered, by the one man with standing to answer it, and the video deserves credit for putting it on the record. Everything else in it about institutional power is built on sand, but this part is bedrock.

And the judged each week answer, read both ways.

Asked whether he looks at the quarterback position week to week or long term, Monken answered neither, he said they are judged each week.

The video reads this as a death sentence for Shedeur Sanders: "as long as Watson is healthy and playing five and three football, Shedeur is not getting that job back by waiting... That is not a career plan. That is a prayer."

That reading is half right and we will give it that much. If a starter keeps the job while he plays well, the backup cannot take it by waiting. True, and true of every backup quarterback in the league.

But "judged each week" cuts both ways and the video only uses one edge. It is also the sentence that keeps the door open. A coach who said "Watson is my starter, full stop" would have closed it. A coach who says every man on the roster is judged weekly has told you that performance, not status, is the standard, which is the most favourable framework a backup can be given.

And the honest version of what Shedeur Sanders needs is not "another injury." It is the thing every young quarterback in history has needed: the starter plays badly enough for long enough that the weekly judgment changes. That is not a prayer. It is the ordinary mechanism by which every quarterback change in football has ever happened.
