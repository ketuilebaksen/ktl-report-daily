The comparable sales. Appraising the Khalil Mack proposal against seven real trades, and why a simulator paid the Chargers backwards.

First, the title. No trade has happened, and the video says so three times.

The title is "Browns Trade For Elite NFL Star Pass Rusher!" That is a completed transaction in the present tense. It did not happen.

And this is the second time in a week we have had to point out that the script under the thumbnail is more honest than the thumbnail. In its own words:

"This is a hypothetical trade proposal developed using the A to Z Sports Trade Simulator. There is no confirmed agreement between Cleveland and Los Angeles and no evidence that either front office has offered these exact terms."

It says it again later, and the host even flags the sack-total discrepancy in his own sourcing. Whoever writes these scripts understands the difference between a proposal and a trade. The headline keeps throwing that work away, and the audience that notices once stops trusting everything after it.

So we are not going to spend this piece correcting a script that already corrected itself. We are going to do the thing the video gestures at and never finishes: put a price on Khalil Mack.

Because here is what makes this week different from every other trade-rumor week. Six real trades have already closed in this league since late September, with public terms and dates. That is not speculation, that is a market. And when you have a market, you do not guess at value. You appraise.

What the proposal actually is.

Credit to the video for naming its sources properly: the idea came from Austin Stanley, Easton Freeze and Ryan Roberts on A to Z Sports' thirteen Personnel podcast, with Brandon Little also discussing it.

The hypothetical package, cleaned up, the transcript garbles the years badly, rendering them as twenty twenty and two eighty-seven:

Cleveland receives: Khalil Mack and a fourth-round pick.
Los Angeles receives: a fifth-round pick (originally acquired from Houston) and another fifth-round pick in twenty twenty-eight.

Read that carefully, because almost nobody will. In that construction Cleveland gives up two fifth-rounders and gets a fourth-rounder back along with the player. Net, the Browns are paying somewhere around a single late pick for a nine-time Pro Bowler, and the Chargers are handing Cleveland draft capital for the privilege of taking him.

Hold that thought. It becomes the whole article.

The method. Appraisal by comparable sales.

When you value a house, you do not ask what the owner hopes for. You find houses on the same street that sold this month and you adjust for differences.

Football has the same tool and almost nobody uses it, because using it ruins most trade proposals. So here are the seven completed sales on this street, with dates and terms, newest first.

The seven comps.

One. Jeffery Simmons, DT, Titans to Bears, October twenty-ninth. Chicago received Simmons and a twenty twenty-eight fifth. Tennessee received a twenty twenty-seven first, a twenty twenty-eight first, and a twenty twenty-seven fourth. Simmons is twenty-nine and signed a three-year extension in June worth one hundred five point eight million dollars with one hundred million guaranteed, under contract through twenty thirty.

Two. DeForest Buckner, DT, Colts to forty-niners, October twenty-seventh. San Francisco received Buckner. Indianapolis received one twenty twenty-seven fourth-round pick. Buckner is a three-time Pro Bowler.

Three. Quenton Nelson, G, Colts to Seahawks, October twenty-seventh. Seattle received Nelson. Indianapolis received a twenty twenty-seven third and a twenty twenty-eight fifth. Nelson is an eight-time Pro Bowler on an expiring contract.

Four. Marvin Harrison Junior, WR, Cardinals to Texans, October eleventh. Houston received Harrison and a twenty twenty-seven seventh. Arizona received a twenty twenty-seven second and a twenty twenty-seven sixth.

Five. Cam Jurgens, C, Eagles to Ravens, October seventh. Baltimore received Jurgens and a twenty twenty-seven seventh. Philadelphia received a twenty twenty-seven fifth and a twenty twenty-eight second.

Six. Joey Porter Junior, CB, Steelers to Cowboys, September thirtieth. Dallas received Porter. Pittsburgh received a twenty twenty-seven sixth and a twenty twenty-eight second.

Seven. J.J. McCarthy, QB, Vikings to Giants, September twenty-eighth. New York received McCarthy. Minnesota received a twenty twenty-seven fifth.

Seven sales, twenty-six days, every term public. Now we can appraise.

The appraisal. What Khalil Mack is actually worth.

Mack's file, as of the end of October: thirty-five years old, in his thirteenth season, three sacks and sixteen pressures through six games, the video says eleven pressures; the figure we found was sixteen, on a one-year contract that expires after this season, signed after he nearly retired last offseason. Pro Football Focus has him inside the top two dozen edge defenders, consistent against run and pass. His team is zero and six.

Now find his comp. It is sitting at number two on the list and it is almost perfect.

DeForest Buckner went for one twenty twenty-seven fourth-round pick. A three-time Pro Bowl interior defender, thirty-two years old, moved by a selling team to a contender for a single Day Three pick. Different position, same shape of asset: a decorated veteran past his peak, on a short runway, leaving a bad team for a good one.

That is not our analysis alone. The most detailed Mack piece we read reaches the same conclusion independently, projecting a Day Three pick as the realistic range and citing that exact Indianapolis trade, an interior lineman, age thirty-two, for a twenty twenty-seven fourth, as the comparable price.

So the appraisal is: a fourth-round pick, maybe a third if two contenders bid. Going to Los Angeles. Not coming from them.

And that is where the simulator paid backwards.

Lay the proposal next to the appraisal.

The appraisal: Cleveland sends a fourth-round pick to Los Angeles and receives Khalil Mack.
The proposal: Cleveland sends two fifths to Los Angeles and receives Khalil Mack plus a fourth.

The difference between those two is roughly a full round in Cleveland's favour, plus the Chargers volunteering a pick they have no reason to volunteer. In other words: the simulator produced a deal in which the selling team pays the buying team. That happens in real life when a club is dumping a contract it cannot carry, and Mack is on an eight-million-dollar base in the final year of a one-year deal, which is not a contract anybody needs rescuing from.

This is not a knock on the podcast that built it. A trade simulator is a calculator with a pleasant personality; it balances numbers, not incentives. The error is treating its output as a price. The seven comps above are prices. A simulator result is a suggestion.

And this is exactly the discipline we try to hold ourselves to at Ktl Report: when a real market exists, use the market.

The biggest comp of all, and what it did to every other price.

Now the part that should reframe the entire Browns deadline conversation, and the video touches it in one sentence before moving on.

Tennessee got two first-round picks and a fourth for Jeffery Simmons. Two firsts. For a twenty-nine-year-old defensive tackle under contract through twenty thirty on a hundred-million-guaranteed extension.

That single transaction tells you two things.

One: the premium in this market is for control, not reputation. Simmons brought two firsts because a buyer gets him for four more seasons. Buckner brought a fourth because a buyer gets a short window. Mack is the shortest window on the board, nine weeks and a playoff run, then free agency. Reputation does not change that.

Two: the sellers are now anchored high, and that is bad for buyers. After a two-first trade clears, every selling general manager's phone expectations go up for a week. If Cleveland is shopping, it is shopping in the most expensive room of the season.

And the sentence that should have ended the segment.

One more fact the video does not include, and it is the most important one: the Chargers have publicly said they are not shopping Khalil Mack.

Reporting also indicates the Chicago Bears have called about bringing him back, with New England named as another natural fit. Chicago spent four years of his prime with him and has just spent two first-round picks on a defensive tackle, a team in that posture calling about an edge rusher is a real market signal.

What is not a market signal: a simulator, a podcast, and a thumbnail. Cleveland is not named by anyone as having interest in Khalil Mack. That is not a secret being kept. There is simply no reporting.

The comp that applies to Cleveland, not to Mack.

Here is the appraisal nobody has run, and it is the one Cleveland will actually face a phone call about: Jerry Jeudy.

ESPN's Jeremy Fowler put Jeudy's chances of being traded at twenty percent, said the Browns are not sending out feelers but would consider a "substantial" offer, and gave the price in one sentence: "I'm not sure, say, a fourth-round pick would move anyone in Cleveland."

Now find the comp. It is number four on the list. Marvin Harrison Junior brought Arizona a twenty twenty-seven second and a twenty twenty-seven sixth, a receiver moved mid-season from a selling team to a buyer. That is the market rate for a wideout with name value, and it lands exactly where Fowler's language points: above a fourth, in the neighbourhood of a second.

Jeudy is twenty-seven, two years removed from a twelve-hundred-twenty-nine-yard Pro Bowl season, on a deal reported at fifty-two and a half million signed in twenty twenty-four. Against Tennessee he caught four of four targets for twenty-one yards while two rookies carried the receiving load.

So the sell-side appraisal reads: a second is the ask, a fourth is an insult, and Cleveland is five and two. Which is precisely why Fowler's number is twenty percent and not sixty. A first-place team does not sell a starting receiver, and a buyer does not pay a second for a player whose own team has stopped targeting him. That standoff is the whole Jeudy story, and it is worth more airtime than a simulator.

And the two firsts precedent cuts both ways.

One more appraisal, and this one is about Cleveland's own wallet.

The Browns hold two twenty twenty-seven first-round picks, the second from the Myles Garrett trade, plus the Rams' twenty twenty-eight second and twenty twenty-nine third from that same deal. "Protect the draft capital" has been the refrain around this deadline for three weeks, including from us.

But the Simmons sale just put a public price tag on that currency. Two first-round picks and a fourth now buys a twenty-nine-year-old, three-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle with four years of contract control and one hundred million guaranteed. That is the exchange rate, as of October twenty-ninth, set by the Chicago Bears.

So "protect the picks" stops being a slogan and becomes a question with an answer: is there anybody available right now who is worth what Chicago just paid? Not Khalil Mack at thirty-five on an expiring deal, nowhere close. Not a rotational edge rusher. Possibly not anybody on this market at all.

Which is the real conclusion of the appraisal, and it is more useful than a yes or a no: Cleveland should be shopping in the Buckner aisle, where a fourth-round pick buys a decorated veteran for nine weeks, and nowhere near the aisle where Chicago just spent two firsts. That is the memo at Ktl Report, and it is the same one we wrote before the simulator ever ran.

The sack numbers, resolved.

Here the video does something admirable and then leaves it unresolved. Its own words: "The original report listed the Browns with twenty sacks, tied for second in the NFL. A subsequent A to Z Sports report cited twenty-five sacks, leading the league at that point. Those figures refer to different reporting snapshots."

They are not different snapshots. They are different statistics, and one of them is not a compliment.

Through seven games, Cleveland's defense has recorded twenty sacks, top six in the league, with an earlier count of nineteen through six games sitting second at the time behind Detroit, and eleven different players registering at least one.

The twenty-five is sacks allowed. Deshaun Watson has been sacked twenty-five times, the most in the National Football League. That is not Cleveland leading the league in pressure; it is Cleveland leading the league in absorbing it.

If you read "twenty-five sacks, leading the league" on camera as a defensive achievement, you have told your audience the single worst number on this roster is its best one. Say twenty made, twenty-five allowed, and the whole picture snaps into focus: this defense does not need rescuing. The offensive line does.

The Jared Verse question, stated fairly.

The video's honest premise is that Verse has not produced finished plays: seventeen tackles and no sacks through seven games, by its count. We could not source the tackle figure independently, but the sack drought is real and documented, he has not recorded a sack as a Brown, the longest stretch of his young career.

The fair version has three more pieces.

Verse is not a one-for-one replacement for Myles Garrett and nobody inside the building expected fifteen sacks. Against Baltimore in Week six he had three run stops, holding Derrick Henry to fifty-eight yards on seventeen carries. He was a Wednesday non-participant with a knee this week and was upgraded to limited on Thursday. And he has spent this week telling anyone who will listen that he is not worried about anybody on Pittsburgh's roster, which, with zero sacks as a Brown, is a large cheque to write.

So: is "add a veteran rusher" a reasonable idea? Yes, and the video argues it well, a rotational veteran for third downs and four-minute situations is exactly the kind of cheap, sensible addition a five-and-two team makes. It is simply not the first thing on this roster's list, and the price is a fourth going out, not a fourth coming in.

The bottom line.

No trade happened. The script said so three times and the thumbnail said it anyway, which is now a pattern worth naming.

What is real is a market, and markets have receipts. In the last five weeks this league has priced a three-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle at one fourth-round pick, an eight-time Pro Bowl guard on an expiring deal at a third and a fifth, and a twenty-nine-year-old with four years of control at two first-round picks and change. Against those receipts, Khalil Mack at thirty-five on an expiring deal appraises at a Day Three pick going out of Cleveland, not a fourth-rounder coming in alongside him.

And the number the whole segment got backwards is the one that should decide Cleveland's deadline anyway. Twenty sacks made, top six in football. Twenty-five sacks allowed, worst in football. This defense is not the thing that needs a veteran. The man taking the hits is.
