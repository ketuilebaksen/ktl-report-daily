There is no decision in this press conference. None. What there is instead is the entire coaching curriculum Todd Monken runs the Cleveland Browns by, given away by accident in nineteen minutes, so today we are going to treat this video as what it really is. A syllabus.

First, the title. There is no decision in this video.

The title is "Todd Monken Bombshell Decision Shocks The NFL!"

We went through all nineteen minutes of this press conference looking for a decision. There is not one.

Not a quarterback decision. Not a roster decision. Not a lineup change, not a benching, not a promotion, not a trade, not a signing, not an injury ruling. Nobody in the room asked him to decide anything, and he did not volunteer a decision. There is no bombshell and nothing in it shocked the NFL.

What there is, instead, is better than a decision, and this is why we are writing twenty-five thousand characters about a press conference with no news in it.

Somewhere around the eleven-minute mark, a reporter asks Monken about a presentation he once gave at a coaching academy about the keys to an explosive offence. And Monken, off the cuff, recites his entire philosophy of football. Six elements of what wins. A doctrine on staff alignment. A theory about why young players make the same mistake twice. A rule about practice looks. He gave away the curriculum.

So here at Ktl Report, we are not going to pretend a decision happened. We are going to treat this video as what it actually is: a syllabus. And by the end of it you will understand how this football team is being run better than any "bombshell" video could ever tell you.

Module one. The six elements of what wins.

This is the centrepiece and it is a direct quote. Write it down.

"First off, there's the elements of what wins, and that's one part of it, which is turnovers, explosive plays, touchdowns in the red zone, conversion downs, eliminate lost yardage plays, and quarterback off schedule. That's what wins. That's the starting point of what wins and those categories. The rest are just fillers, how you fit into those categories."

Six items. Let us take them one at a time, because every single one of them explains something you have watched this season.

One. Turnovers. The single most predictive statistic in football and the one Monken has mentioned in nearly every press conference we have covered. After Houston he specifically noted Cleveland had not turned the ball over early.

Two. Explosive plays. Which the reporter asked about and which Monken immediately refused to romanticise, more on that below.

Three. Touchdowns in the red zone. Not points. Touchdowns. And remember what he said after the Saints loss: "we got to find a way to get it in the end zone" and "red zone, fourth down." This is the item Cleveland has failed at most visibly all year.

Four. Conversion downs. Third down and fourth down. Also on his postgame list of failures twice.

Five. Eliminate lost-yardage plays. Sacks, tackles for loss, negative runs. The quiet killer. A second-and-fifteen is a drive that has already half-ended.

Six. Quarterback off schedule. The most interesting one and the least understood. "Off schedule" means the quarterback has been forced off the play's intended timing, the first read is gone, the pocket has moved, the throw is now improvised. You want your own quarterback on schedule and theirs off it. This is one phrase that explains both why Cleveland has twenty-seven sacks and why their own offence stalls in first quarters.

And then the sentence that makes it a real philosophy rather than a list: "The rest are just fillers, how you fit into those categories."

Translation: every other statistic in football is only interesting insofar as it feeds one of those six. Total yards do not matter. Time of possession does not matter. Completion percentage does not matter. They matter only if they produce turnovers, explosives, red-zone touchdowns, conversions, fewer negative plays, or a quarterback off schedule.

That is a coherent, teachable, falsifiable theory of football, delivered without notes, in a bye-week press conference, in a video titled "Bombshell Decision."

Module two. Why he hates the word "explosive."

Immediately after, the best thirty seconds of the whole press conference. He is asked about chasing explosive plays and he does something coaches almost never do on camera: he mocks his own profession.

"I get a big kick out of when someone gets hired and they're like, 'Oh, we're going to chase explosives.' And everybody runs, 'Oh man, he's a guy that chased explosives.' Well, no. I mean, it's one of the ways you win. Oh, turnovers are important. I mean, come on, man. Now, it's how you go about it, right?"

"Oh, turnovers are important. I mean, come on, man."

He is saying: naming a goal is not a plan. Every coach who has ever been hired wants explosive plays and fewer turnovers. Announcing the objective is the easiest part of the job and it is the part that gets written up as a philosophy.

And then he says what the actual work is:

"First of all, it helps if you have explosive players, helps if you have diligent players. And then how you come to work every day, how you drill it, how you teach it, how you drill it, how you get on the same page, how you make sure the looks in practice are the way you want it so it shows up on Sunday."

He says "how you drill it" twice in one sentence. That is not a transcript error, that is a man whose brain genuinely runs on repetition.

Module three. "You try to eliminate as many almosts as you can."

This is the single best line in the video and it belongs on a wall.

"Nothing's worse than getting a bad look during the week and you're just you have a lot of almost. You try to eliminate as many almosts as you can. Man, we almost, if we'd have just gotten this block, if we'd have just gotten this protection, if we'd have just gotten this route right."

An "almost" is a play that was designed correctly, called correctly, and failed anyway because one of eleven men was slightly wrong.

And the cause he identifies is not the players. It is the practice look. If the scout team gives you a coverage the opponent does not actually run, then your receivers learn the wrong landmarks, your quarterback learns the wrong timing, and on Sunday every one of those reps is an almost.

So the chain of blame runs backwards, from Sunday to Wednesday, and lands on the coaching staff. A bad practice look is a coaching failure that shows up as a player's mistake three days later. That is an unusually honest thing for a head coach to say in public.

And it connects directly to the diagnosis he gave the same day about his own offence:

"early in the game the other day didn't do well the first drive, but then the second drive we had a drop and then we have an open receiver that we throw behind. And those are things that kind of stop it, and run the headset going, we're not that far away. We just got to do it better."

A drop. A throw behind an open receiver. Two almosts, back to back, in the first ten minutes of a game Cleveland won by three.

Module four. The alignment doctrine, and the most revealing thing he has ever said.

Asked whether assembling a staff "wired the right way" is the most important thing about the season, he gives a long answer, and inside it is a passage that tells you more about Todd Monken than a year of press conferences.

First, the correction, which is important and which most coaches skip:

"It's important. Talent's important. I'll start with that. And we have talented guys. They may be young, but you still have to have talent."

He will not let the romantic answer stand. Culture without talent loses. Then the word he actually wants:

"But I think the word alignment is the word I like to use. You're aligned philosophically the same way. And you have to have your staff aligned in your beliefs and how you see the everyday approach to improvement."

And then, the part that is genuinely remarkable, because he interrupts himself to say it:

"I'm sorry, but it just those things of saying, you know, not everything is optional, that's, I'm still rolling here, crazy enough, it's not. A lot of things, not everything's up for a vote. That's not the way I work. This is the way we do it. This is the way we carry the message. This is what I believe in. I waited my whole life to get here. This is the way we're going to do it. And get on board, or we got to change."

"I waited my whole life to get here."

Todd Monken is in his first season as an NFL head coach after decades as an assistant and a coordinator. That sentence is the sound of a man who spent thirty years being told how somebody else wanted it done, and has decided that this time it gets done his way.

And then, immediately, he takes the edge off it himself:

"And those aren't threats. I don't mean it that way. I just mean like I have a belief in certain ways of going about it, and the guys, it's important for the coaching staff to relay that to the players."

He heard how it sounded and softened it in real time. That is not weakness, it is the same self-awareness he says he hires for.

Module five. How he built the staff, and who told him to.

Cleveland has three first-time coordinators. Asked about the learning curve, Monken credits the hiring strategy to a conversation with another head coach, and this is a real, checkable piece of coaching-industry mechanics.

"A lot of that came from early on from a visit with Mike MacDonald about it, and him choosing his staff, and he talked about, you know, you've got to fight to get the coaches you want and you might have to get them a year early. You might have to, for rising superstars, because if you wait, you won't get them."

"You might have to get them a year early."

That is the whole thing. A coordinator who is obviously ready is a coordinator five teams are calling. The only way to hire a future star is to hire him before everybody agrees he is one, which means hiring somebody with no track record in the chair and accepting the first-year mistakes that come with it.

Which is exactly what Cleveland is living through right now. Three coordinators learning on the job, on a six-and-four team, with a roster full of rookies.

And his version of the deal he made with them:

"They're wired the right way and how I see things. And that's the most important thing is self-awareness, a diligence, a work ethic, a loyalty, that they understand that we're human and we're going to fail, but we're gonna fight like hell to fix it. We're always under construction. You guys will keep hearing me say that, but that is true."

"We're always under construction" is now the fourth or fifth time we have logged that phrase. It is his thesis statement.

Module six. Sunday night football, and a line about one o'clock games.

The press conference opens with the flex to Sunday Night Football, and Monken gives the most unguarded answer of the day.

"I said it at the beginning of the year. I mean, you want to be on Thursday night, Sunday night, Monday night, you want to be flexed. Means you're having a good year. As nice as it is to have a one o'clock schedule every week, I mean, you're on a trajectory to be fired at some point because you're at one o'clock every week. So you're fighting like hell to get to where you're on national TV."

"You're on a trajectory to be fired at some point because you're at one o'clock every week."

That is the most quotable sentence Todd Monken has produced all season and nobody has run it. The networks pick the games. The networks pick the good teams. A schedule full of one o'clock kick-offs is a public statement that nobody expects you to be worth watching, and he knows exactly what that means for a head coach's employment.

And then the honest framing of the stakes: "an opportunity to get to seven."

Not a playoff speech. Not a statement game. One win. Six and four becomes seven and four.

And on the home-field advantage, which is a tactical point and not a cheerleading one:

"Oh, it was a huge advantage. Huge advantage. Making hard for the opposing offence cadence-wise, being able to function, and making an advantage for us when we're on offence."

Crowd noise is not atmosphere. It is a cadence problem for the visiting offence, the silent count, the hand signals, the false starts, and an advantage for the home offence, which can use its own cadence freely. He also adds "grass helps" and "colder helps," then immediately caveats it: "That doesn't eliminate players, but it helps."

Module seven. The Tomlin answer, which is a retraction in disguise.

Asked about facing Mike Tomlin, Monken does a careful piece of de-escalation.

"I am. I'm a big Mike Tomlin fan. I mean, people have made more out of this than it really is. Because I am a believer in you are how you play, as a player, as an organisation, as a coach. That's you on the field. That's your team. That's your name. That's what started all that at Tampa. It all started, I was talking about the name on your jersey, be it the Browns or your individual name, you want to change a narrative, play better individually and collectively, and the only way you can do that is through hard work and a day-to-day approach. That's really all it is that I was getting at."

Something he said earlier, apparently dating back to his time at Tampa, was read as a shot at Pittsburgh, and this is him putting it away. "People have made more out of this than it really is" and "that's really all it is that I was getting at" are a coach declining to continue a feud he did not intend to start.

We have no record of the original remark and we are not going to reconstruct it from a transcript. If you cover this, cover the retraction, not the rumour.

Module eight. What a bye week actually is.

Here is the most useful practical answer in the video, and it is a straightforward description of a process most fans have never had explained.

"Both. I think you do internal scouting. You look at other situational football from around the league. What are we doing? What aren't we doing? What are other people doing? And then you're taking a look at the Raiders. All the above."

Three separate jobs in one week, and at Ktl Report we think it is the clearest thing he said all day: audit yourself, study what the rest of the league is doing situationally, and get ahead on the next opponent.

And then the reason, which is Thanksgiving:

"Like I told the staff, it'd be a damn shame if we don't take at least some amount of time and get ourselves ahead on the Raiders so we're not scrambling. There's no reason next week with Thanksgiving to be scrambling."

The bye is not two weeks of rest. It is a week of banking work so that a short holiday week does not become a disaster. Every coaching staff in the league is doing this right now and almost nobody explains it on camera.

Module nine. The surprise question, and the answer nobody expected.

A reporter offers him a gift: six and four is a pleasant surprise, nationally you are a positive story, what has surprised you?

And Monken declines the gift. "I wouldn't say that."

"Like I've said plenty of times, the biggest surprise was how we played at Jacksonville. That was the biggest surprise. With our off-season and training camp, I did not anticipate that. I did anticipate the way we've played since then."

Read that carefully, because it is the opposite of what the question invited. The only surprise of his season was a bad performance. Everything good that has happened since, he says he expected.

And then the Pittsburgh loss, handled with a phrase we have not heard from any other coach:

"even though we weren't clean, we earned that lopsided loss at Pittsburgh. Our guys fought and they battled. We just didn't coach well enough. We didn't play well enough."

"We earned that lopsided loss." Not "we ran into a good team." Not "the ball did not bounce our way." We earned it. And the coaching failure is listed first.

Module ten. The players he actually talked about.

Three, and all three are instructive.

Dylan Sampson. The answer is about versatility, not highlights: "not only his ability to run the football, but in the pass game. I think that's where it shows up, and the protections. He's our third-down back." And the context, which matters: Cleveland lost its previous third-down back, and the man Monken calls "Rocket" is out of the picture. "It's been nice to have him healthy because he's wired the right way. Comes to practice every day. He's diligent."

A third-down back is a protection job before it is a receiving job. You are the last line between a blitzing linebacker and your quarterback. That is why Monken lists protections before routes.

The rookie receivers, whom he credits to the front office rather than to coaching: "it's Andrew and his staff doing a great job behind the scenes to drill down what makes individual players tick." And then the scouting distinction, which is excellent:

"it's easier to see a player's skill set. You can turn on the tape and say balance, body control, quickness, acceleration, catch radius, but drilling down to what's going to separate them from the pack of others is a real credit to Andrew and his staff."

Everybody can see the athleticism on tape. Nobody can see the work ethic on tape. The second one is the draft.

Denzel Ward, and a detail worth noting: "a guy that really tore his finger in practice and still played." Monken's framing of what the cornerback does well is almost entirely mental: "the anticipation of what they're going to see, right? And then if they don't, how do they apply their rules when it isn't exactly right? Because we don't know for sure what every team's going to run. We have an idea."

That is the honest version of defensive game-planning. You do not know what is coming. You teach rules so that a player can be wrong about the formation and still right about his job.

Module eleven. The recall gap. The best explanation of a young team we have heard.

Asked what separates a good young player from a good veteran, Monken gives the answer of the day.

"I think they have a little bit more recall. Teams are running duo and they're running inside zone or wide zone or they're running these pass concepts, and a lot of our guys don't have any recall of anywhere else they've been, let alone the system. So there's things that come up all the time, like, oh, maybe we didn't hit on this enough coaching-wise, and they don't have enough recall to get through that. Some of that's an excuse and some of it's true, and we certainly have to be better at it."

"Recall" is the word, and it is not the same as experience.

A veteran has seen duo, inside zone and wide zone run against him by fifteen different teams in four different schemes. When something unfamiliar appears, he has a library to search. A rookie has one season in one system and nothing to compare it to, so when the look is new, there is nothing to recall and the play becomes an almost.

And then he refuses to hide behind it: "Some of that's an excuse and some of it's true."

And the closing definition of a roster, which is as clear as anything you will read in a textbook:

"your starters not only have talent, but they play a consistently high level. And the guys that are next in line are your guys that either have real talent, but they're inconsistent, or they are a little bit lower talent-wise, but they give you everything they got. And that's kind of the core of your team."

Three tiers: consistent talent, inconsistent talent, and limited talent with maximum effort. That is the depth chart of every team in the NFL, described in one breath.

Module twelve. The offensive line, and a coach changing his mind on camera.

A reporter needles him: the line played well against Houston, you were reserved afterwards, you did not give them a game ball.

And watch what happens.

"I think I gave one at the Titans, didn't I, in Nashville?... I swear I gave them one after the Titans game, the hundred yards, didn't we?... I felt like they played well. There was a couple times inside we gave up a little bit of interior pressure right in the quarterback's face, but I do think they played well. I agree with you.... First of all, we didn't have any false starts. Okay, let's start there. Did we have a holding call? No, we did not. I mean, so those are the things that we are trying to clean up. So maybe I should, Tony, you might be right. Maybe I need to go back and give them one."

A head coach being told in public that he under-praised his offensive line, checking the record, agreeing, and saying he will fix it.

And then, when the reporter tries to push it into a narrative, this is the group turning a corner, now you expect more, Monken shuts the door twice: "I don't know that. I don't know that."

He will not take one clean game and turn it into a trend. He lists the reasons it happened instead: home, grass, crowd noise, cold, and not playing on turf on the road. He even reaches back twenty years to Jacksonville and playing the Colts indoors as the example of the opposite.

Zero false starts and zero holding calls is a real, specific, verifiable improvement, and remember what he said after the Houston win: Cleveland had "solved a little bit of our penalty issue, for one week." He is still saying "for one week."

Your turn.

Here is the question this video should have asked.

Monken said the only surprise of his season was a bad game in Jacksonville, and that he anticipated everything good that has happened since. He also said Cleveland earned a lopsided loss at Pittsburgh and that the coaching, listed first, was not good enough. And he said that his team is "always under construction" and that he is "fighting for consistency."

The fan base says ahead. The coach says he expected it. Those are not the same answer, and only one of them comes from the man who sees the practice film.

And the second question, which is the one we actually care about. Of his six elements of what wins, turnovers, explosive plays, red-zone touchdowns, conversion downs, lost-yardage plays and quarterback off schedule, which one is costing Cleveland the most right now?

Our answer is red-zone touchdowns, because he has named it himself after two different games. Tell us yours in the comments, and tell us whether you had ever heard a head coach say out loud that a bad practice look on Wednesday is what produces an "almost" on Sunday.

The bottom line.

There is no decision in this video and nothing shocked the NFL. It is a bye-week availability in which Todd Monken was asked about a flex to Sunday Night Football, Mike Tomlin, internal scouting, his coordinators, a running back, two rookies, Denzel Ward and his offensive line, and announced absolutely nothing.

What he did instead was hand over the curriculum. Six elements of what wins: turnovers, explosive plays, touchdowns in the red zone, conversion downs, eliminating lost-yardage plays, and the quarterback off schedule, and the ruthless addendum that "the rest are just fillers." Then he laughed at his own industry for announcing goals as if they were plans: "Oh, turnovers are important. I mean, come on, man." Then he gave the best sentence of his season, "you try to eliminate as many almosts as you can", and traced the almost all the way back to a bad practice look on a Wednesday, which makes it a coaching failure and not a player's mistake.

He corrected the romantic answer about culture by insisting on talent first. He named his actual priority, alignment, and then said the quiet part out loud: "not everything's up for a vote... I waited my whole life to get here. This is the way we're going to do it", before immediately clarifying, unprompted, "those aren't threats." He explained how he hired three first-time coordinators, with the industry truth he learned from Mike MacDonald: "you might have to get them a year early... because if you wait, you won't get them."

He refused the compliment he was offered. Six and four is not a pleasant surprise to him. The only surprise was Jacksonville, and it was bad. Cleveland "earned that lopsided loss at Pittsburgh," and "we didn't coach well enough" came before "we didn't play well enough." He was told his offensive line had turned a corner and said "I don't know that" twice, then listed home, grass, crowd and cold as the reasons it looked clean, while conceding, graciously, that he probably owed them a game ball. Zero false starts, zero holding calls, "for one week."

And he explained a young team better than anyone has all season with one word: recall. A veteran has a library to search when the look is unfamiliar. A rookie has nothing to compare it to, and that gap, Monken says, is "some of it an excuse and some of it true."

Here at Ktl Report we will take that over a fake bombshell every single week. Six and four. The flex to Sunday night. An opportunity to get to seven. And one line about the one o'clock schedule that tells you exactly what this job is: "you're on a trajectory to be fired at some point because you're at one o'clock every week."

He is not at one o'clock any more.
