Five Tryouts, Two Positions, One Message: The Browns Just Told You Exactly Where They're Nervous

Nobody makes a highlight reel out of a Thursday tryout list.

There's no press conference. No jersey reveal. No breaking-news graphic. Five names walk into Berea, get weighed, get timed, run through a workout in front of a position coach, and most of them go home the same afternoon and never wear brown and orange.

And yet — if you actually want to know what a front office is worried about eleven days before kickoff, the tryout list is the most honest document they produce all week. Press conferences are managed. Depth charts are strategic. Podium answers are lawyered. But nobody brings in five players for a look because everything's fine.

Cleveland worked out five players on Thursday. All five came from exactly two position groups. That's not a coincidence, and it isn't random scouting. That's a franchise pointing at its own soft spots and saying, out loud, we're not comfortable here yet.

Ktl Report breaks down every name, the injury that started it, the offensive line subplot underneath it, and why the boring transactions are the ones that actually decide seasons.

THE INJURY THAT STARTED ALL OF THIS 🩹

Trace the whole thing back and it begins in one place: Kalia Davis to injured reserve with a quad injury.

Under the current rules, that designation means Davis misses at least the first four games of the season. Not a week. Not a game-time decision. A full month of football, minimum, gone before Cleveland has played a single snap that counts.

Now, if you're being honest, Davis was never the headline of this defensive line. He was a rotational interior body. But that's precisely the point people miss about NFL rosters: the interior defensive line is the position where depth isn't a luxury item, it's a functional requirement.

Think about what these guys actually do for a living. They play the most physically brutal snaps on the field, in the tightest space, absorbing double teams on nearly every run down. No coaching staff on earth asks an interior defender to play 65 snaps a game. You rotate them or you watch them disappear in the fourth quarter. Which means a defensive tackle room isn't three starters and some spare parts — it's a rotation of five or six players who all have to be capable of real snaps.

Take one out and you're not down a backup. You're down a shift.

Cleveland still has real names in there. Mason Graham — the young interior disruptor we've been telling you to watch all summer, and the single most important player on this defensive front in a post-Myles Garrett world. Maliek Collins, the veteran. Mike Hall Jr., who appeared in nine games last season with a sack and a half and now has a genuine opportunity in front of him. Adin Huntington rounding out the group.

That's a functional four. It is absolutely not a comfortable four. And "functional" evaporates the second one more person gets hurt — which is exactly what happens in the NFL, every single year, usually in October.

So the Browns went shopping.

THE HEADLINE NAME: ZION LOGUE 🐺

The name that jumps off the list is Zion Logue, and he's not a random body brought in to fill out a workout group.

Logue is a defensive tackle out of Georgia — and not just Georgia, but those Georgia teams. He was part of the back-to-back national championship rosters, which means he spent his college career in the most talent-saturated defensive line room in America, going against future NFL starters in practice every single day. That environment matters. Players who survive it arrive in the league already familiar with a professional standard.

The Atlanta Falcons took him in the sixth round of the 2024 draft. He's a genuinely massive human being: 6-foot-6, 325 pounds. Across five seasons with the Bulldogs he played 50 games with 16 starts, finishing with 52 tackles, four and a half tackles for loss, and a sack and a half.

Now let's be straight about that stat line, because Ktl Report isn't going to oversell a workout. Those aren't dominant college numbers. But they're also not what he was asked to do. In that Georgia system, the interior guys eat blocks so linebackers can run free. Their production shows up in other people's box scores. Evaluating a Georgia nose tackle by his sack total is like evaluating an offensive lineman by his receptions.

His NFL path has been rough. Rookie year on Atlanta's practice squad. Then Buffalo, where he appeared in three regular season games and recorded four tackles and half a sack. Then released in the Bills' final cuts this summer.

So what's the actual pitch? A 6-foot-6, 325-pound former draft pick, from the best defensive line program in college football, who's only two years into his career, available for nothing. That's not a savior. That's a lottery ticket with a real floor — and at the moment, Cleveland needs a stack of exactly those.

THE OTHER TWO INTERIOR LOOKS 💪

The Browns didn't stop at one.

Aaron Graves came in for a workout, and his college résumé is the most statistically impressive of the group. An Iowa product who signed with Baltimore as an undrafted free agent, Graves played 52 games for the Hawkeyes and finished with 122 tackles, 35 tackles for loss, 16 and a half sacks, and four forced fumbles.

Read those numbers again, because they're unusual. Thirty-five tackles for loss from an interior player is genuine, sustained penetration — that's a guy who consistently won his gap rather than just occupying it. And four forced fumbles from a defensive tackle is a real indicator, because interior forced fumbles almost always come from beating a block and arriving at the quarterback's arm before he expects it. Iowa also happens to be one of the most technically demanding defensive line development programs in the country.

Undrafted doesn't mean unqualified. It usually means something specific — a testing number, an injury, or simply the brutal math of 32 teams and seven rounds. Cleveland is checking whether the board got this one wrong.

Then there's Kristian Williams, another interior defender who entered the league undrafted with Denver in 2025. He played at Oregon before transferring to Missouri, and across 63 college games he posted 107 tackles, 13 tackles for loss, and three sacks. Sixty-three games is a lot of college football — durability and accumulated experience, exactly what you want from a depth signing who might have to learn a defense in ten days.

Three interior defenders in one afternoon. Nobody does that unless they're genuinely uneasy about the position.

THE OFFENSIVE TACKLE SUBPLOT 🧱

Here's the part of the story that got buried, and it might be more interesting than the defensive line.

Cleveland kept ten offensive linemen on the initial 53-man roster. Ten. That's a healthy, even generous allocation. And they're still working out tackles.

Two of them came through on Thursday, and both are enormous.

Luke Felix-Fualalo stands 6-foot-7. He played college football at Utah and Hawaii, starting 15 games across his final two seasons with the Rainbow Warriors, then signed with Seattle as an undrafted free agent in 2025 before landing on Philadelphia's practice squad through the NFL's International Player Pathway Program.

Baron Moss is also listed at 6-foot-7 and 334 pounds, with one of the more unusual backgrounds you'll find on an NFL workout sheet. Born in the Dominican Republic, Moss originally played college basketball at New Mexico and South Florida before converting to football. He spent 2024 on Miami's practice squad through the International Player Pathway Program and later joined Indianapolis.

Basketball-to-football conversions at offensive tackle are one of the league's favorite bets, and the logic is sound: elite footwork in space, natural bend, hand quickness, and the body control to mirror a moving target. The gap is everything that happens in a phone booth — pad level, anchoring against a bull rush, and reading a stunt before it develops. Those are teachable. Length and feet are not.

But here's why this matters beyond two individual names. Cleveland rebuilt this entire offensive line group this offseason. We covered that structural story at Ktl Report after cutdowns — Zion Johnson and Titus Howard projecting into the starting mix, Austin Barber pushing Teven Jenkins for a guard spot, and George Warhop's coaching quietly being one of the most consequential hires on the staff.

When a line is that new, the starting five is only half the question. The other half is: what happens on the first Sunday somebody goes down? A backup becomes a starter, another lineman slides to a position he's practiced twice, and suddenly your protection calls are being executed by a group that's never played a snap together.

That's the nightmare scenario for a team already ranked 31st in explosive plays with a quarterback situation this fragile. Cleveland keeping a live list of 6-foot-7 tackles isn't paranoia. It's insurance.

HOW IT ALL FITS WITH THIS WEEK'S MOVES 📋

Stack the tryouts on top of everything else Cleveland has done in the last several days and a clear pattern emerges.

Cornerback Noah Igbinoghene and edge defender Sam Williams both went straight onto the active roster — we broke both of those signings down in detail earlier this week. Williams brings 49 career games in Dallas with nine and a half sacks, four fumble recoveries and three forced fumbles, and he'll wear number 54. Igbinoghene, a seventh-year corner and former first-round pick out of Auburn with 69 career games across Miami, Dallas and Washington, takes number 20.

Kalia Davis went to IR. Cornerback Michael Coats Jr. — an undrafted defender we tracked all camp — was waived. And four players joined the practice squad: defensive tackle Sam Kamara, who has already appeared in 18 games with Cleveland since 2022; running back Jaylen McLaughlin, a fourth-year back out of Youngstown State with 1,093 career rushing yards and 59 receptions in 41 games with Denver; tight end Messiah Swinson, a 6-foot-7 developmental piece who's cycled through Green Bay, San Francisco and Arizona; and defensive tackle Chris Williams, a fifth-year player out of Wagner who spent time on Cleveland's practice squad back in 2023.

Now count the positions being touched: interior defensive line, edge, cornerback, offensive tackle, running back depth, tight end depth.

That's not a team polishing a finished product. That's a team still building.

WHY THE BORING MOVES ARE THE ONES THAT DECIDE SEASONS 🏈

We all want the headlines. We want to argue about the quarterback, chase the receiver rumor, debate the trade calls. That stuff is fun and it drives every conversation in this fan base.

But NFL seasons are quietly decided somewhere else entirely.

They're decided by the backup defensive tackle who suddenly has to play 30 snaps in a December road game. By the third tackle thrown into a Sunday because a starter's ankle rolled on the second series. By the practice squad player who gets elevated in Week 11 and makes the play that flips a game.

Every contender you've ever watched had four or five of those players. Every collapse you've suffered through had a roster that ran out of them by Halloween.

That's the entire logic behind Thursday. None of these five is guaranteed anything — Cleveland is evaluating size, athleticism, versatility, conditioning, football intelligence and scheme fit, and most tryouts end with a handshake and a flight home. But the front office is keeping a live, current board of who's available, so that when the phone call comes at 6 a.m. on a Monday in October, they're not starting from zero.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Here's the honest read from Ktl Report.

Losing Kalia Davis for a minimum of four games is a real problem — not a catastrophic one, but real enough that a front office responded within days by bringing in three interior defenders. Mike Hall Jr. now has a genuine opportunity in front of him, and Mason Graham's importance to this defense just went up another notch.

The offensive tackle workouts are the tell nobody's discussing. Ten linemen on the roster and Cleveland is still looking. That's a staff that knows a rebuilt line has zero margin for a lost starter.

And the broader message is one Browns fans should actually find encouraging: this front office is not pretending. They're not standing on a finished 53 and calling it good. They're scanning, evaluating, and reacting to reality — which, after the summer this franchise has had, beats the alternative.

The regular season opens on the road in Jacksonville. The coaching staff has to sort out who helps immediately, who needs development, and who stays on the radar for the phone call nobody wants to make. That work is happening right now, in a building most fans never see, on a Thursday nobody's going to remember.

Until October, when one of these names is playing 30 snaps and everybody's asking where he came from.
